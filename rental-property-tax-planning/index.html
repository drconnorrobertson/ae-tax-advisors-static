1. Depreciation for Rental Properties

Under the general depreciation system (GDS), residential rental buildings generally use 27.5-year straight-line depreciation and nonresidential buildings generally use 39 years. The alternative depreciation system (ADS) can apply to particular property or elections and uses different recovery periods. Land is not depreciable, and separately classified assets may have shorter lives. Depreciation reduces adjusted basis and can affect gain and recapture when you sell.

Placed-in-Service Rules

Depreciation begins when the property is ready and available for its rental use, not automatically on the purchase date. For example, a property bought March 15 but first ready and available for rent April 1 is placed in service April 1. Under GDS, the mid-month convention generally applies to the building. Keep listing, inspection, renovation-completion and availability records.

Allocating Between Land and Building

Allocate acquisition cost and applicable capitalized closing costs among land, the building and other assets using supportable relative fair market values. An appraisal or an appropriate assessment ratio can support the allocation; the objective is accurate basis, not the smallest possible land value. A home converted to rental use generally uses the lower of adjusted basis or fair market value at conversion for depreciation. Our team reviews every acquisition to ensure the allocation is documented.

Partial Dispositions Under Treas. Reg. 1.168(i)-8

When you replace a building component, a valid partial disposition election may let you deduct the old component's remaining adjusted basis and stop depreciating it. Document the original allocated cost, depreciation already allowed or allowable and disposition date. Election timing and a reasonable basis-allocation method matter; the replacement's cost is not the old component's remaining basis.

Hypothetical example, not a client result: If an old roof has $30,000 of supported allocated basis and $12,000 of accumulated depreciation, its remaining adjusted basis is $18,000. A qualifying timely election may permit that loss, subject to applicable limits. The new roof is evaluated separately for capitalization and depreciation. See the IRS tangible property guidance.

2. Repairs vs. Capital Improvements

The distinction between a deductible repair and a capitalizable improvement is one of the most contested areas in rental property taxation. Under IRC Section 263(a), amounts paid for betterments, restorations, or adaptations to a new or different use must be capitalized and depreciated. Amounts that merely maintain the property in its ordinary efficient operating condition are deductible as repairs in the current year.

The Three Capitalization Tests

The Treasury Regulations under Treas. Reg. 1.263(a)-3 establish three tests. If an expenditure meets any one of these, it must be capitalized:

  • Betterment: The expenditure corrects a material condition or defect that existed before acquisition, results in a material addition, or results in a material increase in capacity, productivity, efficiency, strength, or quality.
  • Restoration: The expenditure returns the property to its ordinarily efficient operating condition after it has deteriorated to a state of disrepair, rebuilds the property after the end of its economic useful life, or replaces a major component or substantial structural part.
  • Adaptation: The expenditure adapts the property to a new or different use from the use for which it was placed in service.

Safe Harbors That Benefit Landlords

De Minimis Safe Harbor (Treas. Reg. 1.263(a)-1(f)): Eligible taxpayers without an applicable financial statement may elect a $2,500-or-less threshold per invoice or substantiated item; the threshold is $5,000 with an applicable financial statement. The accounting policy, book-expensing and other requirements must be met. Make the election on a timely filed original return, including extensions. Eligibility should be reviewed for each client.

Routine Maintenance Safe Harbor (Treas. Reg. 1.263(a)-3(i)): Qualifying recurring work may be deductible when, at the time the building or system is placed in service, you reasonably expect to perform it more than once during the following 10 years. Betterments, adaptations and certain restorations are excluded; a label such as maintenance does not by itself establish eligibility.

Small Taxpayer Safe Harbor (Treas. Reg. 1.263(a)-3(h)): Eligible taxpayers generally need average annual gross receipts of $10 million or less for the preceding three tax years and an eligible building with unadjusted basis of $1 million or less. Total annual repairs, maintenance, improvements and similar costs must not exceed the lesser of $10,000 or 2% of building basis. Review the cost-counting rules and make the annual election for each eligible building; exceeding the limit does not permit a partial safe-harbor deduction.

3. Passive Activity Loss Rules for Landlords

Long-term rental activities are generally passive under Section 469 even when an owner is involved. Passive losses generally offset passive income, not wages, operating-business profits or portfolio income. Some short-stay arrangements fall outside the rental-activity definition and require a separate participation analysis; see short-term versus long-term rental treatment.

The $25,000 Special Allowance (IRC 469(i))

An eligible individual who actively participates and meets the ownership requirement may deduct up to $25,000 of rental real estate losses against nonpassive income. Active participation includes management decisions and is less demanding than material participation. Generally you and your spouse must own at least 10% by value throughout the year.

For single and married-filing-jointly taxpayers, the allowance generally decreases by $1 for every $2 of modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) over $100,000 and reaches zero at $150,000. At $130,000 MAGI, the maximum allowance is $10,000: $25,000 minus half of the $30,000 excess. This is a deduction limit, not a refund. Married-filing-separately rules differ, including whether spouses lived apart all year. Other loss limits still apply.

Check the Other Loss Limits Before Forecasting Savings

  • Basis: Establish property basis and any applicable partnership or S-corporation owner-level basis limitation.
  • At risk: Determine the amount economically at risk; a mortgage balance does not automatically equal an at-risk amount. Qualified nonrecourse real estate financing has specific rules.
  • Passive activity: Apply Section 469 after basis and at-risk limits. REPS requires a separate material-participation test.
  • Excess business loss: Section 461(l) can limit an individual's otherwise allowable business losses after those tests. A nonpassive label does not make deductions unlimited. Review the 2026 excess business loss rules.
  • Personal use and other restrictions: Allocate mixed rental/personal expenses. If personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days, Section 280A may limit deductions. Interest limitations, elections and state rules can also change the result.

Suspended Passive Losses

Track carryforwards by activity and by limitation. Passive losses may be used against passive income or generally released when you dispose of your entire interest in the activity in a fully taxable transaction to an unrelated person. A sale of one property in a grouped activity, a gift or a tax-deferred exchange may not release all losses. Becoming a real estate professional does not automatically free prior-year suspended losses. See IRS Publication 925.

4. Real Estate Professional Status (REPS)

Real Estate Professional Status removes the automatic passive classification of rental real estate under Section 469(c)(7); it does not by itself make every rental nonpassive. You must also materially participate in the rental activity. Otherwise allowable nonpassive losses may offset other income, but basis, at-risk, personal-use, Section 461(l) and other applicable limits remain.

The Two-Part Qualification Test

  1. More than 750 hours: Perform more than 750 hours of services during the tax year in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate.
  2. More than half: Those qualifying services must exceed half of all personal services you perform in trades or businesses during the year. Operating a separate full-time business can make this test difficult.

On a joint return, one spouse must independently meet both REPS tests; spouses cannot combine hours to reach them. A spouse's participation can count for the separate material-participation test. Real estate employee services generally do not count toward REPS unless the employee owns more than 5% of the employer. Investor research and monitoring are not automatically qualifying participation. See IRS Publication 925.

Material Participation in Each Rental Activity

Test each rental separately unless a valid election treats the rental real estate interests as one activity. Common tests include more than 500 hours, substantially all participation, or more than 100 hours with no other individual participating more. Document property-manager and contractor involvement when relevant; exceeding 100 hours alone is not enough.

The Election Under Treas. Reg. 1.469-9(g)

A qualifying taxpayer can elect to treat all rental real estate interests as a single activity. A statement normally accompanies a timely filed original return, including extensions; limited late-election relief may apply. The election generally binds later qualifying years and can delay suspended-loss release on the sale of a single property. Review prior returns before making or changing it.

Documentation Is Everything

Maintain dated, specific records of the work, hours and property involved, plus calendars and supporting communications. The IRS permits reasonable substantiation methods, not only daily logs, but unsupported estimates create risk. We build a time-tracking system for REPS clients with categories tied to qualifying services. Our tax attorneys, including Jacob Simany, review each REPS filing for audit defensibility before submission.

5. Cost Segregation for Rental Properties

A cost segregation study identifies and documents assets that qualify for shorter recovery periods instead of the building's 27.5-year or 39-year life. Examples may include 5-year appliances or carpeting, 7-year furniture where the applicable asset class requires it, and 15-year qualifying land improvements. Classification depends on the asset's function and facts; land itself is not depreciable.

Bonus Depreciation Under IRC 168(k)

Current law generally permits 100% bonus depreciation for eligible property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. Qualifying used-property, acquisition, binding-contract and placed-in-service rules matter, and a taxpayer may elect out. The building itself and land do not qualify merely because a study is completed. ADS property is generally ineligible. Older acquisitions may follow different bonus rates. See IRS Notice 2026-11.

No fixed percentage of a purchase price or building basis is guaranteed to qualify. Compare the study fee and implementation costs with deductions you can actually use, future depreciation, potential recapture and state treatment.

A Basis-First Illustration

Hypothetical example, not a forecast or client result: Assume a $500,000 acquisition cost, with $100,000 allocated to land and $400,000 to depreciable assets, ignoring additional capitalized costs. If a supported study identifies $80,000 of eligible short-lived assets, that is 20% of the $400,000 depreciable basis and 16% of the total purchase price. Those percentages are assumed inputs, not typical results.

If all $80,000 qualifies for 100% bonus depreciation, the bonus deduction is $80,000. It is not an $80,000 tax saving. At an assumed 32% federal marginal rate, $80,000 multiplied by 32% is $25,600 of illustrative tax reduction only if the full deduction is currently usable at that rate, before interactions with other taxes, deductions, fees and state rules. This calculation is not the incremental benefit over regular depreciation. Suspended losses may produce no current-year reduction.

Catch-Up Depreciation (IRC 481(a) Adjustment)

A study on an older property may support a Form 3115 accounting-method change and a Section 481(a) catch-up adjustment, subject to eligibility and procedural requirements. It is not automatically an amended-return claim or a new 100% bonus deduction. Use the acquisition and placed-in-service rules applicable to the assets, account for depreciation already taken and review prior elections. See our Form 3115 guide.

6. 1031 Like-Kind Exchanges

Section 1031 can defer gain on an exchange of qualifying real property held for investment or productive use in a trade or business. A sale followed by a purchase is not automatically an exchange. Property held primarily for sale, personal-use property and non-real-property assets generally do not qualify; U.S. real estate is not like-kind to foreign real estate. Deferred gain is generally carried into the replacement property's basis rather than erased.

Key Timelines and Receipt Rules

  • 45-day identification: Identify replacement property in a signed writing delivered to a permitted exchange party within 45 days after transferring the relinquished property. The three-property and 200% rules limit identification choices; discuss the applicable rule with the intermediary.
  • Receipt deadline: Receive replacement property by the earlier of 180 days after transfer or the federal return due date, including extensions, for the year of transfer.
  • Actual or constructive receipt: In a typical deferred exchange, arrange a qualified intermediary and compliant restrictions before the sale closes. Receiving proceeds, or having an unrestricted right to them, can trigger taxable treatment even if you later reinvest. Receipt of the full consideration before replacement property generally makes the transaction a sale; partial cash or other non-like-kind consideration can create a partially taxable exchange rather than automatically invalidating all deferral.
  • Boot and basis: Cash, non-like-kind property or net debt relief can cause recognized gain. Model liabilities, expenses, carryover basis and any separately classified assets; buying an equally priced property does not by itself prove complete deferral.

Reverse Exchanges

Buying the replacement property first requires advance structuring. A qualifying exchange accommodation arrangement generally uses an exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT) to hold title under the applicable safe harbor, with its own written-agreement and timing requirements. An ordinary purchase in your own name is not automatically repaired by selling another property later.

Our team coordinates with qualified intermediaries and exchange accommodation titleholders nationwide. Business Attorney Mike Zara reviews every exchange agreement to protect your interests. Review the IRS exchange and receipt rules in Publication 544 and our 1031 exchange planning guide before closing.

7. Self-Rental Rules

The self-rental rule under Treas. Reg. 1.469-2(f)(6) is one of the most commonly overlooked traps in rental property taxation. If you rent property to a trade or business in which you materially participate, any net rental income from that property is recharacterized as nonpassive income. This means it cannot be sheltered by passive losses from other rental activities.

The recharacterization rule does not make a self-rental net loss nonpassive. Losses generally remain passive unless a separate exception or valid grouping applies. The outcome depends on ownership, participation and grouping facts, not just the existence of a related-party lease.

Common Scenarios

Self-rental issues arise frequently when a business owner rents their commercial building to their own S corporation or LLC. The rental income from the property is generally recharacterized as nonpassive, and losses from other passive rental activities generally cannot offset that income. Proper entity structuring, lease terms, and grouping strategies can mitigate the impact of this rule.

Our real estate tax planning team analyzes every self-rental arrangement for optimal structuring before year-end.

8. Grouping Elections for Rental Activities

The general grouping rules in Treas. Reg. 1.469-4 require an appropriate economic unit and restrict when rental and operating-business activities can be combined. Common ownership alone is not enough in every case. Grouping affects participation, suspended losses and what counts as disposing of an entire activity.

Real Estate Election Under Treas. Reg. 1.469-9(g)

The special election for a qualifying real estate professional treats all rental real estate interests as one activity. It is distinct from general grouping and does not supply missing REPS hours or remove other deduction limits. Retain the election statement and review it when properties or participation change.

Regrouping Is Not a General Fresh-Start Right

Revenue Procedure 2010-13 concerns disclosure of groupings and changes; it does not provide a universal one-time regrouping election. A material change or a clearly inappropriate original grouping may justify a change. A separate, limited NIIT regrouping provision under Treas. Reg. 1.469-11(b)(3)(iv) has specific eligibility and timing requirements. Do not regroup simply because a different result would save tax. See IRS Publication 925's grouping and disclosure guidance.

9. Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

The 3.8% NIIT generally applies to the lesser of net investment income or MAGI above the filing-status threshold: $200,000 for single or head-of-household filers, $250,000 for married filing jointly or qualifying surviving spouses, and $125,000 for married filing separately. Rental income and gains may be included.

REPS Is Not an Automatic NIIT Exemption

REPS and material participation address passive status. Exclusion from NIIT also requires the applicable trade-or-business conditions. The real estate professional safe harbor generally requires more than 500 hours in the rental activity in the current year, or in any five of the preceding ten years. A valid all-rentals election affects the activity tested. Failure to meet the safe harbor does not prevent a facts-and-circumstances showing that the exclusion applies.

Coordinate the Separate Tests

Self-rental and qualifying rental/business groupings have specific NIIT rules. Evaluate rental income and disposition gains separately rather than assuming that a nonpassive label removes every tax. Our team reviews NIIT exposure as part of the annual advisory engagement. See the IRS Form 8960 instructions.

10. Record-Keeping Best Practices for Landlords

Keep records that substantiate income, deductions, basis and participation. Reconcile these records to the return and retain prior-year schedules so carryforwards and elections are not lost when you change preparers.

Essential Records

  • Income Documentation: Copies of all lease agreements, rent rolls, security deposit records, and 1099-MISC or 1099-NEC forms received from tenants or property managers.
  • Expense Documentation: Receipts, invoices, and canceled checks for all operating expenses, including repairs, maintenance, insurance, property management fees, utilities, advertising, legal and professional fees, and travel to rental properties.
  • Capital Improvement Records: Invoices, contracts, and photos documenting all capital improvements, with dates and costs. These feed directly into your depreciation schedules.
  • Depreciation Schedules: Maintain current depreciation schedules for each property and each component, including cost segregation detail if applicable.
  • Mileage Logs: If you drive to manage or inspect your properties, keep a contemporaneous mileage log with dates, destinations, purposes, and miles driven.
  • Time Logs for REPS: Track dates, tasks, time and the property or business involved, plus each spouse's working hours and relevant manager or contractor time. Detailed contemporaneous records are useful; other reasonable supporting evidence may also substantiate participation.
  • Closing Statements and Loan Documents: Retain HUD-1 settlement statements, ALTA statements, mortgage documents, and any refinancing paperwork. These establish your cost basis, closing costs, and deductible interest.

Participation, Use and Carryforward Records

Keep personal-use and fair-rental-day calendars, prior grouping elections, cost segregation reports, Form 3115 filings and separate basis, at-risk and passive-loss carryforwards. Include prior exchange documents and replacement-property basis calculations when applicable.

Retention Periods

Keep supporting tax records through the applicable limitation period, ordinarily three years after filing, with longer periods in specified cases. There is no general seven-year rule for every rental loss; the IRS seven-year rule specifically covers claims for worthless securities or bad-debt deductions. Keep property basis and depreciation records until the limitation period expires for the year of taxable disposition, and retain carryover-basis exchange records for the replacement property as needed. AE recommends at least seven years for ordinary rental records and permanent retention of closing statements and depreciation schedules as a practical policy, not a universal statutory rule. See the IRS records-retention guidance.

Scenario A: W-2 Employee With a Growing Rental Portfolio

A married couple earning $280,000 in combined W-2 income owned six residential rental properties generating a combined net rental loss of $45,000 after depreciation. Because their MAGI exceeded $150,000, the $25,000 special allowance under IRC 469(i) was fully phased out. Their prior CPA suspended all $45,000 in losses, meaning they received zero current-year benefit from their rental deductions.

Our team identified that one spouse could qualify as a Real Estate Professional by transitioning from full-time employment to part-time consulting while increasing involvement in property management. After restructuring, filing a grouping election under Treas. Reg. 1.469-9, and performing cost segregation studies on all six properties, the couple claimed $187,000 in first-year deductions against their remaining W-2 and consulting income. The result: a federal tax refund of over $52,000 and state tax savings of $11,000.

Scenario B: Commercial Property Owner Using Partial Dispositions

A landlord who owned a commercial strip mall (39-year property) replaced the entire roof and HVAC system at a cost of $210,000. Their prior CPA simply capitalized the new components and began depreciating them over 39 years, producing a first-year depreciation deduction of approximately $2,700 per year for each improvement.

We filed partial disposition elections under Treas. Reg. 1.168(i)-8 for the old roof and old HVAC system, writing off $135,000 in remaining undepreciated basis in the year of replacement. Combined with a cost segregation study on the new components that reclassified $62,000 into 15-year land improvement property eligible for 100% bonus depreciation, the total first-year deduction exceeded $197,000, compared to approximately $5,400 under the prior CPA's approach.

Scenario C: 1031 Exchange Into a Higher-Cash-Flow Property

A landlord sold a single-family rental that had appreciated from $320,000 to $575,000 over 12 years. Without a 1031 exchange, the capital gains and depreciation recapture taxes would have exceeded $68,000. Our team structured a forward 1031 exchange with a qualified intermediary, identifying a replacement property (a 12-unit apartment building) within the 45-day window. The exchange deferred all $68,000 in taxes, and a cost segregation study on the replacement property generated $142,000 in bonus depreciation deductions in the acquisition year. The client increased monthly cash flow by $3,200 while deferring all taxes on the sale.

Frequently Asked Questions: Rental Property Tax Planning

What is the depreciation period for a residential rental property?

Under the general depreciation system, a residential rental building generally uses 27.5-year straight-line depreciation with the mid-month convention. Land is not depreciable. ADS requirements or elections can change the recovery period, and qualifying separately classified assets may have shorter lives. Depreciation begins when the property is ready and available for rental use.

Can I deduct rental property losses against my W-2 or business income?

Rental losses are generally passive. An eligible active participant may use the limited $25,000 allowance, which generally phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of MAGI for single and joint filers. REPS plus material participation can make a rental nonpassive, but basis, at-risk, personal-use, Section 461(l) and other applicable limits still apply. Neither a cost segregation study nor REPS guarantees a current deduction against wages or business income.

What qualifies as a repair vs. a capital improvement on a rental property?

Betterments, restorations and adaptations generally must be capitalized under Section 263(a). Qualifying repairs may be currently deductible. The de minimis safe harbor can apply to amounts of $2,500 or less per invoice or substantiated item without an applicable financial statement, or $5,000 with one, when accounting-policy, book-expensing and annual-election requirements are met.

How do I qualify for Real Estate Professional Status (REPS)?

For each tax year, one person must perform more than 750 hours and more than half of all personal services in real property trades or businesses in which that person materially participates. Spouses cannot combine hours to meet those REPS tests. A spouse's participation may count toward the separate material-participation test for a rental. Test rentals individually unless a valid all-rentals election applies; employee services generally count toward REPS only if the employee owns more than 5% of the employer.

What is a cost segregation study and how does it benefit landlords?

A study identifies assets eligible for shorter recovery periods based on documented classifications and basis. There is no guaranteed reclassification percentage. Land is excluded, and purchase price is not the same as depreciable basis. Current law generally allows 100% bonus depreciation for eligible assets acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, subject to acquisition, used-property, ADS and other rules. Loss limitations can defer the tax benefit; a later study does not automatically create a new 100% bonus deduction.

What is the 1031 like-kind exchange and how does it work for rental properties?

Section 1031 can defer gain on an exchange of qualifying business or investment real property. In a typical deferred exchange, arrange a qualified intermediary and restrictions on actual or constructive receipt before the sale closes. Identify replacement property within 45 days and receive it by the earlier of 180 days or the return due date, including extensions. Receiving full sale proceeds before replacement property generally makes it a sale; partial cash or other boot can create taxable gain in an otherwise qualifying exchange. Reinvesting proceeds alone is not enough.

How does the Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) apply to rental property owners?

The 3.8% NIIT generally applies to the lesser of net investment income or MAGI above the applicable threshold: $200,000 for single or head-of-household filers, $250,000 for joint filers or qualifying surviving spouses, and $125,000 for married filing separately. REPS and material participation alone do not establish the trade-or-business condition for excluding rent. Review the real estate professional safe harbor or other support for the exclusion and the separate rules for self-rentals.

What is the self-rental rule and how does it affect my taxes?

Under Treas. Reg. 1.469-2(f)(6), net rent from property used in a business in which you materially participate is generally recharacterized as nonpassive. Passive losses from other activities generally cannot offset that income. The rule does not itself make a self-rental loss nonpassive; a separate exception or valid grouping must support that treatment.

Can I group my rental properties together for passive activity purposes?

A qualifying real estate professional can elect under Treas. Reg. 1.469-9(g) to treat all rental real estate interests as one activity. The election does not replace REPS qualification or other loss limits and may delay suspended-loss release when one property is sold. General grouping and NIIT regrouping have separate conditions. Rev. Proc. 2010-13 addresses disclosure, not an unrestricted fresh-start election. Review prior election statements before making a change.

What records should I keep as a rental property owner?

Keep leases, income and expense support, closing statements, land/building allocations, improvement invoices, depreciation schedules, participation records, personal-use calendars, grouping elections and separate loss carryforward schedules. Preserve exchange and Form 3115 records when applicable. Keep property basis records through the limitation period for taxable disposition and longer when needed for carryover basis. The general three-year period has exceptions; seven years is not the statutory rule for every rental loss.

What is the partial disposition rule and how can it save me taxes?

A qualifying partial disposition election may allow a loss for an old building component's remaining adjusted basis when it is replaced. Support the original basis allocation, accumulated depreciation and disposition, and make the election within the applicable deadline. The new component is evaluated separately for capitalization and depreciation. The deduction is subject to applicable loss limits and is not equal to the replacement cost.
