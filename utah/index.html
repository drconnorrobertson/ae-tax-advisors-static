Cost Segregation Study in Utah

Utah tax advisory for real estate investors and business owners | Updated August 2026

Utah conforms fully to the federal Internal Revenue Code, allows bonus depreciation with no addback, and taxes income at a flat 4.5%. That combination makes it one of the easiest states in the country to run a cost segregation study in, with a single depreciation schedule and no state-level complications.

The one Utah wrinkle is worth knowing about, because it makes deductions worth more than the flat rate suggests. Utah's taxpayer tax credit phases out as income rises, so within the phase-out band each dollar of deduction reduces tax at roughly 5.8% rather than 4.5%.

How Utah Income Tax Interacts With Federal Strategy

Utah imposes a single flat rate on all taxable income. The rate has been reduced repeatedly, from 4.95% to 4.85% to 4.65% to 4.55%, and stands at 4.5% following House Bill 106 in the 2025 session.

Utah does not use conventional deductions and exemptions. Instead it computes tax on federal adjusted gross income with limited adjustments and then applies a nonrefundable taxpayer tax credit, which is reduced by 1.3 cents for every dollar of state income above a threshold that depends on filing status.

Inside that phase-out band, the effective marginal rate is the 4.5% statutory rate plus the 1.3% credit reduction, or roughly 5.8%. A cost segregation deduction that lands in the phase-out range is worth about twenty-nine percent more than the headline rate implies, because it both reduces tax directly and restores credit.

Utah taxes capital gains as ordinary income at the flat rate with no preference. Utah does offer a capital gain transactions credit for gains reinvested in a Utah small business corporation within a defined period, which is narrow but occasionally relevant.

Utah conforms to the federal passive activity loss rules of IRC Sec. 469.

Utah Pass-Through Entity Tax

Utah enacted its elective pass-through entity tax through Senate Bill 173 in 2022, retroactive to tax years beginning on or after January 1, 2022. The election is annual.

The rate equals the Utah individual income tax rate for the year, currently 4.5%, applied to the electing entity's Utah income attributable to eligible owners. Owners receive a refundable Utah credit for their share of the tax paid.

The federal benefit is the standard one. The entity deducts the Utah tax on its federal return, converting a capped individual state and local tax deduction into an uncapped business expense. At 4.5% the absolute dollars are moderate, but for a profitable Utah pass-through with owners who are already over the federal cap, the election is straightforward value.

Utah requires the entity to have filed the election and made the payment within the statutory window, so this belongs on the entity's compliance calendar rather than being addressed at extension time.

Utah Depreciation Conformity

Utah is a rolling conformity state. It begins the individual computation with federal adjusted gross income and the corporate computation with federal taxable income, and it does not require an addback of bonus depreciation under IRC Sec. 168(k) or a reduction of Section 179 expensing.

Full federal bonus depreciation is fully deductible for Utah. Full federal Section 179 is fully deductible for Utah. There is no Utah decoupling form and no separate state depreciation schedule.

Utah basis equals federal basis for the life of the asset, so on disposition Utah gain equals federal gain and there is no reconciliation to perform. Section 1245 recapture and unrecaptured Section 1250 gain are both taxed at the same flat 4.5%, meaning the study creates no Utah rate disadvantage on exit.

Cost Segregation Considerations Specific to Utah

Three items are worth building into a Utah model.

First, the phase-out effect. Identify whether the taxpayer sits inside the taxpayer tax credit phase-out band in the placed-in-service year. If so, the Utah benefit of the deduction is roughly 5.8% rather than 4.5%. This also cuts the other way on exit: a large gain that pushes income through the phase-out band is taxed at the higher effective rate on the way up. Modeling the credit phase-out is the one piece of genuine Utah-specific arithmetic in a cost segregation analysis.

Second, timing against the rate schedule. Utah has cut its rate in four of the last five legislative sessions. A Utah deduction is worth marginally more taken sooner rather than later if the trend continues, which points the same direction as the federal time value argument. When evaluating a Form 3115 lookback study on a property placed in service in an earlier year, remember that the catch-up deduction is taken in the current year at the current rate, not at the rate that applied in the original year.

Third, the short-term rental question. Utah has a large short-term rental market around Park City, Moab, St. George, and the Wasatch resorts. The federal short-term rental exception to the passive activity rules under Treasury Regulation 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A), which turns on an average customer use period of seven days or less plus material participation, is what makes a cost segregation study usable against ordinary income for most Utah investors. Local restrictions on nightly rentals vary sharply by municipality in Utah, and a property that cannot legally operate as a short-term rental cannot support the tax position. Confirm the local ordinance before building the tax plan around it.

Utah Cost Segregation and Tax Questions

Does Utah allow bonus depreciation on a cost segregation study?

Yes. Utah is a rolling conformity state that begins with federal adjusted gross income for individuals and federal taxable income for corporations, and it requires no addback of bonus depreciation under IRC Sec. 168(k) or reduction of Section 179. There is no Utah decoupling form and no separate state depreciation schedule. Utah basis equals federal basis through disposition.

Why is a Utah deduction sometimes worth more than the 4.5% flat rate?

Because of the taxpayer tax credit phase-out. Utah applies a nonrefundable credit that is reduced by 1.3 cents for every dollar of state income above a filing-status threshold. Inside that band, a dollar of deduction both lowers tax at 4.5% and restores 1.3 cents of credit, for an effective marginal benefit of roughly 5.8%. The same effect works against you when a large gain pushes income through the band.

What is the Utah pass-through entity tax rate?

The rate equals the Utah individual income tax rate for the year, currently 4.5%, applied to the electing entity's Utah income attributable to eligible owners. The election was created by Senate Bill 173 in 2022 retroactive to that year, is made annually, and gives owners a refundable Utah credit. The election and payment must be made within the statutory window, so it belongs on the compliance calendar.

Can I use a cost segregation study on a Park City or Moab short-term rental?

Usually yes, if the property qualifies federally and can legally operate. The short-term rental exception under Treasury Regulation 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A) turns on an average customer use period of seven days or less plus material participation, and it is what lets the loss offset ordinary income. Utah municipalities vary sharply in how they regulate nightly rentals, so confirm the local ordinance permits the operation before building the tax position on it.

How does Utah tax the gain when I sell a property that had a cost segregation study?

At the same flat 4.5% that applies to everything else. Utah gives no preferential rate to long-term capital gains, so Section 1245 ordinary recapture and unrecaptured Section 1250 gain are taxed identically and the study creates no Utah rate disadvantage on exit. Because Utah conforms to federal depreciation, Utah basis equals federal basis and Utah gain equals federal gain with no reconciliation needed.
