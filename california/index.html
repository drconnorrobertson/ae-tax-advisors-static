Cost Segregation Study in California

California tax advisory for real estate investors and business owners | Updated August 2026

California is the most expensive state in the country to earn income in and one of the least generous on depreciation. Those two facts pull in opposite directions on a cost segregation study, and the resolution is almost always to run the study anyway, because the federal benefit dwarfs the California cost.

What California does demand is that the analysis be run on two sets of books from the beginning. Investors who commission a federal-only report and hand it to a preparer in March discover the California adjustment as a surprise, and surprises in a 13.3% state are expensive.

How California Income Tax Interacts With Federal Strategy

California imposes graduated personal income tax rates topping out at 12.3%, plus a 1% Mental Health Services Tax on taxable income above $1 million, for an effective top rate of 13.3%. There is no preferential rate for long-term capital gains. A California investor selling an appreciated property pays ordinary California rates on the entire gain, including the portion taxed federally at 20%.

The corporate franchise tax rate is 8.84% for C corporations, with a 10.84% rate for financial institutions and an $800 minimum franchise tax that applies to essentially every LLC, LP, and corporation registered in the state. California also imposes an LLC gross receipts fee that scales with total California-source income and is owed regardless of profitability.

The practical effect is that California drives the marginal rate on almost every planning decision. A deduction that saves 37% federally saves another 9.3% to 13.3% in California when California allows it, and saves nothing in California when it does not. Knowing which bucket a given deduction falls into is the entire game here.

California Pass-Through Entity Tax

California enacted an elective pass-through entity tax under AB 150 in 2021, later modified by SB 113. Qualified entities pay 9.3% on the consenting owners' distributive share of California-source income, the entity deducts that payment federally, and the owners claim a credit against their California personal tax.

California's version has real mechanical traps. The election is annual and irrevocable, it requires a prepayment by June 15 of the tax year equal to the greater of $1,000 or 50% of the prior year elected tax, and missing that June 15 payment disqualifies the entity for the entire year. There is no cure. We have seen owners lose a six-figure federal deduction over a missed June deadline.

The election is also owner by owner. Not every partner has to consent, which matters in partnerships with nonresident or tax-exempt members whose economics differ. Single-member LLCs disregarded for federal purposes do not qualify at all, which is a common structuring problem for California real estate held one property per LLC.

The credit is nonrefundable and is applied after certain other credits, so owners with large California credits from other sources can generate an elected tax payment they cannot fully use. Modeling the credit ordering before electing is part of the work, not an afterthought.

California Depreciation Conformity

California does not conform to IRC Sec. 168(k). Bonus depreciation is not available for California purposes for either the personal income tax or the corporation tax. California depreciation is computed under the pre-1987 or MACRS rules that California has adopted, with no special first-year allowance.

California's Section 179 expensing limit is $25,000 with a $200,000 investment phaseout, unchanged for decades and radically below the federal limit that now exceeds $1.2 million. For an investor placing $400,000 of five-year and fifteen-year property in service, California allows $25,000 of expensing and MACRS on the rest.

This means every California taxpayer running a cost segregation study maintains two depreciation schedules for the life of the assets: the federal schedule with the accelerated first-year deduction, and the California schedule recovering the same basis over the full MACRS lives. The difference reverses over time, so California is a timing cost, not a permanent one, but the timing runs for decades.

Cost Segregation Considerations Specific to California

The first California-specific point is that the study still wins on the math. A $1 million property with a 25% reclassification generates roughly $250,000 of accelerated federal deduction. At a 37% federal rate that is about $92,500 of federal tax deferred into the current year. The California cost is not a payment, it is simply the absence of a corresponding state deduction, which the taxpayer recovers over the MACRS life.

The second is that California conforms to the federal passive activity loss rules of IRC Sec. 469 with modifications, so the material participation analysis that governs whether a short-term rental loss is usable federally generally governs in California too. A California investor who qualifies under the seven day average stay rule and materially participates has a usable loss on both returns, subject to California's smaller depreciation deduction.

The third is depreciation recapture on sale. Because California basis is higher than federal basis after a cost segregation study, the California gain on sale is smaller than the federal gain. That is the reversal working in your favor, and it is routinely missed by preparers who use the federal basis on the California return. On a large disposition the error runs into five figures of overpaid California tax.

The fourth is that California has an aggressive residency and sourcing regime. Investors who own California real estate but live elsewhere still file a California nonresident return on the California-source income, and California-source rental losses are tracked separately. Moving out of state does not release California property from California tax.

California Cost Segregation and Tax Questions

Does California allow bonus depreciation?

No. California does not conform to IRC Sec. 168(k) for either the personal income tax or the corporation tax. Depreciation for California purposes is computed on the regular recovery periods with no first-year special allowance, which means a cost segregation study produces a federal deduction and a California timing difference that reverses over the life of the assets.

What is California's Section 179 limit?

$25,000, with a $200,000 investment phaseout. That is roughly two percent of the current federal limit. On a typical cost segregation study the California Section 179 allowance is exhausted immediately and the remaining reclassified basis recovers on regular MACRS for California purposes.

How does the California pass-through entity tax work?

Under AB 150 a qualified entity may elect to pay 9.3% on consenting owners' California-source income. The entity deducts the payment federally and owners claim a nonrefundable California credit. The election is annual and requires a prepayment by June 15 of the tax year equal to the greater of $1,000 or 50% of the prior year elected tax. Missing that payment disqualifies the entity for the whole year with no cure.

Is cost segregation worth it in California?

Usually yes. California's non-conformity removes the state-side deduction but does not remove the federal one, and the federal benefit at up to 37% is the larger number. The correct way to size the decision is to model the federal acceleration against the study cost and treat California as a timing difference that reverses rather than as a cost.

Do I owe California tax on a rental I own there if I live in another state?

Yes. California taxes nonresidents on California-source income, which includes rental income from California real property and gain on its sale. You file a California nonresident return, and California-source passive losses are tracked separately from your resident state. Relocating does not remove California property from California tax.
