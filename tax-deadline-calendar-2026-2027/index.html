Tax Deadline Calendar 2026-2027 for Business Owners

By AE Tax Advisors Team · Published October 3, 2026 · Updated October 3, 2026

Your complete month-by-month guide to every federal tax deadline, quarterly estimated payment, payroll deposit, retirement contribution cutoff, and international filing requirement from October 2026 through December 2027.

Missing a tax deadline can cost business owners thousands of dollars in penalties, interest, and lost deduction opportunities. Whether you are filing as a sole proprietor, an S-Corp or LLC, a C-Corporation, or a partnership, there are dozens of critical dates throughout the year that demand your attention. This comprehensive tax deadline calendar covers every major federal filing obligation from October 2026 through December 2027, organized month by month with strategic planning notes to help you stay ahead.

Q4 2026: October, November, December

The fourth quarter of 2026 is a critical period for business owners. Extended returns come due, year-end tax planning decisions must be finalized, and retirement plan contributions need attention before December 31.

October 2026

DateDeadlineDetails
October 15Extended Individual Returns (Form 1040)Final deadline for individuals who filed Form 4868 for a six-month extension. All tax due was payable by April 15; late payment penalties and interest apply to any unpaid balance.
October 15Extended C-Corp Returns (Form 1120)Calendar-year C-Corporations that filed Form 7004 must submit their completed return by this date.
October 15FBAR Automatic Extension DeadlineFinCEN Report 114 (FBAR) for foreign financial accounts exceeding $10,000 in aggregate value. The original deadline is April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15. No separate extension form is required.
October 15SEP-IRA Contribution Deadline (Extended Returns)Last day to establish and fund a SEP-IRA for the 2025 tax year if you filed an extension on your individual return. November 2026

November has no major federal filing deadlines, but it is one of the most important months for year-end tax planning. This is the time to review your projected income, evaluate entity structure decisions, plan equipment purchases under Section 179 and bonus depreciation, and schedule a year-end planning call with your tax advisor.

  • Review projected annual income and adjust Q4 estimated payments if necessary
  • Evaluate whether an S-Corp election or entity restructuring makes sense for 2027
  • Identify equipment purchases, vehicle acquisitions, or property improvements that qualify for accelerated depreciation
  • Consider cost segregation studies on any properties acquired during the year
  • Review charitable contribution strategy and donor-advised fund contributions

State Filing Deadlines: General Guidance

State tax obligations vary significantly across jurisdictions. While most states align their income tax return deadlines with the federal due dates (April 15 for individuals, March 15 for S-Corps and partnerships), there are important exceptions and additional obligations to consider.

States with No Income Tax

Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire (interest and dividends only, phasing out), South Dakota, Tennessee (interest and dividends fully repealed), Texas, Washington, and Wyoming do not impose a state income tax. However, Texas imposes a franchise tax, Washington has a business and occupation (B&O) tax, and Nevada has a commerce tax for businesses exceeding $4 million in gross revenue. Always confirm whether your state imposes alternative business taxes even if it does not have an income tax.

State Estimated Tax Payments

Most states that impose an income tax also require quarterly estimated tax payments, generally following the federal schedule: April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15. Some states use slightly different dates. Business owners operating in multiple states should work with their advisor to determine nexus obligations and file in every state where filing is required.

Pass-Through Entity Tax Elections

Many states now offer pass-through entity tax (PTET) elections that allow S-Corps and partnerships to pay state income tax at the entity level, generating a federal deduction that bypasses the $10,000 SALT cap. Election deadlines vary by state and are often due before the start of the tax year or with the return. This is an area where proactive planning can save significant taxes. Strategic Planning by Quarter

Beyond simply meeting deadlines, each quarter offers distinct opportunities for proactive tax planning. Here is how to think about each period strategically.

Q4 (October to December): Year-End Optimization

This is the most important quarter for tax savings. Review your income projections, accelerate deductible expenses, defer income where possible, purchase equipment that qualifies for Section 179 or bonus depreciation, make retirement plan contributions, and finalize charitable giving strategies. If you acquired real property during the year, commission a cost segregation study before year-end to maximize first-year depreciation deductions.

Q1 (January to March): Filing Season Preparation

Ensure all information returns (W-2s, 1099s) are issued on time. Gather documents and organize records for return preparation. File pass-through entity returns (S-Corp and partnership) by March 15 or request extensions. Review prior-year returns for missed deductions or amendment opportunities.

Q2 (April to June): Filing and Mid-Year Checkup

File individual and C-Corp returns or extensions by April 15. Make first and second quarter estimated payments. Conduct a mid-year income review to ensure estimated payments are adequate and identify any planning opportunities for the second half of the year.

Q3 (July to September): Pre-Year-End Planning

File extended S-Corp and partnership returns by September 15. Make third quarter estimated payments. Begin year-end planning discussions with your advisor. Review entity structure, retirement plan design, and any planned transactions that will affect year-end tax positions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if I miss a quarterly estimated tax payment?

If you miss a quarterly estimated tax payment, the IRS will assess an underpayment penalty calculated using the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points. The penalty accrues from the date the payment was due until it is paid or until the return due date, whichever comes first. You may qualify for a penalty waiver if your total tax owed is less than $1,000, if you paid at least 90% of the current year tax or 100% of the prior year tax (110% for high-income filers), or if you experienced a casualty, disaster, or unusual circumstance. Filing Form 2210 allows you to calculate whether an exception applies.

Can I get an extension for my business tax return, and does it extend my payment deadline?

Yes, you can file for an automatic extension using Form 7004 for business returns or Form 4868 for individual returns. However, an extension to file is not an extension to pay. All taxes owed are still due by the original filing deadline. If you do not pay the full amount by the original due date, the IRS will charge interest and may assess a failure-to-pay penalty of 0.5% per month on the unpaid balance, up to a maximum of 25%. For S-Corps and partnerships, late filing penalties are $220 per month per partner or shareholder, making timely filing especially important for multi-owner entities.

When is the deadline for SEP-IRA and Solo 401(k) contributions?

SEP-IRA contributions are due by the tax return filing deadline, including extensions. For sole proprietors, this means contributions can be made as late as October 15 if you file an extension on your personal return. For S-Corps and C-Corps, the deadline follows the corporate return extension date. Solo 401(k) plans have different rules: employee salary deferral elections must be made by December 31 of the plan year, but employer profit-sharing contributions can be made until the business tax return deadline, including extensions. The plan itself must be established by December 31 to make contributions for that tax year.

What is the FBAR deadline and who needs to file?

The FBAR (FinCEN Report 114) is due on April 15 each year, with an automatic extension to October 15. You must file an FBAR if you had a financial interest in, or signature authority over, one or more foreign financial accounts and the aggregate value of all foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during the calendar year. This includes foreign bank accounts, foreign brokerage accounts, and certain foreign mutual funds. Penalties for willful failure to file can reach $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per violation, so compliance is critical. The FBAR is filed electronically through the BSA E-Filing System, not with your tax return.

How do state estimated tax payment deadlines differ from federal deadlines?

Most states follow the federal quarterly estimated tax schedule of April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15. However, several states have different deadlines or requirements. For example, some states require estimated payments only if your expected tax liability exceeds a certain threshold. A few states, such as Illinois and Iowa, use slightly different quarterly dates. States without income tax, such as Texas, Florida, Wyoming, Nevada, South Dakota, Alaska, and Washington, do not require estimated income tax payments but may have franchise tax or gross receipts tax obligations with their own deadlines. Always verify your specific state requirements, especially if you operate in multiple states.
