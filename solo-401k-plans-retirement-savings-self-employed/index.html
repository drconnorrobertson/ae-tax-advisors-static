Solo 401(k) Plans for Self-Employed Owners: 2026 Rules

Reviewed November 6, 2026. Tax-year 2026 limits are identified separately from examples.

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

A solo 401(k) can combine employee deferrals and employer contributions for a self-employed owner, or owner and spouse, without eligible common-law employees. For 2026, the non-catch-up annual additions limit is $72,000, subject to eligible compensation and plan terms. A single owner cannot assume six-figure annual contributions to a solo 401(k) alone; permitted catch-up contributions are separate.

This guide covers contribution calculations, Roth treatment, participant loans, plan-owned real estate, adoption deadlines and annual filing. Before choosing a plan, review earned income, any eligible employees, related businesses and contributions at other jobs. Ordinary rental income generally does not generate self-employment earnings for plan contributions merely because the owner actively manages property; property services or other business facts can change that analysis.

Solo 401(k) Contribution Architecture

A Solo 401(k) accepts contributions from two sources: employee deferrals and employer contributions. Understanding the interplay between these two contribution streams is fundamental to maximizing your annual contribution capacity.

Employee elective deferrals for 2026 are limited to $24,500 before catch-up, not an amount per account. The limit generally follows the individual across applicable employer retirement plans, so deferrals at another job reduce available solo 401(k) room. For reference, the limits were $23,000 in 2024 and $23,500 in 2025.

Employer contributions are generally up to 25% of eligible W-2 compensation for an S-corporation owner; shareholder distributions do not count. For a sole proprietor, a stated 25% plan rate generally becomes 20% of adjusted net earnings after subtracting the deductible part of self-employment tax. The owner contribution itself affects the compensation calculation. Use the Publication 560 worksheets and the combined contribution and compensation limits rather than taking 25% of Schedule C profit.

For a simple 2026 sole-proprietor illustration, assume $200,000 of adjusted net earnings after the deductible part of self-employment tax, before the owner contribution adjustment; the owner is under 50, has no other deferrals and no eligible employees. A 25% stated employer rate produces $40,000 under the 20% reduced-rate calculation. Adding $24,500 of deferrals gives $64,500, below the $72,000 annual additions limit. This is not $50,000 plus deferrals, and the compensation worksheet still must be checked.

The 2026 Contribution Limits and Other Plans

The 2026 annual additions limit is generally the lesser of $72,000 or 100% of eligible compensation, excluding permitted catch-up. Plans of the same employer or employers required to be aggregated share applicable limits. Genuinely unrelated employers may have separate annual additions limits, while the employee deferral limit still follows the individual.

Multiple LLCs do not automatically create multiple contribution ceilings. Controlled-group and affiliated-service-group rules can aggregate ownership, employee coverage and limits. An owner can split a permitted employee deferral between plans but cannot claim a fresh $24,500 limit in each. For 2026, permitted catch-up is $8,000 at age 50 or older, or $11,250 instead for participants attaining ages 60 through 63. The plan must support the provision.

Roth Component Advantages

A solo 401(k) may include designated Roth contributions. They are after-tax and share the employee deferral limit with traditional deferrals. A qualified Roth distribution generally requires the five-tax-year participation period and age 59½, death or disability. Nonqualified distributions and conversions have additional rules.

Roth deferrals may make sense when the current tax rate is expected to be lower than the rate on future distributions. For 2026, an eligible owner could allocate some or all of the $24,500 ordinary elective deferral limit to Roth, but traditional and Roth deferrals share that limit. Participants with prior-year wages from the sponsoring employer exceeding $150,000 generally must make 2026 catch-up contributions as Roth. This wage test does not treat net self-employment earnings or household income as employer wages.

Roth deferrals do not reduce your current-year taxable income, unlike traditional deferrals. If you need an immediate tax deduction, traditional deferrals take priority. Conversely, if you have optimized retirement contributions and wish to build additional tax-free assets, Roth deferrals within the plan offer excellent upside.

If the plan permits an in-plan Roth rollover, converting pre-tax funds generally adds the taxable amount to income in the conversion year; it does not create a deduction in that year. Previously deducted contributions and untaxed earnings are not made tax-free by conversion. Model conversion tax, basis and distribution rules before proceeding.

Loan Provisions and Liquidity

A solo 401(k) may permit participant loans, but the written plan does not have to offer them. The normal limit is the lesser of $50,000 or 50% of the vested account balance, subject to statutory exceptions, the plan document, and aggregation of loans from plans of the employer and related employers. Existing and recent loans can reduce what is available.

A compliant loan needs an enforceable written agreement, a reasonable interest rate and substantially level principal-and-interest repayments at least quarterly, normally over no more than five years. A qualifying principal-residence acquisition can have a longer term under plan rules; buying an investment rental is not that exception. Loan repayments are not new retirement contributions and do not create another deduction.

With a $150,000 vested solo 401(k) account and no relevant prior or outstanding loans, the normal maximum participant loan is $50,000: the lesser of $50,000 and $75,000. It is not $75,000. The $50,000 cap is reduced by the excess of the highest outstanding plan-loan balance during the preceding 12 months over the outstanding balance on the new-loan date; current outstanding loans also count toward the amount permitted. For example, if the prior 12-month high was $20,000 and the current balance is zero, the adjusted dollar cap is $30,000. A participant may use a compliant loan for a personally owned investment, but that is different from the plan itself owning the property.

Track actual repayments, not just a note on paper. A loan that violates the terms or repayment rules can become a deemed taxable distribution and may incur the 10% additional tax on early distributions unless an exception applies. Cure periods, offsets and rollover rules are fact-specific. A custodian or administrator should review the loan before funds move.

Real Estate Investment Within Solo 401(k)s

Some self-directed plans allow the trust to own real estate. That is a different transaction from the participant borrowing money and buying an asset personally. The trust owns the investment, pays its expenses, and receives its income; the participant does not claim personal depreciation or expense deductions for property held inside the plan.

For plan-owned property, purchase documents and title must reflect the authorized plan ownership structure. All income and expenses must be handled by the plan. Traditional retirement assets generally grow tax-deferred, not permanently tax-free, and distributions are generally taxable. Unrelated business income, debt-financed income, and any applicable qualified-plan exception need separate tax review.

Illustration only: a plan uses $100,000 of existing assets and $300,000 of permissible nonrecourse financing to buy property for $400,000. Financing must be reviewed for prohibited transactions and any unrelated business or debt-financed income exposure. If the property later is worth $600,000, the $200,000 appreciation stays inside the retirement arrangement until distributed, subject to applicable plan-level tax rules. A later distribution is valued using the then-current interest actually distributed, including liabilities and basis where relevant, not automatically the original price or a tax-free capital gain.

Confirm that the plan document and provider permit direct real estate, borrowing, valuations and the required reporting. A specialist provider can help administer the investment, but provider approval does not make a prohibited transaction permissible.

Prohibited Transaction Caution

Section 4975 restricts sales, leases, loans, services and use of plan assets involving disqualified persons, including the owner and specified family members or controlled entities. Do not sell personally owned property to the plan, use a plan-owned rental for personal stays, or personally guarantee its debt. Services, compensation and any statutory exemption require qualified review. A compliant participant loan can fall within a specific exemption; it does not authorize unrestricted dealings between the owner and plan.

A prohibited transaction can create excise taxes and require correction; disqualification or other tax consequences depend on the violation. Do not assume all family members are treated identically, that every service is forbidden, or that every owner action is harmless. Obtain an independent prohibited-transaction review before committing plan funds.

Plan Administration and Documentation

Establishing a Solo 401(k) requires a written plan document. Most custodians offer prototype plans that meet IRS qualification requirements without custom drafting. You complete an adoption agreement selecting plan terms, contribution elections, and investment options.

Most qualified employer plans may be adopted by the employer tax-return due date, including extensions, for the prior tax year. That does not extend every employee deferral election or deposit deadline. Employee deferrals generally require an election before compensation becomes available, and a later adoption cannot recreate S-corporation payroll deferrals for wages already paid.

A narrow first-year exception permits a sole proprietor who owns the entire unincorporated business and is its only employee to adopt a new 401(k) and make first-year elective deferrals by the unextended individual return deadline. Employer contribution timing and deductibility have separate return-deadline rules. Have the provider confirm adoption, election, deposit and tax-year treatment rather than relying on a blanket December 31 or October 15 rule.

Investment Flexibility and Control

Solo 401(k)s offer exceptional investment flexibility. You direct investments into stocks, bonds, mutual funds, exchange-traded funds, and alternative investments. Some custodians permit precious metals and other alternative holdings within the plan.

Investment authority depends on the plan document and provider. Concentration, illiquidity, valuations and leverage can create retirement and compliance risks even when an investment is permitted. Keep enough cash to meet expenses, distributions and any loan obligations.

Coordination with Business Structuring

For an S-corporation owner, eligible W-2 wages support the employer contribution; distributions do not. W-2 wages are generally subject to payroll taxes, not self-employment tax. Retirement contribution design must be coordinated with defensible reasonable compensation and the actual payroll records.

A 2026 S-corporation illustration for an owner under 50 with no other deferrals: $150,000 of eligible W-2 wages supports a $37,500 employer contribution at 25%. Adding $24,500 of employee deferrals yields $62,000. At $200,000 of wages, 25% would be $50,000, but a full $24,500 deferral leaves only $47,500 under the $72,000 non-catch-up additions limit. Raising wages also changes payroll taxes and business cash flow; it is not a cost-free way to raise contributions.

This payroll strategy must remain reasonable. The IRS scrutinizes S-Corporation owner compensation to ensure W-2 wages reflect reasonable compensation for services. Artificially low wages to minimize payroll taxes invite audits and compensation adjustments. Coordination must balance retirement contribution optimization with reasonable compensation documentation.

Key Implementation Steps

Choose a provider and written plan that support the intended contributions, investments and any loans. Document eligible earnings, outside-plan deferrals, ownership and employee eligibility before funding. Record timely elections and the designated contribution tax year. Review Form 5500-EZ obligations: generally file when combined year-end assets of the employer's one-participant plans exceed $250,000, and file a final return on termination regardless of the asset threshold. The form is not Form 5500-N, and this asset threshold is a filing rule, not a universal independent-audit trigger.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can a business owner contribute to a retirement plan?

For 2026, the elective deferral limit is $24,500 and the defined contribution annual additions limit is $72,000, excluding permitted catch-up contributions. The ordinary age-50-or-older catch-up is $8,000; participants attaining ages 60 through 63 may use $11,250 instead, if permitted. Catch-up contributions for participants whose prior-year wages from the sponsoring employer exceeded $150,000 generally must be Roth in 2026. That wage test is not household income or net self-employment earnings, and Roth contributions are not current deductions.

Can I have both a 401(k) and a cash balance plan?

The 6% figure is not a universal contribution or deduction cap. Under Section 404(a)(7), employer defined contribution amounts up to 6% of covered compensation are excluded from the combined deduction calculation; when employer contributions exceed 6%, the excess enters the applicable combined test. Employee elective deferrals are excluded. A PBGC-covered defined benefit plan is excluded from this combined limit, but separate plan limits still apply. Have the actuary and tax preparer calculate both the minimum funding and maximum deductible contribution.

When must a retirement plan be established?

Most qualified employer plans may be adopted by the employer tax-return due date, including extensions, and treated as adopted for the prior tax year. Adoption, employee deferral elections, deposit deadlines, and employer contribution deductibility are separate tests. Salary deferrals generally require a timely election before compensation becomes available; adopting a plan later does not authorize retroactive S-corporation payroll deferrals. A narrow first-year exception permits an individual who owns the entire unincorporated business and is its only employee to adopt a new 401(k) and make first-year elective deferrals by the unextended individual return deadline.

What is a backdoor Roth contribution?

A backdoor Roth strategy combines a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution with a Roth conversion. Traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRA balances are aggregated under the pro-rata rule to determine taxable conversion income. The conversion is not itself a deduction, and IRA basis and year-end balances must be reported correctly.
