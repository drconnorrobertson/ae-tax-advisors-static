Solo 401(k) vs SEP IRA for Profitable Businesses

Reviewed November 6, 2026. Tax-year 2026 limits are identified separately from examples.

A solo 401(k) can reach the shared contribution ceiling with less compensation. Compare timing, employee costs, Roth options, and administration before choosing.

Request Your Free Tax Assessment

By AE Tax Advisors Team · Billings, Montana · Updated 2026-11-06

A SEP IRA accepts employer retirement contributions; a solo 401(k) adds employee elective deferrals. The solo 401(k) can allow more at moderate compensation, but both can reach the same $72,000 non-catch-up ceiling in 2026. The best choice depends on eligible earnings, deferrals already made at another job, employees, deadlines, and administrative cost.

The Structural Difference

For 2026, the elective deferral limit is $24,500 and the defined contribution annual additions limit is $72,000, excluding permitted catch-up contributions. The ordinary age-50-or-older catch-up is $8,000; participants attaining ages 60 through 63 may use $11,250 instead, if permitted. Catch-up contributions for participants whose prior-year wages from the sponsoring employer exceeded $150,000 generally must be Roth in 2026. That wage test is not household income or net self-employment earnings, and Roth contributions are not current deductions.

Employer contributions are generally limited to 25% of eligible W-2 compensation. For a self-employed owner, a stated 25% rate generally becomes 20% of adjusted net earnings after the deductible part of self-employment tax; use the Publication 560 worksheets rather than multiplying Schedule C profit by 25%. Contributions also cannot exceed the applicable compensation limits.

For a 2026 owner under 50 with $100,000 of eligible W-2 pay, no other deferrals and no employees, a 25% employer contribution is $25,000. A SEP can receive $25,000; a solo 401(k) can receive that plus $24,500 of employee deferrals, totaling $49,500. At $288,000 of eligible pay, a 25% SEP contribution reaches $72,000, matching the solo 401(k) non-catch-up ceiling. These are contribution illustrations, not a recommendation to change reasonable compensation.

The employee deferral limit follows the individual across applicable employer plans. Deferrals already made at a day job reduce the remaining solo 401(k) deferral room. Annual additions are generally aggregated for plans of the same or related employers; genuinely unrelated employers may have separate limits. Review controlled groups and affiliated service groups before assuming separate businesses create separate capacity.

Where the Solo 401(k) Wins

Contribution room at moderate compensation. Employee deferrals can fill a gap that percentage-limited employer contributions cannot, provided the owner has eligible earnings and unused deferral room.

Roth flexibility. A plan may allow designated Roth deferrals and in-plan Roth conversions. SECURE 2.0 also permits Roth SEP contributions where the arrangement and provider support them, so Roth treatment is not exclusive to a 401(k). Roth employee deferrals do not reduce the participant's current taxable income. Employer Roth SEP contributions are generally included in the participant's income when deposited, while the employer deduction is evaluated separately under the SEP deduction rules.

Loan provisions. A solo 401(k) may permit participant loans within statutory and plan limits; SEP IRAs do not permit participant loans.

Cash balance coordination. A 401(k) can be paired with a defined benefit plan, subject to census testing and combined deduction calculations. A SEP is not automatically barred from another plan, but the IRS model Form 5305-SEP cannot be used when maintaining another qualified plan, apart from its stated exception. Obtain a compatible document and design review.

Backdoor Roth planning. Pre-tax traditional SEP IRA balances enter the IRA pro-rata calculation, along with traditional and SIMPLE IRAs. Qualified-plan balances do not. A rollover of eligible pre-tax SEP assets into a 401(k) that accepts them may help, but review basis, year-end balances, and rollover eligibility first.

Where the SEP IRA Still Wins

Simple administration. A SEP still needs a written agreement, required employee disclosures, and an IRA for each eligible participant. It generally has no annual Form 5500 filing. A solo 401(k) requires a qualified plan document and may trigger Form 5500-EZ filing when total one-participant plan assets exceed $250,000 at year-end; a final return is generally required on termination regardless of assets.

Prior-year employer funding. A SEP can generally be established and funded by the extended employer return deadline. A solo 401(k) can also be adopted after year-end for eligible employer contributions; missing December 31 does not automatically make a SEP the only option.

Most qualified employer plans may be adopted by the employer tax-return due date, including extensions, and treated as adopted for the prior tax year. Adoption, employee deferral elections, deposit deadlines, and employer contribution deductibility are separate tests. Salary deferrals generally require a timely election before compensation becomes available; adopting a plan later does not authorize retroactive S-corporation payroll deferrals. A narrow first-year exception permits an individual who owns the entire unincorporated business and is its only employee to adopt a new 401(k) and make first-year elective deferrals by the unextended individual return deadline.

Variable income. SEP employer contributions can be discretionary, including zero for a year. Discretionary profit sharing can also be available in a 401(k); neither label removes promised safe harbor or other required contributions.

The Employee Problem Applies to Both

A SEP generally requires the same contribution percentage for every eligible participant under the plan formula, which can make owner contributions costly when staff qualify. A 401(k) covering employees has coverage, nondiscrimination and reporting requirements; safe harbor designs address some testing but do not remove every obligation.

A solo 401(k) generally covers only an owner, or owner and spouse, without eligible common-law employees. Review employee service, including applicable long-term part-time rules, and all controlled or affiliated businesses before using an owner-only plan. Ordinary rental receipts and S-corporation distributions do not themselves create eligible retirement-plan compensation.

How to Choose

Bring projected eligible W-2 pay or adjusted self-employment earnings, employee census, all related businesses, outside-plan deferrals, existing IRA balances, and the tax year you want to fund. Compare total owner savings, required staff contributions, administration, Roth treatment, and cash needed by each deadline.

A solo 401(k) often fits an eligible owner seeking maximum contributions at moderate compensation or a compatible cash balance design. A SEP may fit an owner prioritizing simpler administration who does not need loans or employee deferrals. At sufficient compensation both can reach the same non-catch-up limit. Confirm provider support and obtain the written calculation before adopting or rolling funds.

Key Takeaways

  • Both plans can reach the $72,000 non-catch-up ceiling in 2026; a solo 401(k) can reach it with less compensation.
  • Other employer-plan deferrals reduce the individual deferral room available to a solo 401(k).
  • Both plans require written arrangements; a SEP generally avoids annual Form 5500 filing.
  • After-year-end employer adoption is possible; deferral elections and funding have separate deadlines.
  • Roth SEP contributions may be available, and employee or related-business coverage can change the answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which allows a larger contribution, a solo 401(k) or a SEP IRA?

At moderate compensation, a solo 401(k) may permit more because it adds unused employee deferral room to employer contributions. Both can reach the $72,000 non-catch-up annual additions limit for 2026 at sufficient eligible compensation. Outside-plan deferrals and related-employer limits must be coordinated.

Can I set up a retirement plan after the year ends?

Most qualified employer plans may be adopted by the employer tax-return due date, including extensions, and treated as adopted for the prior tax year. Adoption, employee deferral elections, deposit deadlines, and employer contribution deductibility are separate tests. Salary deferrals generally require a timely election before compensation becomes available; adopting a plan later does not authorize retroactive S-corporation payroll deferrals. A narrow first-year exception permits an individual who owns the entire unincorporated business and is its only employee to adopt a new 401(k) and make first-year elective deferrals by the unextended individual return deadline.

Does a SEP IRA interfere with a backdoor Roth?

Pre-tax SEP IRA balances count in the pro-rata calculation for traditional IRA conversions, along with traditional and SIMPLE IRA balances. A solo 401(k) balance does not. Any rollover into a 401(k) must be eligible and accepted by the plan, with IRA basis reviewed separately.

Can I have both a SEP and a solo 401(k)?

It may be possible with compatible plan documents, but plans of the same or related employer share applicable annual additions limits. IRS model Form 5305-SEP generally cannot be used while maintaining another qualified plan. A second account does not double the deduction.
