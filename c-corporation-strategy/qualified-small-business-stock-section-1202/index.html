Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) and IRC Section 1202

Acquisition-date-based QSBS limits, holding periods and eligibility checks for C corporation owners planning a stock sale.

← Back to Complete Guide

Section 1202 can exclude part or all of eligible gain when a noncorporate taxpayer sells qualifying domestic C corporation stock. Start with the stock's acquisition and issuance dates: the 2025 law changed the holding periods, per-issuer dollar limit and gross-assets ceiling for newer stock. Entity status, original issuance, business activity, redemptions and records still determine whether the stock qualifies. The eligible-gain ceiling is the greater of the remaining per-issuer dollar limit or 10 times the adjusted basis of that issuer's QSBS sold during the tax year, ignoring additions to basis after original issuance. The dollar limit is $10 million for stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025, or $15 million for stock acquired after July 4, 2025 (the 2026 amount, with inflation adjustments beginning after 2026). Prior eligible gain from the same issuer reduces the dollar limit; mixed old and new lots require coordination, not two independent allowances. Apply the applicable exclusion percentage to the eligible gain after this limit.

This benefit is available exclusively to C corporation shareholders. S corporations, LLCs, partnerships, and sole proprietorships cannot issue QSBS. For business owners who are building a company with the intention of selling it, this provision alone can justify the C corporation structure. Our comprehensive QSBS guide covers the full breadth of Section 1202 planning, but this chapter focuses on how QSBS fits within the broader C corporation tax strategy.

The Five Requirements for QSBS Eligibility

To qualify for the Section 1202 exclusion, five requirements must be met. Each one is strictly enforced, and failure to satisfy any single requirement disqualifies the stock entirely.

Requirement 1: The Stock Must Be C Corporation Stock

The issuing entity must be a domestic C corporation at the time the stock is issued and throughout substantially all of the taxpayer's holding period. If the corporation elects S corporation status at any point during the holding period, the stock loses QSBS eligibility. This is one of the most common planning traps: a founder who starts as a C corporation, converts to an S corporation for a few years to avoid double taxation on current distributions, and then converts back to a C corporation before selling has likely destroyed their QSBS eligibility.

Section 1202 requires C corporation status during substantially all of the holding period. There is no general statutory 80%-of-time safe harbor for this phrase. The separate 80% rule measures active-business assets by value; do not use it to excuse a period of S corporation status.

Requirement 2: The Stock Must Be Acquired at Original Issuance

The taxpayer generally must acquire original-issue stock directly from the corporation for money, property other than stock, or services. Secondary purchases generally do not qualify, subject to specific carryover rules such as gifts and transfers at death. Revoking an S election does not turn existing S corporation shares into QSBS and does not create a deemed new stock issuance. A genuine new original issuance by an eligible domestic C corporation must be analyzed separately. Incorporating an LLC taxed as a partnership or disregarded entity can involve qualifying original-issue C corporation stock in exchange for property, if all requirements are met. The qualifying holding period generally starts at that incorporation, and Section 1202(i) prevents pre-contribution appreciation from becoming excludable gain.

This requirement means that QSBS planning must begin at the formation of the corporation or at the time of a new capital contribution. A business owner who has been operating as an S corporation for ten years and converts to a C corporation will need to issue new stock in exchange for new consideration to start the QSBS clock. The existing stock does not retroactively qualify.

There are important exceptions. Stock received as compensation for services qualifies if the recipient recognizes the value as income (such as through an IRC Section 83(b) election). Stock received in certain tax-free reorganizations can inherit QSBS status from the predecessor stock under IRC Section 1202(h). And stock received by gift retains its QSBS character in the hands of the donee, though the holding period does not reset.

Requirement 3: The Corporation Must Be an Active Business (the 80% Test)

During substantially all of the taxpayer's holding period, at least 80% of the corporation's assets (by value) must be used in the active conduct of one or more qualified trades or businesses. This is known as the active business requirement or the 80% asset test.

At least 80% of assets by value must be used in one or more qualified active trades or businesses during substantially all of the holding period. This is an asset test, not an 80% gross-income or revenue test. Reasonably required working capital and qualifying investment funds expected to be used within two years for research or increased working capital can count, but after the corporation has existed for at least two years no more than 50% of its assets may qualify solely under the working-capital rule. Separate limits apply to portfolio securities and nonbusiness real estate.

This requirement creates tension for profitable C corporations that accumulate cash. If the corporation retains $5 million in earnings and invests that cash in a stock portfolio while the operating business assets total only $3 million, the 80% test may fail. Careful asset management is required to ensure that retained earnings are deployed in ways that satisfy the active business requirement.

Requirement 4: The Applicable Gross-Assets Ceiling

For stock issued on or before July 4, 2025, aggregate gross assets must not exceed $50 million; for stock issued after July 4, 2025, the ceiling is $75 million in 2026. The test covers the corporation and any predecessor at all times after August 9, 1993 and before issuance, and immediately after issuance including the new proceeds. Parent-subsidiary controlled-group rules also apply. Gross assets generally mean cash plus adjusted tax basis of other property, with contributed property measured using fair market value at contribution. Enterprise valuation is not the same measure.

Once validly issued, stock is not disqualified solely because the corporation later grows beyond the applicable gross-assets ceiling. But the issuance test includes prior history, not only the balance sheet on one date.

Later stock issuances need their own review of the applicable ceiling, predecessor and controlled-group assets, amounts raised, and contributed-property valuations.

Requirement 5: The Acquisition-Date Holding Period

Stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025 must be held for more than five years. Qualifying stock acquired after September 27, 2010 and on or before July 4, 2025 can receive a 100% exclusion. For stock acquired after July 4, 2025, the exclusion is 50% after at least three years, 75% after at least four years, and 100% after at least five years. These percentages apply to eligible gain after the per-issuer limit, not automatically to all sale proceeds. Acquisition dates reflect applicable holding-period tacking rules.

Section 1045 may defer gain when a noncorporate taxpayer sells QSBS held for more than six months, elects rollover treatment, and buys replacement QSBS during the 60-day period beginning on the sale date. Full deferral generally requires reinvesting the amount realized, not just the gain. Deferred gain reduces replacement-stock basis; applicable holding-period tacking must be analyzed. A rollover does not automatically secure a later Section 1202 exclusion or increase its cap.

The Exclusion Amount: Dollar Limit or 10x Basis

The eligible-gain ceiling is the greater of the remaining per-issuer dollar limit or 10 times the adjusted basis of that issuer's QSBS sold during the tax year, ignoring additions to basis after original issuance. The dollar limit is $10 million for stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025, or $15 million for stock acquired after July 4, 2025 (the 2026 amount, with inflation adjustments beginning after 2026). Prior eligible gain from the same issuer reduces the dollar limit; mixed old and new lots require coordination, not two independent allowances. Apply the applicable exclusion percentage to the eligible gain after this limit.

A shareholder with $2 million of qualifying basis in original-issue stock has a $20 million basis-based eligible-gain ceiling, compared with the applicable remaining dollar limit. This does not automatically exclude $20 million: actual eligible gain, holding period and exclusion percentage still matter. Contributed property must be reviewed under Section 1202(i), and later additions to basis are ignored for the 10-times test.

The limit applies per taxpayer, per issuer. A married couple filing jointly does not get separate $10 million or $15 million dollar limits merely because each spouse owns shares. Married filing separately has special half-dollar-limit rules. Separate qualifying issuers are analyzed separately, and prior eligible gain reduces the available dollar ceiling.

State Tax Treatment: The Critical Variable

While the federal QSBS exclusion is clear and well-established, state tax treatment varies dramatically. This is one of the most important planning considerations for business owners who live or operate in states that do not conform to Section 1202.

State treatment must be checked for the sale year, residency, sourcing and any trust. California does not conform to the federal Section 1202 exclusion. A federal exclusion therefore does not establish a zero state-tax result, and the 2025 federal expansion should not be assumed to apply in every state. A change of residence or trust location is not an automatic cure for state tax.

California does not conform. California Revenue and Taxation Code Section 18152.5 explicitly decouples from IRC Section 1202. California taxes the full capital gain at up to 13.3%, even if the gain is excluded from federal income. For a California resident selling $10 million in QSBS gain, the state tax bill could exceed $1.3 million despite owing zero federal tax. This makes California residency one of the most significant tax planning issues for QSBS holders.

State conformity to the original exclusion and to the 2025 expansion are separate questions. Check current state guidance rather than relying on a nationwide conforming-state list.

For business owners in non-conforming states, establishing residency in a conforming state before the sale is a common planning strategy. The relocation must be genuine and well-documented to withstand a state tax audit. Our team works with clients on residency planning to ensure that the state tax benefits of QSBS are fully captured.

Advanced QSBS Planning Strategies

Gifting Stock to Family Members to Multiply the Exclusion

A qualifying gift or transfer at death can preserve the original issuance treatment and holding period under Section 1202(h); it does not reset old stock into the post-July 4, 2025 regime. Separate noncorporate taxpayers may have separate per-issuer limits, but a married couple filing jointly does not get two dollar limits merely by holding shares separately. Grantor trusts do not create additional income-tax taxpayers. Non-grantor trusts require review of ownership, trust aggregation, completed gifts, valuation, gift-tax reporting and assignment-of-income risk. There is no universal safe number of trusts or waiting period.

A valid gift can carry the donor's holding period and original-issue status. Even where the holding period is already satisfied, a gift immediately before a sale may face assignment-of-income or other challenges; holding-period tacking alone does not establish a valid separate exclusion.

For a conditional older-stock example, assume one joint-return couple and four adult children are valid separate taxpayers, each with sufficient qualifying gain and unused dollar limits. The couple has one $10 million limit, not two; with four children the combined dollar ceilings could total $50 million. Gift-tax reporting, ownership, valuation, actual gain and anti-abuse rules must all be checked. Do not treat this as an automatic family entitlement.

Contributing QSBS to Grantor Trusts

QSBS can be contributed to a grantor trust without losing its QSBS character, provided the trust is treated as owned by the grantor for income tax purposes under IRC Sections 671 through 679. When the trust sells the stock, the gain is reported on the grantor's personal return, and the Section 1202 exclusion applies.

This strategy is particularly useful for estate planning purposes. A founder can transfer QSBS to an irrevocable grantor trust, removing the stock from their taxable estate while preserving the QSBS exclusion for the eventual sale. The trust can be structured as a Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT), an Intentionally Defective Grantor Trust (IDGT), or another grantor trust variant depending on the family's estate planning objectives.

Combining QSBS with Qualified Opportunity Zone (QOZ) Investments

For a historical-cohort example, assume $15 million of eligible stock gain and an available $10 million Section 1202 exclusion. The $5 million taxable excess could require separate planning. A qualifying pre-2027 Qualified Opportunity Fund deferral generally ends no later than December 31, 2026, so a 2026 investment does not provide a fresh multiyear deferral. Post-2026 investments have a different statutory regime and need their own analysis.

A qualifying long-term Opportunity Fund investment may receive separate treatment for its own appreciation, but that does not convert the original $5 million of taxable QSBS excess into permanently excluded gain. Investment suitability, deadlines and future rules must be reviewed independently.

Which Industries Qualify for QSBS

Section 1202(e)(3) lists specific industries that are excluded from QSBS eligibility. The excluded industries are:

Named services in health, law, engineering, architecture, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services and brokerage are excluded. Section 1202(e)(3)(A) separately excludes businesses whose principal asset is employee reputation or skill; the named fields do not need to meet that extra test.

Banking, insurance, financing, leasing, and investing are excluded under Section 1202(e)(3)(B).

Farming (including the raising or harvesting of trees) is excluded under Section 1202(e)(3)(C).

Mining, oil, gas, and other extractive industries are excluded under Section 1202(e)(3)(D).

Hotels, motels, and restaurants are excluded under Section 1202(e)(3)(E).

Manufacturing, software and other product businesses may qualify, but actual activities and asset use control. Leasing and nonbusiness real estate raise specific exclusions and asset tests; a construction, development or digital marketing label is not a safe harbor. Evaluate the business facts before selecting an entity structure.

Planning for Business Exit with QSBS

QSBS planning is most useful before stock issuance, financing rounds, conversions and sale negotiations. The acquisition-date holding period, issuance-date gross-assets ceiling, active-business test, redemptions and sale structure all require documented review.

Business owners should take the following steps to protect QSBS eligibility:

Document everything from day one. Maintain records of the stock issuance, the consideration paid, the corporation's gross assets at the time of issuance, and the nature of the corporation's business activities. These records will be needed to support the exclusion years later.

Monitor the 80% active business test annually. Ensure that the corporation's asset composition satisfies the test at all times. If retained earnings are accumulating in passive investments, consider deploying them into active business assets or paying them out as deductible compensation or retirement plan contributions.

Never convert to S corporation status. Once the S election is made, QSBS eligibility is almost certainly lost. If the corporation's current income needs make the C corporation structure burdensome, explore alternative extraction strategies (increased salary, retirement plan funding, fringe benefits) rather than converting to an S corporation.

Plan gifts and trust transfers before a sale is imminent. Gifts of QSBS made in anticipation of a prearranged sale may be challenged by the IRS under the step transaction doctrine or the assignment of income doctrine. Transfers should be made well in advance of any sale negotiations and should be supported by independent appraisals.

Coordinate with state tax planning. If the shareholder resides in a non-conforming state such as California, begin the residency change process early enough to establish a genuine domicile in a conforming state before the sale closes.

The QSBS Advantage in the C Corporation Framework

A qualifying stock sale may exclude shareholder-level gain within the applicable limits. It does not erase the corporation's tax on operating income, dividends paid during ownership or a corporate asset sale. Compare total taxes over the holding period and the transaction structure, not only the potential exclusion at exit.

For an illustration involving stock acquired in 2019 and sold after seven years, $10 million of eligible gain could be fully excluded if all requirements are met and the dollar limit is unused. Corporate income taxes and any taxable distributions during ownership remain separate. The outcome depends on the facts and is not guaranteed by selecting C corporation status.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Section 1202 qualified small business stock?

Section 1202 can exclude eligible gain on qualifying domestic C corporation stock. Stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025 requires more than five years; qualifying acquisitions after September 27, 2010 can receive 100% exclusion. Stock acquired after July 4, 2025 has 50%, 75% and 100% tiers at three, four and five years. The eligible-gain ceiling is the greater of the remaining $10 million or $15 million acquisition-date-based dollar limit (2026 amounts) or 10 times qualifying basis. Original issuance, active-business eligibility, stock-lot history and state tax must also be checked.

Should real estate be held in a C-Corp?

Almost never. Appreciated property distributed out of a C corporation triggers gain at both the corporate and shareholder level, with no equivalent of the partnership rules permitting tax-free property distributions.

Can I use a management company to shift income to a C-Corp?

Yes, if the arrangement has substance. The corporation must actually provide services, the fee must be arm's length, and there must be a written agreement with supporting records. Section 482 permits the IRS to reallocate income between commonly controlled entities where pricing does not reflect economic reality.

What happens if I convert a C-Corp back to an S-Corp?

The built-in gains tax of Section 1374 applies to gains that existed at conversion and are recognized within the following five-year period. This makes the C election harder to unwind than to make, which is why it should be modeled over a full holding period.

Before You Rely on a QSBS Exclusion

Bring formation documents and tax elections, the cap table and each issuance agreement, option or convertible-instrument records, consideration and stock basis, historical balance sheets and contributed-property valuations, active-business records, redemption history, prior Section 1202 claims, trust or gift documents, state residency details, and the proposed sale timeline. Ask for a written lot-by-lot eligibility analysis and a stock-sale versus asset-sale comparison before relying on an exclusion.

A rental or leasing business does not become eligible merely by converting to a C corporation; Section 1202(e) also limits nonbusiness real estate. A useful first engagement should identify which share lots may qualify, missing evidence, the remaining issuer limit, state differences and the proposed transaction structure. Confirm the written scope, responsible tax and legal advisers, timing and fees before proceeding. Request a QSBS eligibility review.
