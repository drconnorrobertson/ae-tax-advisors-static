Physician Tax Planning: W-2, Locums and Practice Ownership

By AE Tax Advisors · Published 2025-02-23

Updated November 8, 2026.

Physician tax planning starts with how income is earned. A W-2 hospital employee, independent locum tenens contractor and practice owner do not have the same deductions, payroll obligations or retirement-plan options. Build the plan from contracts and tax records before choosing entities or investments.

Identify each income stream

Separate employee wages, genuine independent-contractor income, practice distributions, investment income and rentals. Receiving a Form 1099 does not by itself establish proper worker classification. Professional licensing and state ownership restrictions also matter when forming a practice entity.

For employee work, review employer benefit elections and reimbursement policies. Paying an employment-related expense personally does not automatically create a federal deduction. A side business must have its own business purpose and records; it cannot simply absorb personal expenses or hospital wages.

Model a practice or locums S election carefully

An S corporation can change employment-tax treatment, but the owner must receive reasonable compensation for services. There is no universal physician salary or revenue percentage that creates a safe harbor. Specialty, duties, hours, geography, employees and the source of practice receipts affect the analysis.

Compare payroll taxes using the Social Security wage base, Medicare rules, other employment and employer costs. Moving $100,000 from wages to distributions does not automatically save $15,300. Include payroll administration, state taxes, retirement funding and the QBI result.

Coordinate retirement plans across jobs

Employee elective deferral limits generally apply across the individual's relevant employer plans; a new solo 401(k) does not create an unrestricted second deferral limit. Employer contributions, controlled-group rules, plan type and compensation require separate analysis.

For 2026, the general employee 401(k) deferral limit is $24,500, with eligible catch-up contributions governed by age and other rules. The general annual-additions limit is $72,000 before catch-up. These are ceilings, not amounts every physician can contribute.

A cash balance or other defined-benefit plan requires actuarial design, employee coverage and ongoing funding review. A Roth conversion is a separate tax decision; traditional IRA balances can affect the taxable portion. Do not count a nondeductible Roth contribution as an immediate income-tax deduction.

Review locums travel by tax home and assignment

Travel must satisfy the applicable away-from-tax-home and business-purpose rules. Temporary and indefinite assignments can have different results. Licensing in another state or renting a second apartment does not alone establish deductible travel.

Keep contracts, expected assignment duration, work locations, receipts and reimbursements. Meals, lodging, commuting, professional education and licensing costs each require their own classification. Avoid assuming a fixed expense percentage for every locums engagement.

Test real estate losses before relying on a wage offset

Real estate professional status generally requires more than 750 hours in qualifying real property trades or businesses and more than half of the taxpayer's personal services in those businesses. Material participation in the relevant rental activity is another requirement. The threshold is not 100 hours.

For a joint return, one spouse must independently meet the professional-status thresholds. A full-time clinical schedule can make the more-than-half test difficult. Short-term rental rules offer a different activity-classification analysis, but an Airbnb listing does not automatically make a loss nonpassive.

Review average customer use, material participation, personal use, basis, at-risk and excess-business-loss rules. A study may accelerate depreciation without producing a current deduction against clinical wages. Start with the STR planning guide and actual household facts.

Separate residence from source-state obligations

Relocation changes more than an address. States apply their own domicile, residence, sourcing, withholding and credit rules. Moving to a state without an individual income tax does not automatically eliminate tax on services or income sourced elsewhere.

Model the actual income and each state's rules. Multiplying all income by a state's top marginal rate generally overstates the benefit and ignores credits, filing status and other tax items.

Bring a complete planning file

  • Employment and locums contracts, W-2s and business-income records.
  • Practice ownership, payroll and reasonable-compensation evidence.
  • All retirement plans and contributions, including outside employers.
  • Assignment dates, work locations and reimbursement policies.
  • Rental records, participation evidence and suspended losses.

Request a physician tax review to compare the complete household projection. Focus on supportable decisions and implementation dates, not a promised annual savings range.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a solo 401(k) double my employee deferral limit?

Not generally. Coordinate the individual deferral limit across relevant plans and review employer contributions separately.

Does an STR loss automatically offset my physician wages?

No. Activity classification, material participation and other loss limits must be satisfied.

Can I save my old state’s top rate by moving?

Not automatically. Residence, income sourcing, credits and the full return determine the result.
