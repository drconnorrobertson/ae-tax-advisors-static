Stacking Retirement Plans for Maximum Deduction

Reviewed November 6, 2026. Tax-year 2026 limits are identified separately from examples.

A 401(k) and cash balance plan can build larger retirement savings, but contribution limits, deductible amounts, staff costs, and funding deadlines must all fit.

Request Your Free Tax Assessment

By AE Tax Advisors Team · Billings, Montana · Updated 2026-11-06

Stacking retirement plans means operating a defined contribution plan, usually a 401(k), alongside a defined benefit or cash balance plan. Their benefit and contribution limits differ, but that does not make the maximum amounts automatically deductible together. An actuarial study and tax projection must establish a sustainable owner benefit, required employee funding, and the allowed deduction.

Why Two Plans Beat One

For 2026, the elective deferral limit is $24,500 and the defined contribution annual additions limit is $72,000, excluding permitted catch-up contributions. The ordinary age-50-or-older catch-up is $8,000; participants attaining ages 60 through 63 may use $11,250 instead, if permitted. Catch-up contributions for participants whose prior-year wages from the sponsoring employer exceeded $150,000 generally must be Roth in 2026. That wage test is not household income or net self-employment earnings, and Roth contributions are not current deductions.

A defined benefit plan instead funds a promised retirement benefit. The 2026 Section 415(b) annual-benefit dollar limit is $290,000, subject to compensation, age, participation, and payment-form adjustments. That is not an annual contribution allowance. An enrolled actuary calculates minimum required funding and the maximum deductible contribution using the plan formula, assets, compensation history, and applicable assumptions.

Older owners may have fewer years to fund a benefit, which can support larger annual contributions. Age alone does not establish a permitted deduction.

What the Numbers Look Like

Illustrative 2026 arithmetic only: assume a 55-year-old sole proprietor has $300,000 of plan-eligible compensation after the required self-employment adjustments, no eligible employees across related businesses, no other deferrals, and no prior-year wages from this sponsor triggering Roth catch-up. Assume an actuary separately validates a $200,000 cash balance contribution as both required or permitted funding and deductible, and verifies the combined design.

This is not a client result or a funding quote. It deliberately does not assume the full $72,000 defined contribution limit can be stacked automatically. Actual self-employed compensation and deduction calculations are interdependent and require the Publication 560 worksheets and actuarial review. Current tax savings also depend on QBI, tax brackets, Roth treatment and state law.

The Rules Governing the Combination

The 6% figure is not a universal contribution or deduction cap. Under Section 404(a)(7), employer defined contribution amounts up to 6% of covered compensation are excluded from the combined deduction calculation; when employer contributions exceed 6%, the excess enters the applicable combined test. Employee elective deferrals are excluded. A PBGC-covered defined benefit plan is excluded from this combined limit, but separate plan limits still apply. Have the actuary and tax preparer calculate both the minimum funding and maximum deductible contribution.

Coverage and nondiscrimination. Review controlled groups and affiliated service groups under Section 414, employee service, compensation and ownership before treating a business as owner-only. Where aggregation is permitted for testing, both plans still must satisfy the applicable coverage, minimum participation and nondiscrimination rules.

Funding and permanence. A defined benefit plan is intended as an ongoing retirement program. A weak year does not eliminate minimum funding, and an early termination needs a legitimate facts-and-circumstances review. There is no three-year or five-year legal safe harbor.

The Employee Cost

With employees, model total employer cash outlay, the benefits allocated to owners and staff, and administration costs. Cross-tested designs can be useful when owners are older than staff, but no standard owner allocation percentage is guaranteed.

For arithmetic only, $200,000 allocated to owners and $50,000 to staff means owners receive 80% of $250,000 total contributions. That percentage describes assumed inputs, not proof that a census will pass testing or that the contributions are deductible. Two businesses with equal profit can have very different results.

Deadlines That Cannot Be Missed

Most qualified employer plans may be adopted by the employer tax-return due date, including extensions, and treated as adopted for the prior tax year. Adoption, employee deferral elections, deposit deadlines, and employer contribution deductibility are separate tests. Salary deferrals generally require a timely election before compensation becomes available; adopting a plan later does not authorize retroactive S-corporation payroll deferrals. A narrow first-year exception permits an individual who owns the entire unincorporated business and is its only employee to adopt a new 401(k) and make first-year elective deferrals by the unextended individual return deadline.

Defined benefit minimum funding is generally due 8.5 months after the plan year ends, normally September 15 for a calendar-year plan. Quarterly installments may apply. An October 15 extended individual tax-return deadline does not postpone that funding deadline. Contributions paid by the applicable extended employer return deadline may be deductible for the prior tax year when properly designated and within Section 404 limits; the funding and deduction calculations must both be satisfied.

Starting early leaves time for actuarial design, participant notices, elections, investment arrangements and cash planning. A December or post-year-end inquiry is not automatically too late, but the administrator must verify every deadline before promising a prior-year deduction.

Whether the Deferral Is Worth It

Pre-tax contributions generally defer rather than eliminate income tax. Compare current deductions, any reduction in the QBI deduction, later ordinary-income distributions, investment risk, required staff benefits and ongoing administration. Roth contributions do not produce current deductions.

A freeze may stop future accruals prospectively but does not erase already earned benefits, existing funding shortfalls, annual actuarial work or reporting. A termination can require additional funding, participant notices and PBGC procedures where covered. Size the plan for a poor year and review a business sale before committing capital.

Key Takeaways

  • Separate plan limits do not guarantee that both maximum contributions are deductible together.
  • The $290,000 limit in 2026 is a defined benefit annual-benefit ceiling, not a contribution limit.
  • Employee costs require a complete census, related-employer review and actuarial testing.
  • Adoption, employee deferral elections, minimum funding and tax-deduction deadlines are different.
  • The 6% rule is an exclusion within the combined deduction calculation, not a universal cap.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can a business owner deduct with stacked retirement plans?

The amount depends on eligible compensation, age, benefit formula, plan assets, employee census, other plans and the Section 404 deduction calculations. The $250,500 illustration on this page assumes a separately validated $200,000 cash balance contribution; it is not a quoted entitlement or client result.

Can I have a 401(k) and a cash balance plan at the same time?

The 6% figure is not a universal contribution or deduction cap. Under Section 404(a)(7), employer defined contribution amounts up to 6% of covered compensation are excluded from the combined deduction calculation; when employer contributions exceed 6%, the excess enters the applicable combined test. Employee elective deferrals are excluded. A PBGC-covered defined benefit plan is excluded from this combined limit, but separate plan limits still apply. Have the actuary and tax preparer calculate both the minimum funding and maximum deductible contribution.

What happens if I have a bad year and cannot fund the plan?

Minimum required funding can remain due despite a weak year. Prospective amendments or a freeze do not erase earned benefits or funding shortfalls. Ask the actuary about funding, notice and termination options before the deadline; underfunding can cause excise taxes.

How much do these plans cost to administer?

A cash balance plan needs ongoing actuarial work, a written plan and applicable annual returns, typically several thousand dollars a year. These recurring costs, employee benefits and any PBGC premiums should be modeled against the projected deduction. Owner-only Form 5500-EZ filing exceptions do not remove the actuarial funding obligation.
