QSBS Exclusion Section 1202: How Business Owners Save Millions in Capital Gains Tax

By AE Tax Advisors Team | Published October 3, 2026

What Is Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS)?

Section 1202 can exclude part or all of eligible gain when a noncorporate taxpayer sells qualifying domestic C corporation stock. Start with the stock's acquisition and issuance dates: the 2025 law changed the holding periods, per-issuer dollar limit and gross-assets ceiling for newer stock. Entity status, original issuance, business activity, redemptions and records still determine whether the stock qualifies.

The original 50% regime dates to 1993. The 75% exclusion applies to stock acquired after February 17, 2009 and on or before September 27, 2010; the 100% regime applies to qualifying stock acquired after September 27, 2010 and on or before July 4, 2025, held more than five years. The 2025 law added the newer-stock tiers below. Earlier acquisitions can have different AMT treatment.

Section 1202 rewards qualifying long-term investment, but a large projected exclusion is not a reason by itself to choose C corporation status. Compare annual corporate and dividend taxes, financing plans, likely buyer structure and the evidence supporting eligibility.

Section 1202 Requirements: The Five Tests Your Stock Must Pass

Not every share of stock in a small company qualifies for the Section 1202 exclusion. The IRS imposes five specific requirements that must all be satisfied. Failing even one test disqualifies the stock entirely. Understanding each requirement in detail is essential for founders and investors who want to preserve QSBS eligibility from the moment of incorporation through the eventual sale.

1. The Corporation Must Be a Domestic C Corporation

The issuing entity must be organized as a C corporation under U.S. law. S corporations, LLCs, partnerships, and sole proprietorships do not qualify, regardless of their size or the nature of their business. This requirement catches many founders off guard. Startups commonly organize as LLCs for pass-through tax treatment, and many small businesses elect S corporation status to avoid double taxation. However, these structures are incompatible with QSBS treatment. If you anticipate a large exit and want to preserve the Section 1202 exclusion, the business must be (or must convert to) a C corporation before the stock is issued.

Note that a corporation that was previously an S corporation can qualify, but only stock issued after the conversion to C corporation status is eligible. Stock issued while the entity was an S corp does not retroactively become QSBS upon conversion.

2. The Gross Assets Test by Issuance Date

For stock issued on or before July 4, 2025, aggregate gross assets must not exceed $50 million; for stock issued after July 4, 2025, the ceiling is $75 million in 2026. The test covers the corporation and any predecessor at all times after August 9, 1993 and before issuance, and immediately after issuance including the new proceeds. Parent-subsidiary controlled-group rules also apply. Gross assets generally mean cash plus adjusted tax basis of other property, with contributed property measured using fair market value at contribution. Enterprise valuation is not the same measure.

Later growth above the applicable ceiling does not by itself disqualify previously eligible shares. A later issuance must be tested separately, including all relevant prior asset history. A company cannot simply shrink its balance sheet before an issuance and disregard an earlier breach.

3. Original Issuance Requirement

The taxpayer must acquire the stock at original issuance, directly from the corporation, in exchange for money, property (other than stock), or services. Stock purchased on the secondary market from another shareholder does not qualify. This requirement ensures that the benefit flows to those who provide capital or services directly to the business, not to traders who buy shares after the fact.

Stock received through the exercise of options or warrants can qualify, provided the options or warrants were themselves granted by the corporation. Restricted stock awards and stock received upon conversion of convertible notes or SAFEs can also qualify, though the analysis requires careful attention to the terms of the instrument and the timing of issuance. The IRS has not issued comprehensive guidance on every convertible instrument structure, so working with a qualified tax advisor is strongly recommended.

4. Active Business Requirement (80% Test)

During substantially all of the taxpayer's holding period, at least 80% of the corporation's assets (by value) must be used in the active conduct of one or more qualified trades or businesses. A company that holds large amounts of passive investments, real estate held for investment, or cash equivalents beyond reasonable working capital needs may fail this test.

At least 80% of assets by value must be used in one or more qualified active trades or businesses during substantially all of the holding period. This is an asset test, not an 80% gross-income or revenue test. Reasonably required working capital and qualifying investment funds expected to be used within two years for research or increased working capital can count, but after the corporation has existed for at least two years no more than 50% of its assets may qualify solely under the working-capital rule. Separate limits apply to portfolio securities and nonbusiness real estate.

5. Holding Period by Acquisition Date

Stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025 must be held for more than five years. Qualifying stock acquired after September 27, 2010 and on or before July 4, 2025 can receive a 100% exclusion. For stock acquired after July 4, 2025, the exclusion is 50% after at least three years, 75% after at least four years, and 100% after at least five years. These percentages apply to eligible gain after the per-issuer limit, not automatically to all sale proceeds. Acquisition dates reflect applicable holding-period tacking rules.

Section 1045 may defer gain when a noncorporate taxpayer sells QSBS held for more than six months, elects rollover treatment, and buys replacement QSBS during the 60-day period beginning on the sale date. Full deferral generally requires reinvesting the amount realized, not just the gain. Deferred gain reduces replacement-stock basis; applicable holding-period tacking must be analyzed. A rollover does not automatically secure a later Section 1202 exclusion or increase its cap.

The Exclusion Amount: How Much Gain Can You Exclude?

The eligible-gain ceiling is the greater of the remaining per-issuer dollar limit or 10 times the adjusted basis of that issuer's QSBS sold during the tax year, ignoring additions to basis after original issuance. The dollar limit is $10 million for stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025, or $15 million for stock acquired after July 4, 2025 (the 2026 amount, with inflation adjustments beginning after 2026). Prior eligible gain from the same issuer reduces the dollar limit; mixed old and new lots require coordination, not two independent allowances. Apply the applicable exclusion percentage to the eligible gain after this limit.

  • The remaining $10 million or $15 million dollar limit, determined by acquisition date and prior eligible gain, or
  • 10 times the adjusted basis of the stock disposed of during the taxable year.

Apply the limit separately to each taxpayer and issuing corporation, with prior eligible gain reducing the dollar allowance. A married couple filing jointly does not double that allowance merely by owning shares separately. Gifts and trusts require the additional review described below.

The 10x-basis alternative becomes more valuable when basis is higher. In a pre-July 5, 2025 acquisition eligible for the 100% exclusion with no prior issuer use, $100,000 of basis produces a $1 million basis ceiling, so the $10 million dollar ceiling is larger. With $5 million of qualifying basis and $25 million of eligible gain, the $50 million basis ceiling can permit exclusion of the full $25 million, assuming all other requirements are met.

Eligible Businesses vs. Excluded Businesses

Section 1202 excludes the activities below. The general requirement is at least 80% qualifying active assets by value during substantially all of the holding period, not a blanket rule that any excluded activity above 10% disqualifies the stock. Separate 10% rules address certain portfolio securities and nonbusiness real estate.

  • Professional services: Any trade or business involving the performance of services in the fields of health, law, engineering, architecture, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, or brokerage services.
  • Banking, insurance, financing, leasing, and investing: Financial institutions and companies whose primary activity is managing money or financial instruments.
  • Hotels, motels, restaurants and similar businesses: The Section 1202 exclusion is not determined by the seven-day or 30-day rental tests used for passive-activity analysis.
  • Restaurants: Food and beverage service establishments.
  • Farming: Businesses engaged in the trade or business of farming, including raising or harvesting trees.
  • Mining and natural resource extraction: Oil, gas, and mineral extraction businesses.

The exclusion of professional services is the most frequently encountered limitation. It disqualifies a wide range of high-earning business owners, including doctors, lawyers, consultants, accountants, architects, and financial advisors. If your primary business activity falls into one of these categories, Section 1202 is generally not available to you.

A mixed product-and-services business requires a facts-based analysis of its activities and assets. Proprietary technology does not override a named consulting or other service exclusion. Document the actual qualified business and the active-asset test instead of relying on labels or billable-hours ratios.

Planning Strategies: Multiplying the QSBS Exclusion

Completed gifts can place stock with separate noncorporate taxpayers, but they do not automatically multiply a family exemption. Ownership, prior issuer gain, holding periods and anti-abuse rules determine each recipient's result.

Gifting QSBS to Family Members

A founder may give qualifying shares to adult family members before a sale. For example, pre-July 5, 2025 stock eligible for the 100% exclusion could support a $10 million remaining dollar limit for the founder and each of four adult children, subject to each holder's actual eligible gain and all transfer rules. This is a conditional illustration, not an automatic $50 million family allowance.

A gift must be legally completed and supported by appropriate ownership and valuation records. It may use gift and estate tax exemption and require a gift-tax return. Retained interests, binding sale arrangements and assignment-of-income rules need estate counsel and tax review before the transfer.

Using Trusts to Stack Exclusions

Only trusts treated as separate taxpayers can potentially add separate limits. A grantor trust normally uses the grantor's limit; an irrevocable label alone is insufficient. Review trust aggregation, completed gifts, valuation and assignment-of-income risk before projecting an additional exclusion.

The review should determine whether any proposed non-grantor trust has a genuine independent purpose and separate tax treatment before projecting an additional exclusion. Do not assume a trust arrangement or a stated waiting period is accepted by the IRS.

Issuing Stock to Multiple Family Members at Inception

Family members may acquire original-issue shares for qualifying consideration. Issuing valuable stock at par value does not automatically avoid a gift or compensation issue. Document fair value, who provides the consideration, actual ownership and original issuance eligibility.

Section 1045 Rollovers: Deferring Gain When You Sell Early

Section 1045 may defer gain when a noncorporate taxpayer sells QSBS held for more than six months, elects rollover treatment, and buys replacement QSBS during the 60-day period beginning on the sale date. Full deferral generally requires reinvesting the amount realized, not just the gain. Deferred gain reduces replacement-stock basis; applicable holding-period tacking must be analyzed. A rollover does not automatically secure a later Section 1202 exclusion or increase its cap.

A valid rollover can preserve holding-period continuity for qualifying replacement stock. The replacement investment must be evaluated on its own business merits, and the later exclusion remains subject to acquisition-date, basis and per-issuer rules.

The replacement stock must qualify, and the election, reinvestment window, basis adjustment and return reporting all matter. Have the sale and replacement documents reviewed before the 60-day window expires.

State Conformity Issues: Why California (and Others) Do Not Follow Section 1202

While the federal QSBS exclusion can eliminate capital gains tax at the federal level, state tax treatment varies widely. The most significant outlier is California, which does not conform to Section 1202 at all. California taxes 100% of the gain on QSBS at the state's ordinary income tax rate, which can reach 13.3% for high earners. For a founder selling a $20 million stake, the California tax bill alone could exceed $2.5 million, even if the federal tax is zero.

State treatment must be checked for the sale year, residency, sourcing and any trust. California does not conform to the federal Section 1202 exclusion. A federal exclusion therefore does not establish a zero state-tax result, and the 2025 federal expansion should not be assumed to apply in every state. A change of residence or trust location is not an automatic cure for state tax.

How to Structure a Business to Qualify for QSBS

Founders who want to preserve QSBS eligibility should address the following structural considerations from the very beginning:

  1. Incorporate as a C corporation from day one. While many advisors recommend LLCs or S corps for early-stage businesses, founders who anticipate a large exit should seriously consider C corporation status to preserve the Section 1202 exclusion. The potential tax savings on a multimillion-dollar exit can dwarf the incremental tax cost of C corporation double taxation during the company's early, low-revenue years.
  2. Test gross assets at each issuance. Apply the $50 million ceiling to stock issued on or before July 4, 2025 and the $75 million ceiling to later stock in 2026. Include predecessor history, new proceeds and contributed-property valuations.
  3. Ensure the business is a qualified trade or business. Avoid activities that fall into the excluded categories. If the company is on the borderline (for example, a technology company that also provides significant consulting services), document the proportion of revenue and assets attributable to product versus services activities.
  4. Maintain the 80% active business test. Avoid accumulating large cash balances or passive investments without a documented plan for their use. If the company raises a large funding round, prepare a written working capital plan explaining how the proceeds will be deployed over the next 24 months.
  5. Issue stock at original issuance. Ensure that founders, employees, and investors receive stock directly from the corporation, not from existing shareholders. Structure option plans, SAFEs, and convertible notes so that the stock received upon exercise or conversion is treated as issued at original issuance.

Documentation Requirements: Proving QSBS Status to the IRS

The burden of proving QSBS qualification falls on the taxpayer. The IRS can challenge a Section 1202 exclusion claim years after the stock is sold, and reconstructing records from a decade earlier can be extremely difficult. Founders and their advisors should establish a documentation protocol from the date of incorporation and maintain it throughout the company's life.

Essential records include:

  • Articles of incorporation showing C corporation status from the date of organization.
  • Stock purchase agreements and board resolutions documenting each issuance of stock, the consideration paid, and the identity of each purchaser.
  • Balance sheets and asset schedules establishing the applicable $50 million or $75 million ceiling, prior corporate and predecessor history, amounts raised at issuance and contributed-property fair market value.
  • Annual financial statements demonstrating that at least 80% of corporate assets were used in the active conduct of a qualified trade or business throughout the holding period.
  • Working capital plans for any periods when the company held significant cash or liquid investments.
  • Tax returns showing C corporation status for every year of the holding period (no S election was in effect).
  • Evidence of the qualified trade or business (revenue breakdowns, product descriptions, customer contracts) to demonstrate that the company was not engaged in an excluded activity.

Many companies do not maintain these records systematically, and by the time a sale occurs, critical documents may be lost. The best practice is to create a dedicated QSBS compliance file at the time of incorporation, update it with each stock issuance, and review it annually with a tax advisor.

Common Mistakes That Disqualify QSBS

Even sophisticated founders and their advisors make mistakes that can inadvertently disqualify QSBS. The most common errors include:

  • Assuming a conversion fixes old stock. Revoking an S election does not turn existing S corporation shares into QSBS and does not create a deemed new stock issuance. A genuine new original issuance by an eligible domestic C corporation must be analyzed separately. Incorporating an LLC taxed as a partnership or disregarded entity can involve qualifying original-issue C corporation stock in exchange for property, if all requirements are met. The qualifying holding period generally starts at that incorporation, and Section 1202(i) prevents pre-contribution appreciation from becoming excludable gain.
  • Missing the applicable gross-assets ceiling. Both prior history and immediately-after-issuance assets matter, including the issuance proceeds and controlled-group rules.
  • Significant stock redemptions or buybacks. Section 1202(c)(3) contains anti-abuse rules that disqualify stock if the corporation redeems a significant amount of stock from the taxpayer or a related person within certain time periods around the issuance. Specifically, if the corporation purchases more than a de minimis amount of its stock from the taxpayer (or a related person) within two years before or after the issuance, the stock can be disqualified.
  • Failing the active business test. Companies that accumulate large amounts of cash or passive investments without a documented working capital plan can fail the 80% active business requirement, retroactively disqualifying all outstanding QSBS.
  • Acquiring stock on the secondary market. Employees or investors who buy shares from existing shareholders, rather than receiving them from the corporation, do not meet the original issuance requirement. This is a common issue in late-stage companies where secondary trading is prevalent.
  • Operating an excluded business. Founders sometimes underestimate how broadly the IRS interprets the "consulting" or "financial services" exclusions. A technology company that derives the majority of its revenue from custom consulting engagements may be reclassified as a services firm.

Illustrative Examples of QSBS Tax Savings

Example 1: Software Founder Excludes $10 Million in Gain

A founder incorporates a SaaS company as a C corporation in 2020, investing $50,000 for 1,000,000 shares of common stock. The company grows steadily, and in 2026, six years after issuance, the founder sells all shares for $10,050,000. The capital gain is $10,000,000. Because the stock was held for more than five years, was acquired at original issuance from a C corporation with gross assets under $50 million, and the company operated a qualified technology business, the founder claims the full $10 million Section 1202 exclusion. Federal tax on the gain: $0. At a 23.8% combined federal capital gains and net investment income tax rate, the founder saves $2,380,000 in federal taxes.

Example 2: Family Stacking Strategy Excludes $40 Million

Conditional pre-2025-law illustration: A founder has $40 million of projected eligible gain on stock acquired after September 27, 2010 and on or before July 4, 2025. Assume the founder and three adult children each ultimately hold shares generating $10 million of gain, satisfy all requirements including more than five years of holding, and have no prior issuer exclusions. Each could exclude $10 million. At an assumed 23.8% federal rate, $40 million x 23.8% equals $9,520,000. Completed gifts, valuation and assignment-of-income review are essential; this is not a guaranteed family result.

Example 3: Section 1045 Rollover Preserves the Benefit

An angel investor acquires QSBS for $200,000 in 2022 and receives $2,000,000 in a 2025 sale after three years. The pre-July 5, 2025 stock has not met its more-than-five-year holding requirement. If the investor timely elects Section 1045 and buys $2,000,000 of qualifying replacement stock within 60 days, the $1,800,000 gain can be deferred and replacement basis becomes $200,000. A 2028 sale can satisfy the combined holding period, but the eventual exclusion still requires eligibility, the old acquisition-date regime and the applicable per-issuer cap.

Example 4: California Founder Faces State Tax Despite Federal Exclusion

For a historical-cohort illustration, assume $15 million of gain on stock acquired after September 27, 2010 and on or before July 4, 2025, a satisfied more-than-five-year holding period, all other eligibility tests met and a remaining $10 million dollar limit larger than the basis ceiling. The federal exclusion is $10 million and $5 million remains taxable. California does not conform. Applying an assumed 13.3% marginal rate to the full $15 million gives $1,995,000 as a simplified state-rate illustration, not a progressive-bracket return calculation.

Interaction with Other Tax Provisions

The QSBS exclusion interacts with several other areas of the tax code that business owners should understand:

  • Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT): Gain excluded under Section 1202 is also excluded from the 3.8% net investment income tax. This means the effective federal tax savings can be as high as 23.8% (20% capital gains rate plus 3.8% NIIT).
  • Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT): For stock acquired after September 27, 2010, the excluded gain is not treated as a preference item for AMT purposes. This was a significant change; prior to this date, a portion of the excluded gain was subject to AMT.
  • Section 1244 (Small Business Stock Losses): If QSBS becomes worthless, the taxpayer may be able to claim an ordinary loss deduction (up to $50,000, or $100,000 for married filing jointly) under Section 1244, rather than a capital loss. This provides a partial offset for the downside risk of investing in small businesses.
  • Estate Planning: Stock transferred at death can preserve qualifying original-issuance treatment and the transferor's holding period under Section 1202(h). A qualifying gift can also preserve those attributes. Basis, estate and gift tax, ownership and the eventual sale must be reviewed together; Section 1202 does not make lifetime gifts universally preferable to inheritance.

Next Steps: Is Your Business Positioned for QSBS?

The Section 1202 QSBS exclusion represents one of the most significant tax planning opportunities available to business owners. A successful claim can eliminate millions of dollars in federal capital gains tax. But the requirements are strict, the documentation burden is real, and a single misstep (wrong entity type, missed asset threshold, insufficient records) can invalidate the entire exclusion.

If you are a business owner, founder, or early-stage investor who expects a significant exit in the future, the time to start planning is now. Every year that passes without proper documentation and structural planning increases the risk that the exclusion will be lost.

Does California conform to the federal QSBS exclusion?

No. California is the most notable state that does not conform to the Section 1202 exclusion. California taxes 100% of the gain on QSBS at ordinary state capital gains rates. Founders and investors in California should factor this into their planning, as a large exit could still generate a significant California tax liability.

Can I stack QSBS exclusions across multiple family members?

A qualifying gift or transfer at death can preserve the original issuance treatment and holding period under Section 1202(h); it does not reset old stock into the post-July 4, 2025 regime. Separate noncorporate taxpayers may have separate per-issuer limits, but a married couple filing jointly does not get two dollar limits merely by holding shares separately. Grantor trusts do not create additional income-tax taxpayers. Non-grantor trusts require review of ownership, trust aggregation, completed gifts, valuation, gift-tax reporting and assignment-of-income risk. There is no universal safe number of trusts or waiting period.

What types of businesses are excluded from QSBS treatment?

Several categories of businesses cannot qualify for QSBS treatment under Section 1202. These include professional service firms (health, law, engineering, accounting, consulting, financial services, athletics), banking and insurance companies, hotels and motels, restaurants, farming businesses, and companies involved in mineral extraction. The corporation must use at least 80% of its assets in the active conduct of a qualified trade or business.

What documentation do I need to support a QSBS exclusion claim?

Bring formation documents and tax elections, the cap table and each issuance agreement, option or convertible-instrument records, consideration and stock basis, historical balance sheets and contributed-property valuations, active-business records, redemption history, prior Section 1202 claims, trust or gift documents, state residency details, and the proposed sale timeline. Ask for a written lot-by-lot eligibility analysis and a stock-sale versus asset-sale comparison before relying on an exclusion.
