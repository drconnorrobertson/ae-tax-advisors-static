QSBS Exclusion Section 1202: How Business Owners Save Millions in Capital Gains Tax

By AE Tax Advisors Team | Published October 3, 2026

What Is Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS)?

Qualified Small Business Stock, commonly referred to as QSBS, is one of the most powerful and often underutilized tax benefits available to founders, early employees, and investors in growing companies. Under Internal Revenue Code Section 1202, a non-corporate taxpayer who sells QSBS can exclude up to 100% of the capital gain from federal income tax. For stock acquired after September 27, 2010, the exclusion is 100% of the gain, with no alternative minimum tax (AMT) preference item. This means that a founder who sells a qualifying business could potentially pay zero federal tax on millions of dollars in profit.

The QSBS exclusion was first introduced in 1993 as part of the Revenue Reconciliation Act, but the benefit was initially limited to a 50% exclusion. Congress increased the exclusion to 75% for stock acquired between February 18, 2009 and September 27, 2010, and then to the current 100% exclusion for stock acquired after September 27, 2010. The 100% exclusion was made permanent by the Protecting Americans from Tax Hikes (PATH) Act of 2015, giving founders and investors confidence that the benefit will remain available for future transactions.

At its core, Section 1202 is designed to encourage investment in small businesses by reducing the tax cost of taking equity risk. The rationale is straightforward: if investors and founders know they can sell their stock tax-free (at the federal level), they are more likely to start companies, invest in early-stage ventures, and hold their positions for the long term. The five-year holding period requirement reinforces this goal by rewarding patient capital.

Section 1202 Requirements: The Five Tests Your Stock Must Pass

Not every share of stock in a small company qualifies for the Section 1202 exclusion. The IRS imposes five specific requirements that must all be satisfied. Failing even one test disqualifies the stock entirely. Understanding each requirement in detail is essential for founders and investors who want to preserve QSBS eligibility from the moment of incorporation through the eventual sale.

1. The Corporation Must Be a Domestic C Corporation

The issuing entity must be organized as a C corporation under U.S. law. S corporations, LLCs, partnerships, and sole proprietorships do not qualify, regardless of their size or the nature of their business. This requirement catches many founders off guard. Startups commonly organize as LLCs for pass-through tax treatment, and many small businesses elect S corporation status to avoid double taxation. However, these structures are incompatible with QSBS treatment. If you anticipate a large exit and want to preserve the Section 1202 exclusion, the business must be (or must convert to) a C corporation before the stock is issued.

Note that a corporation that was previously an S corporation can qualify, but only stock issued after the conversion to C corporation status is eligible. Stock issued while the entity was an S corp does not retroactively become QSBS upon conversion.

2. The $50 Million Gross Assets Test

At the time the stock is issued (and immediately after), the corporation's aggregate gross assets must not exceed $50 million. Gross assets are measured by the adjusted basis of all assets held by the corporation, not by fair market value. This is an important distinction: a company with assets that have appreciated significantly in value can still qualify if the tax basis of those assets remains under $50 million.

The $50 million test is applied at the moment of issuance. If the company later grows beyond $50 million in gross assets, stock that was issued while the company was below the threshold remains qualified. This creates a strategic incentive to issue stock early, before the company's asset base grows past the limit. Founders and early investors benefit most, as they receive stock when the company is small. Later-round investors may find that the company has already crossed the $50 million line, making their shares ineligible for QSBS treatment.

3. Original Issuance Requirement

The taxpayer must acquire the stock at original issuance, directly from the corporation, in exchange for money, property (other than stock), or services. Stock purchased on the secondary market from another shareholder does not qualify. This requirement ensures that the benefit flows to those who provide capital or services directly to the business, not to traders who buy shares after the fact.

Stock received through the exercise of options or warrants can qualify, provided the options or warrants were themselves granted by the corporation. Restricted stock awards and stock received upon conversion of convertible notes or SAFEs can also qualify, though the analysis requires careful attention to the terms of the instrument and the timing of issuance. The IRS has not issued comprehensive guidance on every convertible instrument structure, so working with a qualified tax advisor is strongly recommended.

4. Active Business Requirement (80% Test)

During substantially all of the taxpayer's holding period, at least 80% of the corporation's assets (by value) must be used in the active conduct of one or more qualified trades or businesses. A company that holds large amounts of passive investments, real estate held for investment, or cash equivalents beyond reasonable working capital needs may fail this test.

The IRS provides a safe harbor for working capital: cash and investments held as reasonable working capital for the active business are counted as active business assets, provided they are expected to be used within two years and there is a plan for their use. Startups that raise large funding rounds should document a written plan for how the proceeds will be deployed, to ensure that temporary excess cash does not jeopardize the 80% test.

5. Five-Year Holding Period

The taxpayer must hold the stock for at least five years from the date of original issuance. The holding period begins on the date the stock is acquired, not on the date the company was incorporated. For stock received through the exercise of options, the holding period generally begins on the exercise date, not the grant date. For stock received upon conversion of convertible notes, the holding period typically begins on the conversion date.

If a taxpayer sells QSBS before the five-year mark, the gain is not eligible for the Section 1202 exclusion. However, Section 1045 provides an alternative: the taxpayer can defer the gain by rolling the proceeds into replacement QSBS within 60 days of the sale. This rollover provision can bridge the gap when a founder needs to sell early but wants to preserve the eventual tax benefit.

The Exclusion Amount: How Much Gain Can You Exclude?

For QSBS acquired after September 27, 2010, the exclusion is 100% of the eligible gain. The maximum amount of gain that any single taxpayer can exclude with respect to stock in any single corporation is the greater of:

  • $10 million (reduced by any gain previously excluded on stock in the same corporation), or
  • 10 times the adjusted basis of the stock disposed of during the taxable year.

The per-taxpayer, per-issuer nature of this limit is critical. Each taxpayer gets their own $10 million (or 10x basis) limit for each qualifying corporation. A married couple filing jointly shares a single $10 million limit, but each spouse can hold stock in the same corporation and claim separate limits if the stock is held individually. More importantly, different taxpayers (family members, trusts, entities) each receive their own independent exclusion, which opens the door to powerful planning strategies discussed below.

The 10x basis alternative is especially valuable when a founder acquires stock for a low price. For example, if a founder pays $100,000 for stock and the company is later sold for $30 million, the 10x basis amount would be $1 million, which is less than the $10 million cap. In this case, the $10 million cap controls, and the founder can exclude the full $10 million of gain. But if another investor pays $5 million for stock and the gain is $25 million, the 10x basis amount would be $50 million, which exceeds the $10 million cap, allowing the investor to exclude the full $25 million gain under the 10x basis rule.

Eligible Businesses vs. Excluded Businesses

Section 1202 specifically excludes several categories of businesses from QSBS treatment. If more than 10% of the corporation's assets are used in an excluded activity, the stock will not qualify. The excluded categories are:

  • Professional services: Any trade or business involving the performance of services in the fields of health, law, engineering, architecture, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, or brokerage services.
  • Banking, insurance, financing, leasing, and investing: Financial institutions and companies whose primary activity is managing money or financial instruments.
  • Hotels and motels: Lodging businesses where the average period of customer use is seven days or fewer (or 30 days or fewer with significant personal services).
  • Restaurants: Food and beverage service establishments.
  • Farming: Businesses engaged in the trade or business of farming, including raising or harvesting trees.
  • Mining and natural resource extraction: Oil, gas, and mineral extraction businesses.

The exclusion of professional services is the most frequently encountered limitation. It disqualifies a wide range of high-earning business owners, including doctors, lawyers, consultants, accountants, architects, and financial advisors. If your primary business activity falls into one of these categories, Section 1202 is generally not available to you.

However, the line between a "consulting" firm and a "technology" company is not always clear. A company that develops proprietary software and licenses it to customers is generally engaged in a qualified trade or business, even if it also provides implementation services. The key question is whether the company's value is derived primarily from its products and intellectual property or from the personal services of its employees. Companies with significant proprietary technology, recurring product revenue, and scalable business models are more likely to qualify than firms that sell billable hours.

Planning Strategies: Multiplying the QSBS Exclusion

Because the Section 1202 exclusion is calculated on a per-taxpayer, per-issuer basis, one of the most powerful planning strategies involves spreading QSBS ownership across multiple taxpayers before a sale. Each holder gets their own independent exclusion limit. With careful planning, a family can exclude far more than the $10 million cap that would apply to a single shareholder.

Gifting QSBS to Family Members

A founder who holds QSBS can gift shares to family members before a sale. The recipient inherits the donor's basis and holding period, so the five-year test is measured from the original issuance date, not the date of the gift. Each family member who receives gifted shares gets their own $10 million (or 10x basis) exclusion. A founder with four adult children could potentially exclude up to $50 million in gain ($10 million for the founder plus $10 million for each child), assuming each person holds the stock individually.

The gift must be a completed transfer, and the donor must relinquish all control over the shares. Gifts of QSBS count against the donor's lifetime gift and estate tax exemption (currently $13.61 million per person for 2026), but the tax savings from multiplying the QSBS exclusion can far exceed the cost of using exemption.

Using Trusts to Stack Exclusions

Trusts are treated as separate taxpayers for purposes of the Section 1202 exclusion. A founder can create multiple irrevocable trusts, each funded with QSBS, and each trust receives its own $10 million exclusion. Non-grantor trusts are the most effective vehicle for this strategy, because they are treated as completely separate taxpayers. Grantor trusts, by contrast, are disregarded for income tax purposes, meaning that gain recognized by a grantor trust is taxed to the grantor individually and does not generate a separate exclusion.

A well-designed trust strategy might involve creating separate irrevocable, non-grantor trusts for each child or grandchild, funding each trust with a block of QSBS shares, and then waiting for the eventual sale. Each trust claims its own exclusion, dramatically increasing the total gain that can be sheltered from federal tax.

Issuing Stock to Multiple Family Members at Inception

Instead of gifting shares later, some founders issue stock directly to family members at the time the corporation is formed. Because the stock is issued at a very low price (often par value), each family member acquires their shares at original issuance, satisfying the original issuance requirement independently. This approach avoids gift tax concerns entirely and gives each family member their own basis for purposes of the 10x basis alternative.

Section 1045 Rollovers: Deferring Gain When You Sell Early

Section 1045 provides a deferral mechanism for taxpayers who sell QSBS before completing the five-year holding period. If you sell QSBS that you have held for more than six months and reinvest the proceeds in replacement QSBS within 60 days, you can elect to defer (roll over) the gain into the replacement stock. The replacement stock inherits the holding period of the original stock, so the clock keeps ticking toward the five-year mark.

This provision is particularly useful for serial entrepreneurs and angel investors who cycle through multiple investments. By rolling gains from one QSBS investment into another, a taxpayer can continuously defer recognition of gain until they eventually hold a position for the full five years and claim the Section 1202 exclusion.

Important limitations apply to Section 1045 rollovers. The replacement stock must itself qualify as QSBS at the time of purchase. The election must be made on the taxpayer's return for the year of sale. And the 60-day reinvestment window is strict; there is no provision for extensions, and late rollovers are not permitted.

State Conformity Issues: Why California (and Others) Do Not Follow Section 1202

While the federal QSBS exclusion can eliminate capital gains tax at the federal level, state tax treatment varies widely. The most significant outlier is California, which does not conform to Section 1202 at all. California taxes 100% of the gain on QSBS at the state's ordinary income tax rate, which can reach 13.3% for high earners. For a founder selling a $20 million stake, the California tax bill alone could exceed $2.5 million, even if the federal tax is zero.

Other states with notable non-conformity or partial conformity include:

  • Pennsylvania: Does not fully conform; gain may be subject to Pennsylvania personal income tax.
  • Mississippi: Does not conform to the exclusion.
  • Alabama: Partial conformity with limitations.

Most states follow the federal treatment, meaning that the excluded gain is also excluded from state income tax. States like New York, Texas (no state income tax), Florida (no state income tax), and Montana generally follow federal rules, making these jurisdictions more favorable for QSBS holders. Before any exit, founders should review the specific rules in their state of residence and consider whether relocating before the sale would produce meaningful tax savings.

How to Structure a Business to Qualify for QSBS

Founders who want to preserve QSBS eligibility should address the following structural considerations from the very beginning:

  1. Incorporate as a C corporation from day one. While many advisors recommend LLCs or S corps for early-stage businesses, founders who anticipate a large exit should seriously consider C corporation status to preserve the Section 1202 exclusion. The potential tax savings on a multimillion-dollar exit can dwarf the incremental tax cost of C corporation double taxation during the company's early, low-revenue years.
  2. Keep gross assets below $50 million at each issuance. Track the adjusted basis of all corporate assets and issue stock (including stock from option exercises and convertible instrument conversions) before the company crosses the $50 million threshold.
  3. Ensure the business is a qualified trade or business. Avoid activities that fall into the excluded categories. If the company is on the borderline (for example, a technology company that also provides significant consulting services), document the proportion of revenue and assets attributable to product versus services activities.
  4. Maintain the 80% active business test. Avoid accumulating large cash balances or passive investments without a documented plan for their use. If the company raises a large funding round, prepare a written working capital plan explaining how the proceeds will be deployed over the next 24 months.
  5. Issue stock at original issuance. Ensure that founders, employees, and investors receive stock directly from the corporation, not from existing shareholders. Structure option plans, SAFEs, and convertible notes so that the stock received upon exercise or conversion is treated as issued at original issuance.

Documentation Requirements: Proving QSBS Status to the IRS

The burden of proving QSBS qualification falls on the taxpayer. The IRS can challenge a Section 1202 exclusion claim years after the stock is sold, and reconstructing records from a decade earlier can be extremely difficult. Founders and their advisors should establish a documentation protocol from the date of incorporation and maintain it throughout the company's life.

Essential records include:

  • Articles of incorporation showing C corporation status from the date of organization.
  • Stock purchase agreements and board resolutions documenting each issuance of stock, the consideration paid, and the identity of each purchaser.
  • Balance sheets and asset schedules as of each stock issuance date, proving that gross assets did not exceed $50 million.
  • Annual financial statements demonstrating that at least 80% of corporate assets were used in the active conduct of a qualified trade or business throughout the holding period.
  • Working capital plans for any periods when the company held significant cash or liquid investments.
  • Tax returns showing C corporation status for every year of the holding period (no S election was in effect).
  • Evidence of the qualified trade or business (revenue breakdowns, product descriptions, customer contracts) to demonstrate that the company was not engaged in an excluded activity.

Many companies do not maintain these records systematically, and by the time a sale occurs, critical documents may be lost. The best practice is to create a dedicated QSBS compliance file at the time of incorporation, update it with each stock issuance, and review it annually with a tax advisor.

Common Mistakes That Disqualify QSBS

Even sophisticated founders and their advisors make mistakes that can inadvertently disqualify QSBS. The most common errors include:

  • Starting as an LLC or S corp and converting too late. Stock issued before conversion to C corporation status does not qualify. If a founder holds LLC membership interests that are converted to C corp stock, the converted stock may not meet the original issuance requirement.
  • Exceeding the $50 million gross assets limit without tracking it. Companies that grow rapidly may cross the $50 million threshold without realizing it, disqualifying any stock issued after that point.
  • Significant stock redemptions or buybacks. Section 1202(c)(3) contains anti-abuse rules that disqualify stock if the corporation redeems a significant amount of stock from the taxpayer or a related person within certain time periods around the issuance. Specifically, if the corporation purchases more than a de minimis amount of its stock from the taxpayer (or a related person) within two years before or after the issuance, the stock can be disqualified.
  • Failing the active business test. Companies that accumulate large amounts of cash or passive investments without a documented working capital plan can fail the 80% active business requirement, retroactively disqualifying all outstanding QSBS.
  • Acquiring stock on the secondary market. Employees or investors who buy shares from existing shareholders, rather than receiving them from the corporation, do not meet the original issuance requirement. This is a common issue in late-stage companies where secondary trading is prevalent.
  • Operating an excluded business. Founders sometimes underestimate how broadly the IRS interprets the "consulting" or "financial services" exclusions. A technology company that derives the majority of its revenue from custom consulting engagements may be reclassified as a services firm.

Real-World Examples of QSBS Tax Savings

Example 1: Software Founder Excludes $10 Million in Gain

A founder incorporates a SaaS company as a C corporation in 2020, investing $50,000 for 1,000,000 shares of common stock. The company grows steadily, and in 2026, six years after issuance, the founder sells all shares for $10,050,000. The capital gain is $10,000,000. Because the stock was held for more than five years, was acquired at original issuance from a C corporation with gross assets under $50 million, and the company operated a qualified technology business, the founder claims the full $10 million Section 1202 exclusion. Federal tax on the gain: $0. At a 23.8% combined federal capital gains and net investment income tax rate, the founder saves $2,380,000 in federal taxes.

Example 2: Family Stacking Strategy Excludes $40 Million

A founder holds QSBS with a projected gain of $40 million. Before the sale, the founder gifts equal blocks of shares to three adult children. Each child inherits the founder's basis and holding period. At the time of the sale, the founder excludes $10 million of gain, and each of the three children excludes $10 million, for a total family exclusion of $40 million. At a 23.8% federal rate, the family saves $9,520,000 in federal taxes.

Example 3: Section 1045 Rollover Preserves the Benefit

An angel investor acquires QSBS in a startup for $200,000 in 2022. In 2025, after holding the stock for three years, the startup is acquired and the investor receives $2,000,000. Because the investor has not completed the five-year holding period, the gain does not qualify for the Section 1202 exclusion. However, the investor reinvests the full $2,000,000 in replacement QSBS (another qualifying C corporation) within 60 days and elects a Section 1045 rollover. The $1,800,000 gain is deferred. The replacement stock inherits the original holding period, and when the investor sells the replacement stock in 2028 (six years after the original acquisition), the full gain qualifies for the Section 1202 exclusion.

Example 4: California Founder Faces State Tax Despite Federal Exclusion

A California-based founder sells QSBS with a gain of $15 million. At the federal level, the founder excludes $10 million under Section 1202 and pays federal tax on the remaining $5 million. However, California does not recognize the Section 1202 exclusion. The full $15 million gain is subject to California income tax at 13.3%, resulting in a California tax bill of approximately $1,995,000. This example illustrates why state conformity analysis is a critical component of any QSBS exit strategy.

Interaction with Other Tax Provisions

The QSBS exclusion interacts with several other areas of the tax code that business owners should understand:

  • Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT): Gain excluded under Section 1202 is also excluded from the 3.8% net investment income tax. This means the effective federal tax savings can be as high as 23.8% (20% capital gains rate plus 3.8% NIIT).
  • Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT): For stock acquired after September 27, 2010, the excluded gain is not treated as a preference item for AMT purposes. This was a significant change; prior to this date, a portion of the excluded gain was subject to AMT.
  • Section 1244 (Small Business Stock Losses): If QSBS becomes worthless, the taxpayer may be able to claim an ordinary loss deduction (up to $50,000, or $100,000 for married filing jointly) under Section 1244, rather than a capital loss. This provides a partial offset for the downside risk of investing in small businesses.
  • Estate Planning: QSBS that is inherited does not qualify for the Section 1202 exclusion in the hands of the heir, because the heir did not acquire the stock at original issuance. However, gifting QSBS during the donor's lifetime preserves the exclusion, because the donee inherits the donor's basis and holding period. This makes lifetime gifting preferable to testamentary transfers for QSBS planning purposes.

Next Steps: Is Your Business Positioned for QSBS?

The Section 1202 QSBS exclusion represents one of the most significant tax planning opportunities available to business owners. A successful claim can eliminate millions of dollars in federal capital gains tax. But the requirements are strict, the documentation burden is real, and a single misstep (wrong entity type, missed asset threshold, insufficient records) can invalidate the entire exclusion.

If you are a business owner, founder, or early-stage investor who expects a significant exit in the future, the time to start planning is now. Every year that passes without proper documentation and structural planning increases the risk that the exclusion will be lost.

Frequently Asked Questions About QSBS and Section 1202

What is Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) under Section 1202?

QSBS refers to stock in a domestic C corporation that meets specific requirements under IRC Section 1202. If the stock qualifies, a non-corporate taxpayer can exclude up to 100% of the gain on sale from federal income tax, subject to a per-taxpayer limit of the greater of $10 million or 10 times the adjusted basis of the stock.

How long must I hold QSBS before I can claim the exclusion?

You must hold the qualified small business stock for at least five years from the date of original issuance. If you sell before the five-year mark, you may be able to defer the gain by rolling it into replacement QSBS under Section 1045, provided you reinvest within 60 days.

Does California conform to the federal QSBS exclusion?

No. California is the most notable state that does not conform to the Section 1202 exclusion. California taxes 100% of the gain on QSBS at ordinary state capital gains rates. Founders and investors in California should factor this into their planning, as a large exit could still generate a significant California tax liability.

Can I stack QSBS exclusions across multiple family members?

Yes. The $10 million (or 10x basis) exclusion limit applies on a per-taxpayer, per-issuer basis. By gifting QSBS to family members, trusts, or other entities before a sale, each recipient can claim their own separate exclusion. This strategy can multiply the total amount of gain excluded from federal tax.

What types of businesses are excluded from QSBS treatment?

Several categories of businesses cannot qualify for QSBS treatment under Section 1202. These include professional service firms (health, law, engineering, accounting, consulting, financial services, athletics), banking and insurance companies, hotels and motels, restaurants, farming businesses, and companies involved in mineral extraction. The corporation must use at least 80% of its assets in the active conduct of a qualified trade or business.

What documentation do I need to support a QSBS exclusion claim?

You should maintain articles of incorporation showing C corporation status, stock purchase agreements proving original issuance, board resolutions documenting the issuance, records of the corporation's gross assets at the time of issuance (confirming they were under $50 million), annual financial statements proving active business use of at least 80% of assets, and records showing the five-year holding period was met. Keeping these records from the date of issuance is critical, as reconstructing them years later can be extremely difficult.
