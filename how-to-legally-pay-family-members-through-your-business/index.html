How to Pay Family Members Through Your Business Legally

By AE Tax Advisors

Updated November 8, 2026.

You can pay a family member for genuine work at reasonable compensation. The correct reporting depends on the work relationship and business entity. Wages, contractor fees, partnership payments and shareholder distributions are not interchangeable labels you can select just to lower tax.

Start with work and reasonable pay

Describe the job, who supervises it, expected hours and the rate an unrelated worker would receive for comparable services. Keep work records and evidence of payment. A family relationship does not make personal support or an allowance a deductible business expense.

For example, if records support 100 hours of actual administrative work at an independently supported $20 hourly rate, the wage calculation is $2,000. That illustrates the documentation process; it does not establish a market rate for your role or location.

Employee or independent contractor?

Apply the actual worker-classification rules. Behavioral control, financial control and the relationship between the parties matter. A contractor agreement or Form 1099 does not override employee facts. Conversely, being related does not automatically prevent a genuine independent business from providing services.

Determine classification before payment, then obtain the required forms and follow federal and state reporting. Review state labor law and any child-employment restrictions separately from federal income tax.

Family employment taxes depend on the entity

For a child employed by a parent's sole proprietorship, or a partnership in which each partner is the child's parent, special age-based employment-tax rules can apply: wages before age 18 generally are not subject to Social Security and Medicare, and wages before age 21 generally are not subject to FUTA. Income-tax withholding rules still need review.

Corporate employment generally does not receive those parent-child exemptions merely because the parent owns the company. Wages paid by a corporation are generally subject to the ordinary payroll taxes. A spouse working for a sole proprietor has a different rule: Social Security and Medicare generally apply, but FUTA generally does not. Do not apply one family exemption to every entity.

Owners are not all paid the same way

An S-corporation shareholder who works in the business must evaluate reasonable wages. A nondividend distribution is then analyzed separately under stock-basis and other rules; it is not automatically tax-free and cannot replace required compensation.

Partners generally are self-employed rather than employees of the partnership. Guaranteed payments, distributive shares and actual cash draws have distinct reporting. Self-employment tax is not determined simply by how much cash a partner withdraws. Review the partnership agreement and applicable exceptions with the return preparer.

Spouse wages and retirement plans

Moving income between spouses on a joint return does not by itself create a lower income-tax bracket. Payroll taxes, retirement contribution capacity, benefit eligibility and administrative costs can change the household result. Plan contributions require qualifying compensation and compliance with the plan, including any employee-coverage requirements.

Health benefits require an additional analysis. Ownership attribution can affect a spouse's eligibility for tax-free benefits in an S corporation. Do not assume a reimbursement arrangement that works for one entity works for another. See the spouse-employment guide.

Do not rely on a related-party accrual shortcut

An accrual-method company cannot automatically deduct December family compensation simply because it pays it within two-and-a-half months. Related-party matching and deferred-compensation rules may postpone the deduction until the recipient includes the amount in income. Review the recipient, accounting methods and payment dates before booking a year-end deduction.

Keep a file a reviewer can follow

  • Job description, classification decision and applicable employment forms.
  • Contemporaneous work records and support for reasonable pay.
  • Payroll reports, withholding, deposits and year-end reporting.
  • Actual payment evidence and expense-category reconciliation.
  • Benefit and retirement-plan eligibility documentation.
  • Ownership and relationship facts relevant to special rules.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I deduct money paid to my child?

Only where the payment meets the applicable business deduction and compensation rules. Genuine work, reasonable pay and proper records matter; personal support is not deductible wages.

Are children exempt from payroll taxes in every family business?

No. The age-based parent-child rules depend on the employer’s structure. Corporate wages generally do not receive the sole-proprietor exemption.

Can a family member choose to be a contractor?

The facts determine classification. A contract title or Form 1099 does not turn an employee into an independent contractor.

Does paying my spouse automatically lower our income tax?

No. On a joint return, simply shifting earnings does not create a separate lower bracket. Evaluate payroll taxes, benefits, retirement capacity and costs.
