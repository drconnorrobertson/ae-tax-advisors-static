1|A $1 million S corporation profit number may appear on Schedule K-1 while the owner also has W-2 wages. 27|Suppose a non-service business has $100,000 of W-2 wages and no eligible qualified property. The wage-only limb is $50,000; the alternative is $25,000. 29|An owner has operating-company income and a separately managed long-term rental. 30|An S corporation owner proposes increasing wages by $100,000 to relax a QBI wage limit. 32|The federal safe harbor is based on the prior year's tax shown on a complete return, with a higher percentage when prior-year AGI exceeded the applicable threshold. Divide the required annual payment into installments after considering withholding, then compare with a current-year tax projection.|Retain the prior return, this year's withholding statements, dated estimates and Form 2210 working papers.|The safe harbor addresses penalty exposure, not the amount of tax ultimately owed with the next return; states can use different tests.
33|If projected business profit rises from $500,000 to $1 million in September, calculate the updated annual tax and compare it with prior-year safe-harbor payments already made. Set cash aside for the April balance while checking whether annualized-income installments are useful.|Use monthly management accounts, estimated payment confirmations, payroll withholding and a dated full-year forecast.|An owner who meets a penalty safe harbor can still face a very large balance due at filing.
34|An owner who underpaid the first three quarters may ask payroll to withhold additional federal income tax on remaining wages. Recalculate Form 2210 with withholding and actual quarterly payments before assuming the year-end catch-up works.|Check payroll calendar, available gross wages, tax deposit timing, prior-year tax and withholding records.|Withholding cannot exceed available wages, and the state may treat late payments differently.
35|A shareholder might make four personal federal estimated payments while the corporation remits payroll taxes and a state elective pass-through tax. 36|Suppose a business reports $1 million of taxable profit but spends $300,000 on loan principal, has $200,000 more receivables and distributes $250,000. 37|If the owner transfers $50,000 to a personal account, record a draw or distribution rather than a deductible consulting expense. If the business instead pays a bona fide vendor for a necessary business service, document the expense separately.|Reconcile owner equity, distributions, credit-card expenses, payroll and bank transfers against invoices and receipts.|The tax treatment follows what happened, not the account or card used for the payment.
38|Start with the balance due shown on the filed return and identify whose debt it is. Compare an IRS installment agreement's payments, setup cost, interest and penalties with available cash, while remaining current on new payroll and estimated-tax obligations.|Collect notices, filed returns, payment history, bank statements and a realistic monthly cash-flow forecast.|An agreement does not eliminate interest or make a delinquent return optional.
40|A calendar-year S corporation discovers a large profit only after December 31. 46|A 20-person company may find that a one-participant solo 401(k) is unsuitable and that a conventional 401(k) requires nondiscrimination testing or safe-harbor contributions. 50|Before a $5 million company sale, compare whether the buyer is acquiring stock or assets and whether existing employees and the plan continue. 52|A business pays $120,000 in December for a machine that arrives in January. 58|A software company incurs domestic developer payroll, foreign contractor costs and routine SaaS subscriptions. 75|An owner travels to a conference with a spouse and children, adds vacation days, and charges all airfare and hotel nights to the company. 76|A spouse who handles bookkeeping for 15 documented hours per week may be paid reasonable wages and become eligible for a retirement plan under its terms.
