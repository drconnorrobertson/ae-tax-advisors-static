Should a real estate agent put rentals in the same S-corp as commission income?

The ownership decision and the rental-loss decision are separate. Model both before transferring a property.

Usually there is no tax-loss reason to deed an agent's rental into the S corporation that receives commissions. The commission business and each rental remain distinct activities for the passive-loss rules even if one corporation owns them. A cost-segregation deduction will not automatically offset commissions just because both appear on the same Form 1120-S. The answer can differ with the title history, financing, state law, and exit plan, so review the facts before signing a deed.

If you have a commission S-corp, an existing rental, or a study proposal, AE can compare the entity returns and your individual loss limitations before the transaction or filing. Bring both ownership records and the time logs, not just a projected depreciation number.

First separate three questions that often get merged

  1. Who should own the rental? An S corporation may legally own real estate, but putting an appreciated rental inside a commission company can make a later transfer out expensive. Ownership, lender consent, insurance, liability, and state brokerage rules need coordinated review; a separate LLC is not by itself a tax deduction.
  2. Is the rental loss usable now? Rental real estate is generally passive. Brokerage work can count toward real-estate-professional status, but the individual must satisfy the more-than-half and over-750-hour tests in qualifying real property trades or businesses and materially participate in the rental activity. Each rental is generally tested separately unless a valid election groups rental real estate interests. IRS Publication 925 explains those separate tests.
  3. Does cost segregation change the timing? A defensible study can accelerate depreciation on qualifying components; it does not change the passive character of the activity or create basis. Analyze the tax year, asset basis, financing, passive-income capacity, and likely sale before paying for a study. See AE's real-estate cost-segregation guide.

What happens when the S-corp owns both activities?

Combining commission operations and a rental on one set of books is not a loss-offset election. The Form 1120-S instructions require the corporation to report the separate activities to shareholders: ordinary trade or business income in Schedule K-1 box 1 and rental real estate income or loss in box 2, with additional activity detail when needed. The shareholder then applies stock-and-debt basis, at-risk, passive-activity, and other individual limitations in order. The Form 7203 instructions describe that ordering. A book entry netting the two activities cannot override it.

There is also an exit cost to test. The Schedule D (Form 1120-S) instructions explain that a nonliquidating distribution of appreciated property generally causes the corporation to recognize gain as if the property were sold for fair market value. For example, a rental with $250,000 of adjusted basis and $600,000 of value may create $350,000 of corporate-level recognized gain when distributed to the owner, before considering the character of the gain, liabilities, shareholder consequences, and state tax. A deed back to the shareholder is not automatically a tax-free reversal. AE's appreciated-property distribution guide covers that exit in detail.

Worked comparison: the same study, different loss result

Agent A. An owner-operator works 1,700 documented hours in a brokerage S-corp, owns a long-term rental through a separate single-member LLC, spends 40 hours on it, and hires a manager who spends 300 hours. A study proposes $80,000 of additional depreciation. The brokerage hours may help Agent A satisfy the real-estate-professional thresholds, but 40 rental hours do not, on these assumed facts, establish material participation in that rental. The $80,000 is not automatically available to offset commission income; it may be suspended under the passive-loss rules, subject to the full return facts. Deeding the rental into the brokerage S-corp does not cure that problem.

Agent B. Assume the same 1,700 brokerage hours and study, but the agent also documents 550 hours of qualifying participation in the rental. The more-than-500-hour material-participation test may be met for that rental, and the real-estate-professional tests may also be met if the brokerage is a qualifying business in which the agent materially participates. The resulting rental loss may then be nonpassive and potentially offset other nonpassive income on the individual return, but it still must pass basis, at-risk, excess-business-loss, and other applicable limits. That result does not require placing the rental in the commission S-corp. These are simplified comparisons, not projected refunds.

Decision path before you move a deed or order a study

  1. Identify the legal owner of each rental and who receives commissions. Confirm the brokerage agreement, title, debt covenants, insurance, and state licensing requirements with the relevant professionals.
  2. Reconstruct the rental's adjusted tax basis and debt, including previous depreciation and improvements. If it is already in a corporation, model both keeping it there and a future distribution or sale.
  3. Test real-estate-professional status using the individual's complete work calendar, including the employee/5%-owner rule in Publication 925. Then test material participation in each rental or a properly elected rental aggregation. Do not treat a professional license as proof of either test.
  4. Model the proposed depreciation alongside rental income, existing passive-loss carryforwards, stock/debt basis where relevant, at-risk amount, and eventual sale or recapture. Compare a separate-ownership scenario with a corporation-owned scenario.
  5. Only then decide whether the ownership change and study solve a real filing problem. Document the result before the return and K-1 are finalized.

Documents to bring to a coordinated review

  • Commission S-corp Form 1120-S, shareholder K-1, payroll records, reasonable-compensation support, and brokerage payment agreement.
  • Rental deeds, LLC or corporation formation documents, closing statements, loan agreements, insurance declarations, and any contemplated transfer documents.
  • Rental schedules, Form 4562 detail, prior studies, capital-improvement records, Form 8582 carryforwards, and any existing rental-aggregation election.
  • Contemporaneous brokerage and property-level time records, property-manager invoices, and the proposed study scope and asset allocation.

Failure points to avoid

  • Assuming brokerage hours alone make every rental loss nonpassive; the rental participation test is separate.
  • Assuming the S-corp's commission profit and rental loss can be netted on one K-1 line without activity reporting.
  • Using an S-corp distribution as a casual way to move appreciated real estate back out.
  • Buying a study before confirming the loss can be used and the accelerated assets will be supported and tracked through sale.
  • Setting agent wages as an arbitrary percentage. The IRS reasonable-compensation guidance requires a facts-based wage for services before non-wage distributions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will putting a rental in my commission S-corp make its loss nonpassive?

No. The rental and commission activities retain their own character. A qualifying real estate professional must also materially participate in the rental activity, or in a properly elected aggregation of rental interests, before the rental loss is nonpassive. Basis, at-risk, and other limitations may still apply.

Is there a 60/40 rule for an agent's S-corp salary?

No. There is no universal salary-to-distribution percentage. The S corporation must pay reasonable compensation for the services the shareholder-employee provides before making non-wage distributions; compare duties, time, receipts generated by the agent's work, and market compensation.

What happens if my agent S-corp salary is too low?

The IRS may reclassify some non-wage payments as wages. The result depends on the facts and can include employment tax, interest, and applicable penalties. A defensible wage analysis should precede distribution planning.

Does an S-corp election guarantee lower total tax for a real estate agent?

No. S-corp treatment may change employment tax, deductions, qualified business income, state taxes, and compliance costs. Compare complete federal and state returns with a reasonable wage before assuming a net benefit.
