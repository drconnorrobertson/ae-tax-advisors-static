Business Exit Tax Planning: Minimize Taxes When Selling Your Company

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

Start business-exit planning with the after-tax proceeds you will actually keep. A $5,000,000 offer does not establish the tax bill: entity type, adjusted basis, asset allocation, elections, debt, fees and payment timing can change both the amount and when tax is due. Compare those items before agreeing to a letter of intent.

Reviewed November 6, 2026. The calculations below are hypothetical planning illustrations, not a client result or a promise of savings.

Understanding the Gain on Sale

In a simplified stock sale, $5,000,000 proceeds less $100,000 adjusted stock basis produce $4,900,000 of gain, assuming no selling costs or other adjustments. If all of that gain were long-term capital gain taxed at 20%, federal capital-gains tax would be $980,000. If the entire gain were also subject to 3.8% net investment income tax, that adds $186,200, for $1,166,200 before state tax.

Those are assumed rates and applicability, not an estimate for every owner. Stock basis may differ from the amount originally invested, and NIIT treatment for S corporation or partnership interests requires additional analysis. An asset sale needs separate asset bases and gain categories rather than one assumed capital-gain rate.

Entity Type Impacts Tax Outcome

C corporation asset sales can create tax on corporate gain and then shareholder-level tax when proceeds are distributed. S corporation and partnership gains generally pass through, but built-in gains tax, ordinary-income items, basis and other exceptions can change the outcome. A late entity conversion does not erase existing appreciation or its tax consequences.

For a deliberately simplified comparison, assume a C corporation sells assets for $5,000,000 with $100,000 asset basis and pays 21% federal corporate tax on its $4,900,000 gain: $1,029,000. If it immediately liquidates, has no debt, fees or other adjustments, and distributes the remaining $3,971,000 to an individual with $100,000 stock basis, the $3,871,000 shareholder gain taxed at an assumed 23.8% is $921,298. Total federal tax would be $1,950,298.

Under the separate stock-sale assumptions above, the total was $1,166,200. The $784,098 difference illustrates how two tax levels can matter; it is not an available saving unless a buyer accepts a qualifying alternative at an economically comparable price. Different bases, allocation, losses, NIIT applicability, state rules and buyer price adjustments can change or reverse the comparison.

Timing the Sale

Project income and usable losses by year before choosing a closing date. Capital losses generally offset capital gains, while passive losses, basis, at-risk and other limitations require their own analysis. A suspended loss is not automatically available merely because a sale occurs. Compare the business economics and tax consequences together.

Installment Sales

Section 453 can defer eligible gain when at least one payment is received after the sale year. Apply the gross profit percentage to principal payments, with interest accounted for separately. Inventory and other excluded items do not qualify, and ordinary depreciation recapture is recognized in the sale year even if cash is received later. A lower total tax bill depends on actual rates and other facts.

Allocating Purchase Price

An applicable asset acquisition requires a defensible allocation among asset classes. Inventory and receivables generally produce ordinary income, depreciated equipment can produce ordinary recapture, and goodwill or other intangible assets require their own basis, holding-period and recapture analysis. Do not assume every intangible is capital gain or move value between categories solely to obtain a preferred rate.

Support the allocation with the agreement and appropriate valuation work. A covenant not to compete generally produces ordinary income for the seller, while acquired Section 197 intangibles are generally amortized over 15 years by the buyer. Model how allocation affects both parties before negotiating the price.

Seller Financing

A buyer note transfers collection risk to the seller. Compare the down payment with the immediate tax reserve, collateral, guarantees, default remedies, adequate stated interest and the timing of later principal payments. Interest is generally ordinary income and is separate from installment gain. Large notes and pledges can have additional tax costs; deferral does not make the note equivalent to cash.

Stock vs. Asset Sale

A Section 338 election can treat a qualifying stock purchase as a deemed sale and acquisition of the target corporation's assets for federal income-tax purposes. It does not convert an asset sale into a stock sale. The buyer may receive a basis step-up, while the deemed asset sale can create ordinary recapture and other seller or target tax costs.

A qualified stock purchase generally requires one corporation to purchase at least 80% of another corporation's voting power and value within a 12-month acquisition period, applying the statutory purchase rules. Section 338(h)(10) is available only for eligible targets and sellers, including qualifying S corporation or selling-group transactions. It requires a joint election; all S corporation shareholders must consent, including those not selling. A Section 338(g) election has different tax and consent consequences.

Model the deemed-sale allocation, recapture, liabilities, entity and shareholder taxes, buyer deductions and any negotiated price increase before agreeing to an election. Form 8023 generally is due by the 15th day of the ninth month after acquisition. Have the transaction team confirm eligibility, required signatures and deadlines.

Sources: IRS Form 8023 instructions: qualified stock purchases and elections, IRS Form 8883 instructions: deemed asset-sale allocation.

Planning Before the Sale

Begin as early as practical. Some entity, ownership and exclusion requirements need years, while a transaction already in progress can still benefit from allocation, financing and tax-reserve work. Do not change entity status, transfer the building or make gifts just to fit a generic checklist.

Bring the offer or LOI, current ownership and entity elections, stock or partnership basis, asset depreciation schedules, debt, proposed allocation, earnout or rollover terms and any building you intend to retain. Ask your tax advisor and attorney for a comparison of feasible structures, cash at closing, future payments, tax reserves and who will implement each step. Arrange secure document delivery after agreeing scope.

Post-Sale Tax Obligations

Reporting follows the transaction. Form 8594 generally applies to an applicable acquisition of a trade or business's assets under Section 1060. A Section 338 deemed asset transaction instead has Form 8883 allocation reporting in addition to the Form 8023 election. An ordinary stock sale does not automatically require Form 8594.

Installment treatment may require Form 6252 and related gain reporting. Contingent payments, escrow, earnouts and payments for future services need separate characterization; their labels alone do not defer tax. Track estimated payments, state filings and any post-closing allocation changes.

Sources: IRS Form 8594 instructions, IRS Publication 537.

Generational Wealth Transfer

An inheritance and a lifetime gift generally have different basis rules. Property acquired from a decedent commonly receives a fair-market-value basis under Section 1014, subject to exceptions; lifetime gifts generally carry over the donor's basis under Section 1015, with special loss rules. A gift does not ordinarily reproduce the inherited-basis adjustment.

Inherited stock basis also does not automatically step up the corporation's inside asset basis. Coordinate income tax, estate and gift tax, control and cash needs with qualified advisors before transferring ownership.

Sources: IRS Publication 551: inherited and gifted property basis.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a Section 338 election do?

It can treat a qualifying stock purchase as a deemed sale and acquisition of corporate assets for federal income-tax purposes. It does not turn an asset sale into a stock sale. Eligibility, recapture, allocation, required consents and the seller or target tax cost must be modeled.

Do all business sales require Form 8594?

No. Form 8594 generally applies to applicable asset acquisitions under Section 1060. Section 338 deemed asset transactions use Form 8883 allocation reporting with the Form 8023 election. An ordinary stock sale does not automatically require Form 8594.

Can seller financing defer the entire tax bill?

No. Eligible gain may be recognized as principal payments arrive, but ordinary recapture is taxable in the sale year, excluded assets have separate rules, and interest is generally ordinary income. Compare the immediate tax reserve and collection risk before accepting a note.

What should I bring before agreeing to an LOI?

Bring the offer, ownership and entity history, stock or partnership basis, asset depreciation, debt, proposed allocation, earnout or rollover terms and plans for any building. Request a comparison of feasible after-tax proceeds and implementation responsibilities.
