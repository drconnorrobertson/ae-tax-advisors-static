Equipment Tax Planning: Depreciation, Credits and Rental Losses

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

Compare the tax owner, service date, depreciation, research credits and rental losses separately before projecting entity or owner cash taxes.

Establish the tax owner and service date

Collect the purchase invoice, financing or lease contract, installation record, business-use support and asset schedule. An order or deposit does not by itself make equipment available for use.

Under a true business rental the lessee generally evaluates rent. Under a financed purchase or an agreement characterized as a conditional sale, the tax owner evaluates depreciable basis and eligible interest. Confirm the contract treatment before modeling the deduction.

Compare regular depreciation, bonus depreciation and Section 179

Use the actual acquisition and placed-in-service dates, asset class, business use, and applicable elections. Section 179 has limits and special restrictions for certain noncorporate lessors. Bonus depreciation requires eligible property and an analysis of the rules for the actual dates.

Keep research credits separate from operating deductions

If the business is conducting research, identify the qualified activities and costs that support the Form 6765 calculation. Equipment spending or software work does not automatically qualify. Ask the preparer to reconcile the credit and related deduction or basis treatment under current law.

Test rentals and entities before treating losses as usable

Gather the rental-use and personal-use calendar, records of participation, and depreciation schedules. Check limitations on each activity and owner. Real estate professional status alone does not establish material participation in every rental activity. A rental conversion also requires an analysis of service dates and depreciation basis.

A C corporation’s deductions and credits generally enter its own return. Pass-through items require separate owner-level analysis. Use a distinct projection for each return, including filing costs, carryforwards, and the tax effect when an asset is sold.

Worked example: funding needs and deductions are separate

Suppose an operating company is choosing between a $120,000 equipment purchase and a true rental costing $3,000 per month. Neither number by itself answers the question. Obtain the term, financing, maintenance, expected use, buyout or resale value, and service date. Build the cash-flow comparison first.

Then add the deductible rent or the tax owner’s supported depreciation and eligible interest. Keep any research credit and rental loss on separate lines. Compare expected cash taxes under the current plan and each alternative, including scenarios in which a deduction is delayed or unavailable. The numbers are hypothetical.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a business claim depreciation on equipment it rents?

A true lessee generally evaluates rent. In an agreement treated as a conditional sale, the tax owner generally evaluates depreciation. The contract and transaction facts determine treatment.

Does financing equipment make a first-year deduction automatic?

No. Confirm tax ownership, asset class, acquisition and service dates, business use, elections and limits before forecasting any deduction. Loan payments and cost recovery are separate.

Can a short-term rental loss automatically offset wages or business income?

No. Review personal use, the activity classification, material participation, and other applicable loss limits. Use the taxpayer’s actual records to identify when a loss may be deductible.

Can the same taxpayer use every modeled deduction and credit in the current year?

The taxpayer, tax year and applicable limits determine the usable amounts. Build a separate projection for the entity and its owners where relevant. Show deferred or disallowed amounts separately.
