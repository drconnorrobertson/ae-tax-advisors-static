Augusta Rule For Business Owners When It Works And How To Do It Safely

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

The Augusta Rule under IRC Section 280A(g) can exclude rental income from a dwelling used as a residence and actually rented for fewer than 15 days during the tax year. For the residence test in Section 280A(d)(1), personal use must be more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days rented at a fair rental. Exactly 14 personal-use days is not enough.

The exclusion can apply to rentals to unrelated guests. Renting to your own business adds a separate question: whether the business can deduct its payment. Neither a large proposed payment nor a signed agreement, on its own, establishes the tax result.

The Basic Rule: IRC Section 280A(g)

If the dwelling meets the residence test and has fewer than 15 actual rental days for the tax year, Section 280A(g) excludes the rental income and disallows deductions attributable to that rental use. A property does not become a qualifying residence simply because its rental calendar is short.

For example, suppose a Scottsdale vacation home has 30 qualifying personal-use days and is rented to an unrelated company for 10 days at a hypothetical $5,000 per day, with no other rentals. The $50,000 of rent can be excluded under Section 280A(g). The rate and amount illustrate the mechanics, not a current quote or a promised result.

The owner cannot deduct rental-use utilities, repairs, insurance or depreciation against excluded rent. Qualified mortgage interest and property taxes may still be deductible on Schedule A under the ordinary itemized-deduction rules and limits. A mortgage payment is not itself an interest deduction; principal payments do not become deductible.

The Requirement to Use the Property as Your Residence

Personal use must exceed the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. With fewer than 15 rental days, this ordinarily means at least 15 personal-use days. Two weeks of personal use is insufficient. A primary residence may readily meet the test; a vacation or investment property needs a careful calendar review.

Section 280A(d) contains rules for family use, below-market rentals, exchanges of use and repair days. Do not label every visit a personal day or every payment a fair-rental day without applying those rules. Record the actual dates, users and purpose of use, and retain supporting records.

The Fair Market Value Requirement

Reasonable rent is critical when your own business claims a deduction. Compare what an unrelated renter would pay for the same property, space, hours, date, capacity and services. A $1,000 daily proposal and a $3,000 whole-house overnight listing are not automatically comparable. Explain the differences before using either figure.

For a daytime business meeting, obtain comparable meeting-space or venue quotes rather than relying only on a luxury vacation listing. Short-term rental sites, local property managers and appraisals can help when they reflect the actual use. Special-event pricing is not automatically invalid, but it needs relevant evidence. Below-market use can also affect the residence calendar under Section 280A(d).

When the Business Deduction Needs More Review

The homeowner's Section 280A(g) exclusion does not establish the company's deduction. The business must substantiate an ordinary and necessary expense under Section 162, reasonable rent and a genuine business purpose. Keep the agenda, attendees and work performed. Section 274 can disallow entertainment and entertainment-facility expenses even when the owner qualifies for the rental-income exclusion.

A sole proprietor cannot deduct rent paid to themselves for their own property. The same concern applies to an LLC disregarded as separate from that owner for federal income tax. A separately taxed company may qualify as the payer, but incorporation alone does not establish a deductible meeting. This limitation on self-rent does not prevent an individual from renting a qualifying home to unrelated guests.

Building a Defensible Augusta Rule Position

  1. Check residence eligibility. Establish more than the greater of 14 personal-use days or 10% of fair-rental days using the applicable personal-use rules.
  2. Use one annual rental calendar. Count all actual rental days for the dwelling, across unrelated renters and related businesses. The limit is fewer than 15 days in total, not per entity.
  3. Support the business purpose and rate. Keep meeting plans, attendance and dated comparable quotes with an explanation of differences.
  4. Document the transaction. Prepare a written agreement before the event and retain invoices and evidence of actual payment.
  5. Coordinate reporting. Do not report qualifying excluded rent or related rental expenses on Schedule E. Have the payer's and recipient's preparers review information-return obligations for the applicable year and the handling of any Form 1099 received.
  6. Review state and local obligations. Federal income exclusion does not resolve state treatment, local lodging taxes, permits or contractual restrictions.

The Real Constraints Of The Rule

At 15 actual rental days, the exclusion is unavailable for the entire year's rental income. Reporting and permitted deductions then depend on personal use, expense allocation and other applicable rules. The extra day does not automatically permit a full depreciation or rental-loss deduction.

A property advertised at $2,000 per day does not support a $10,000 related-business rate without evidence for the actual use and terms. A high payment accepted by an unrelated guest and a deduction claimed by your own company raise different questions; do not treat the homeowner exclusion as approval of the company's price.

If the property has a $3,000 monthly mortgage payment, $500 property tax and $300 utilities, those amounts cannot be deducted as rental expenses against Section 280A(g) income. Separately review qualifying interest and real estate taxes under the personal deduction rules. These illustrative costs do not establish either current market prices or a tax benefit.

Likewise, $50,000 of excluded gross rent is not $50,000 of tax savings. Compare the actual alternative after its allowable costs, tax rates and limitations. For a related-business rental, model the supported business deduction, any QBI effect and state treatment separately rather than multiplying gross rent by an assumed combined rate.

Comparable Rental Market Analysis

As a planning illustration, Scottsdale vacation-home rates might range from $1,500 to $3,500 per night depending on size, season and amenities. This range is not a current market quote or support for a particular deduction. Obtain dated listings or quotes for the actual location and rental dates.

A proposed $2,000 nightly rate supported by genuinely comparable $1,800 to $2,300 listings is a starting point for analysis. Preserve the screenshots, availability, terms and adjustments. If the business needs only one room for a few hours, compare that use rather than charging a whole-home overnight rate.

The Combination With Cost Segregation

Section 280A(g) does not allow depreciation or cost-segregation deductions attributable to the same excluded rental use. Separately owned investment rentals can have their own depreciation analysis. Other uses of the same home, prior depreciation and a later change in use also require separate review; the exclusion does not erase existing depreciation history.

If the home instead falls under the normal rental rules, allowable depreciation and expenses depend on the actual business or rental use and applicable limitations. Do not describe the choice as an automatic full deduction on one side or unlimited tax-free rent on the other.

When To Use The Rule And When To Avoid It

Consider a review if you genuinely use a home as a residence, expect no more than 14 actual rental days for the whole tax year, and can document the arrangement. If the renter is your own company, confirm the company's business need and independent deduction requirements before setting the rent.

Do not rely on this strategy for a pure investment property that fails the residence test, a home with 15 or more rental days, an invented meeting or an unsupported related-party rate. Before booking, give your adviser the personal and rental calendars, entity tax classification, proposed agreement, comparable quotes and payment plan. That lets the adviser assess both returns and identify what is missing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Augusta Rule?

IRC Section 280A(g) excludes rental income from a dwelling used as a residence and rented for fewer than 15 days during the tax year. Section 280A(d)(1) requires personal use to exceed the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. No deductions attributable to that rental use are allowed.

Can I use my vacation home under the Augusta Rule?

A vacation home may qualify if it meets the residence test and is actually rented for fewer than 15 days in total during the tax year. Exactly 14 personal-use days is insufficient. Count rentals to all renters and related businesses together.

What is the tax benefit of the Augusta Rule?

Qualifying rent is excluded from federal gross income, with no rental-expense deduction for that use. A separate business may have a rent deduction only if it independently satisfies the business-expense rules. Savings depend on the facts, tax rates, QBI effects and state treatment.

What are the risks with the Augusta Rule?

Key risks are failing the residence test, reaching 15 rental days, deducting expenses against excluded rent, or claiming a business deduction without a genuine purpose and reasonable rent. Keep calendars, comparable quotes, agreements and payment records, and coordinate reporting with both preparers.
