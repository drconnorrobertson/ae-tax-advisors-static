How Much Does a Tax Advisor Save You? Real ROI Data

By AE Tax Advisors Team | Published October 3, 2026

Business owners and high-income professionals consistently ask the same question before engaging a tax strategist: "Will this actually save me more than it costs?" The answer, backed by client data and IRS benchmarks, is a decisive yes for the vast majority of earners above $200,000. In fact, strategic tax advisory services routinely deliver a 5:1 to 20:1 return on investment. That means for every dollar you spend on advisory fees, you can expect to save five to twenty dollars in federal and state taxes.

This article breaks down the real numbers. We will show average savings by income level, compare savings by strategy type, illustrate the long-term compounding effect of reinvested tax savings, and address the situations where a tax advisor may not be worth the investment. By the end, you will have a clear, data-driven framework for evaluating whether professional tax advisory is the right move for your financial situation.

Not all tax situations are created equal. The higher your income and the more complex your financial picture, the greater the opportunity for strategic tax reduction. Here is what we typically see across our client base, organized by adjusted gross income.

An important detail: these savings are annual. A business owner who saves $75,000 per year for ten years has reduced their tax burden by $750,000 before accounting for the growth potential of those reinvested savings. We will explore that compounding effect later in this article.

Tax Savings Broken Down by Strategy Type

Understanding where the savings come from helps business owners evaluate which strategies apply to their situation. Below are the six most impactful strategies we implement, along with the typical savings range for each.

1. S-Corp Election and Entity Optimization

For business owners operating as sole proprietors or single-member LLCs, electing S-Corp tax treatment is often the single most valuable first step. By splitting income between salary and distributions, business owners reduce their exposure to the 15.3% self-employment tax (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare). For a business netting $300,000, the savings from a properly structured S-Corp election typically range from $15,000 to $40,000 annually. The exact amount depends on the reasonable compensation level determined for the owner's role, industry, and geography.

2. Retirement Plan Optimization

Most business owners contribute to a SEP IRA or a basic 401(k), which limits their annual deduction to $69,000 or less. By implementing a defined benefit plan or a cash balance plan, high-income owners can shelter $100,000 to $300,000+ per year in tax-deductible retirement contributions. A dentist earning $600,000 annually, for example, might increase their annual retirement deduction from $69,000 to $180,000, producing an additional $40,000 to $50,000 in immediate tax savings at a combined 37% federal and state rate.

3. Cost Segregation Studies

Real estate investors and business owners who own commercial property can accelerate depreciation by reclassifying building components into 5-year, 7-year, and 15-year MACRS categories instead of the standard 27.5-year or 39-year schedule. A cost segregation study on a $1.2 million property typically identifies $350,000 to $500,000 in reclassifiable assets, generating first-year tax savings of $30,000 to $200,000 depending on bonus depreciation rates. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025, bonus depreciation was restored to 100% permanently, making cost segregation one of the most powerful tools available to property owners today.

4. Reasonable Compensation Planning

Setting the right salary for an S-Corp owner is both an art and a science. Too low, and you risk an IRS audit with back taxes and penalties. Too high, and you forfeit the self-employment tax savings that S-Corp election provides. Strategic reasonable compensation analysis ensures that your salary is defensible under IRS standards (using BLS data, industry surveys, and comparable role benchmarks) while maximizing the tax-advantaged distribution portion of your income. The difference between a poorly set salary and an optimized one often represents $8,000 to $25,000 in annual savings.

5. Year-End Tax Planning

Proactive year-end planning involves timing income and deductions, evaluating equipment purchases under Section 179, accelerating or deferring revenue based on projected brackets, and implementing charitable strategies such as donor-advised funds or qualified charitable distributions. These tactics typically save clients an additional $10,000 to $40,000 per year. The key is having an advisor who reviews your financials in October or November rather than looking backward in March.

6. Amended Return Recovery

One of the most overlooked areas of tax savings is the opportunity to amend prior-year returns. Business owners who have been overpaying taxes for years, whether due to missed deductions, unclaimed credits, or suboptimal entity elections, can file amended returns for the previous three tax years. We routinely recover $10,000 to $100,000+ per client through amended returns. In one case, a real estate investor recovered over $87,000 by retroactively applying a cost segregation study to a property purchased four years earlier.

"I Already Have a CPA": What Compliance-Only Preparers Miss

This is the most common objection we hear, and it deserves a thorough response. Having a CPA is important. Compliance-focused accountants ensure your returns are accurate, filed on time, and consistent with IRS requirements. That work is valuable, and we never suggest replacing your CPA.

However, there is a fundamental difference between compliance and strategy. A compliance CPA records what happened last year. A tax strategist plans what should happen next year. These are entirely different disciplines, and most accounting firms focus heavily on the former while leaving the latter to clients who rarely have the expertise to execute it themselves.

Here is what compliance-only CPAs typically miss:

  • Entity election optimization. Many CPAs file returns for the entity structure the client already has, without evaluating whether a different election (S-Corp, C-Corp, or a multi-entity structure) would produce better tax outcomes.
  • Retirement plan upgrades. A compliance CPA records your SEP IRA contribution. A strategist identifies that you could shelter three times as much income with a defined benefit or cash balance plan.
  • Cost segregation referrals. Most CPAs use straight-line depreciation on rental properties because cost segregation studies require specialized engineering analysis. That conservative approach costs property owners tens of thousands of dollars per year in deferred deductions.
  • Proactive year-end planning. The typical CPA engagement is reactive: you send documents in February, they file in April. Strategic planning requires a review of estimated income in Q3 or Q4, with time to implement deductions, contributions, and entity changes before December 31.
  • Amended return opportunities. Compliance CPAs rarely look backward. A strategist reviews the prior three years of returns for missed deductions, unclaimed credits, and structural changes that can be applied retroactively.

In our experience, approximately 80% of new clients who come to us with an existing CPA relationship discover $30,000 or more in annual savings that their previous preparer did not identify. This is not a criticism of those CPAs. It is a reflection of the fact that tax preparation and tax strategy are two distinct services, and most firms offer only one.

When a Tax Advisor Is NOT Worth It

Honesty builds trust, so here is the straightforward answer: strategic tax advisory is not a good fit for everyone. If your financial situation is straightforward, the cost of advisory services may exceed the potential savings. Specifically, a tax advisor may not deliver meaningful ROI if:

  • You are a W-2 employee earning under $150,000 with no investments, rental properties, or business income.
  • You claim the standard deduction and have no itemizable expenses.
  • You have no entity planning opportunities (no LLC, no S-Corp election potential, no rental income).
  • Your financial situation has not changed in years and you expect it to remain stable.

In these cases, a good CPA or even quality tax software is sufficient. The cost of a strategic advisor, typically $5,000 to $15,000 annually, would not be justified by the limited savings available in a simple tax situation.

However, once any of the following triggers appear, the calculus changes dramatically: you start a business, acquire rental property, begin earning over $200,000, sell a business or investment, inherit assets, or plan for retirement. At each of these inflection points, proactive tax strategy becomes the highest-return investment available to you.

The fundamental difference is proactive versus reactive service. Tax software and compliance CPAs file returns based on what already happened. A tax strategist works throughout the year to structure your income, entities, deductions, and investments so that your tax obligation is minimized before the year ends. That forward-looking approach is what produces the 5:1 to 20:1 ROI that makes advisory fees one of the best investments a business owner can make.

It is also worth noting that hiring a tax strategist does not replace your CPA or tax software. Strategic advisory works best as a complementary layer. Your strategist develops the plan, and your CPA implements it on your returns. The two roles work together, each adding value in their area of expertise.

How to Evaluate a Tax Advisory Firm

Not all advisory firms deliver the same results. When evaluating potential partners, look for these indicators of quality:

  • Specific, measurable projections. A credible firm will provide estimated savings based on your actual financial data, not vague promises of "significant savings."
  • Strategy-first approach. The firm should lead with strategy and planning, not with compliance and filing. Filing is important, but it is not where the value is created.
  • Credentialed professionals. Look for Enrolled Agents, CPAs, or tax attorneys on staff. Credentials alone do not guarantee strategic skill, but they establish a baseline of technical competence.
  • Transparent pricing. Fees should be clearly stated and tied to deliverables, not buried in hourly billing that makes the total cost unpredictable.
  • Case studies and client results. Ask for anonymized examples of client outcomes. A firm that consistently delivers strong ROI will have data to prove it.
  • Year-round engagement. Tax strategy is not a once-a-year activity. Look for firms that engage with clients quarterly or more frequently, reviewing projections and adjusting plans as circumstances evolve.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a tax advisor typically save a business owner?

Savings vary by income and complexity. Business owners earning $200,000 typically save $30,000 to $50,000 per year. Those earning $500,000 save $75,000 to $150,000, and earners above $1,000,000 often save $150,000 to $300,000 annually through strategic tax planning. These figures represent net savings after advisory fees are deducted.

What is the typical ROI on tax advisory fees?

Most business owners see a 5:1 to 20:1 return on their advisory investment. For every dollar spent on strategic tax planning, clients typically receive $5 to $20 in tax savings. The ROI increases with income and complexity; a $250,000 earner might see a 5x return, while a $1,000,000+ earner often sees 15x or higher.

Is a tax advisor worth it if I already have a CPA?

Yes, in most cases. Traditional CPAs focus on compliance: filing accurate returns on time. A tax strategist works alongside your CPA to implement proactive strategies such as entity restructuring, cost segregation, advanced retirement plans, and amended return recovery. Approximately 80% of our new clients discover $30,000 or more in annual savings that their existing CPA had not identified.

What strategies generate the biggest tax savings?

The highest-impact strategies include S-Corp election (saving $15,000 to $40,000 in self-employment tax), cost segregation studies ($30,000 to $200,000+ in accelerated depreciation), cash balance retirement plans ($50,000 to $150,000 in annual deductions), and amended return recovery (recovering $10,000 to $100,000+ from prior tax years). The specific mix depends on your income sources, entity structure, and investment holdings.

When is a tax advisor NOT worth the investment?

A tax advisor may not provide meaningful ROI if you are a W-2 employee earning under $150,000 with no investments, rental properties, or side business income. In simple situations with standard deductions and no entity planning opportunities, quality tax software or a basic CPA engagement is typically sufficient. The advisory investment becomes worthwhile once your income exceeds $200,000 or you have business, rental, or investment complexity.

How do tax savings compound over time?

Tax savings reinvested at a 7% annual return grow dramatically over time. The real value of tax planning extends far beyond the annual savings number. It is the long-term wealth accumulation those savings enable that makes strategic advisory one of the highest-return investments available to business owners.
