Crypto Tax for Business Owners: S-Corp and C-Corp Strategies

By AE Tax Advisors Team | Published October 3, 2026

How the IRS Classifies Cryptocurrency

Since IRS Notice 2014-21, the federal government has treated cryptocurrency as property for tax purposes. This classification carries enormous consequences for business owners. Unlike foreign currency transactions, which fall under IRC Section 988, every crypto transaction is treated the same way the IRS treats stocks, bonds, and real estate. When you sell, exchange, or otherwise dispose of cryptocurrency, you realize a capital gain or loss equal to the difference between your cost basis and the fair market value at the time of disposal.

For business owners operating through S-Corps, C-Corps, LLCs, or sole proprietorships, this property classification means that accepting Bitcoin as payment for services is not the same as receiving U.S. dollars. The moment your business receives crypto, you must record the fair market value as ordinary business income. If you later sell that crypto at a higher price, the appreciation is a separate capital gain. If the value drops before you convert to dollars, you have a capital loss. Every transaction creates a taxable event that must be tracked, reported, and documented.

This dual nature of crypto income (ordinary income upon receipt, capital gain or loss upon disposal) creates a layer of complexity that many business owners underestimate. A single Bitcoin payment from a client can generate two separate tax consequences occurring months or years apart.

Short-Term vs. Long-Term Capital Gains on Crypto

The holding period for cryptocurrency follows the same rules as other capital assets. If you hold crypto for one year or less before selling, the gain is classified as short-term and taxed at your ordinary income tax rate, which can reach 37% for high-income business owners (plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax, for a combined federal rate of 40.8%).

If you hold crypto for more than one year, the gain qualifies for long-term capital gains treatment at 0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on your taxable income. The 3.8% NIIT may also apply to long-term gains for taxpayers with modified adjusted gross income above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly).

For business owners, the distinction between short-term and long-term holding periods directly influences entity structure decisions. A C-Corp pays a flat 21% tax on all capital gains regardless of holding period, which can be beneficial for short-term trading but creates double taxation concerns when distributing profits to shareholders. An S-Corp passes the character of the gain through to the shareholder's personal return, preserving the favorable long-term rate when applicable.

Reporting Requirements: Forms and Filings

Cryptocurrency reporting obligations have expanded significantly in recent years. Business owners must understand the full scope of required filings to avoid penalties.

Form 8949 and Schedule D

Every sale, exchange, or disposal of cryptocurrency must be reported on Form 8949, which captures the date acquired, date sold, proceeds, cost basis, and resulting gain or loss. These totals flow to Schedule D of your individual return (or the equivalent schedule on corporate returns). Each transaction is categorized as short-term (Part I) or long-term (Part II), and you must indicate whether the cost basis was reported to the IRS by a broker (Box A or D), not reported by a broker (Box B or E), or the form was not received (Box C or F).

Form 1099-DA: The New Digital Asset Reporting Standard

Beginning with tax year 2025 (with full implementation in 2026), cryptocurrency exchanges and brokers are required to issue Form 1099-DA (Digital Asset Proceeds from Broker Transactions). This form reports gross proceeds from crypto sales, and in subsequent years will also include cost basis information. The IRS designed Form 1099-DA to close the information gap that previously allowed many crypto transactions to go unreported. If you receive a 1099-DA, the IRS has a copy, and any discrepancy between your return and the form will likely trigger an automated notice.

International Reporting: FBAR and Form 8938

Business owners who hold cryptocurrency on foreign exchanges face additional reporting requirements. If the aggregate value of your foreign financial accounts (including crypto held on non-U.S. exchanges) exceeds $10,000 at any point during the year, you must file a Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR, FinCEN Form 114). The filing deadline is April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15.

Separately, Form 8938 (Statement of Specified Foreign Financial Assets) applies to taxpayers whose foreign financial assets exceed $50,000 on the last day of the year or $75,000 at any point during the year (higher thresholds apply to married filing jointly and taxpayers living abroad). Failure to file either form carries severe penalties: up to $12,909 per violation for FBAR (adjusted annually for inflation), and $10,000 per form for Form 8938, with willful violations reaching $100,000 or 50% of the account balance.

Accepting Crypto as Payment in Your Business

When your business accepts cryptocurrency as payment for goods or services, the transaction creates ordinary business income equal to the fair market value of the crypto on the date of receipt. This is true regardless of whether you immediately convert the crypto to dollars or hold it. The income must be reported on your business return (Schedule C, Form 1120-S, or Form 1120) just like any other revenue.

For sales tax purposes, most states treat crypto payments the same as cash payments, meaning you must collect and remit sales tax based on the fair market value of the crypto received. This creates a practical challenge: you need a reliable method to determine the exact fair market value at the time of each transaction, which typically means using a major exchange's spot price at the time of receipt.

Many business owners use payment processors like BitPay or Coinbase Commerce, which automatically convert crypto to dollars at the time of the transaction. In this scenario, the conversion price becomes your income amount, and you avoid the complexity of holding crypto on your balance sheet. If you choose to hold crypto after receiving it as payment, your cost basis is the fair market value at the time of receipt, and any subsequent gain or loss is treated as a capital gain or loss when you eventually dispose of it.

Entity Structure: Holding Crypto in S-Corps vs. C-Corps vs. Personally

Personal Holdings

Holding cryptocurrency personally is often the simplest approach for long-term investors. You benefit from long-term capital gains rates (up to 20% plus the 3.8% NIIT) and have full control over the timing of sales. There is no entity-level tax, no corporate formalities, and no risk of reasonable compensation disputes with the IRS. The primary downside is that short-term gains are taxed at your full ordinary income rate.

S-Corp Holdings

An S-Corp is a pass-through entity, meaning income and losses flow through to the shareholder's personal return. For crypto, this preserves the character of capital gains (short-term or long-term) at the individual level. However, S-Corp ownership introduces several complications.

First, the reasonable compensation requirement applies. If you are actively managing crypto investments through your S-Corp, the IRS may argue that a portion of the income should be classified as wages subject to payroll taxes (Social Security and Medicare) rather than distributions. Second, S-Corp shareholders must maintain adequate basis in their stock and any loans to the corporation in order to deduct losses. Large crypto losses that exceed your basis cannot be deducted until you restore basis through additional capital contributions or loans.

Third, the timing of distributions matters. If your S-Corp holds appreciated crypto and distributes it to you rather than selling it first, the distribution is treated as a sale at fair market value, triggering gain recognition at the corporate level that passes through to your personal return. You do not get to defer the gain simply by taking the crypto out of the entity.

C-Corp Holdings

C-Corps pay a flat 21% federal tax on all income, including capital gains. This rate can be attractive for business owners who would otherwise pay 37% on short-term crypto gains personally. However, the double taxation issue is significant: when the C-Corp distributes after-tax profits as dividends, the shareholder pays an additional 15% or 20% (plus the 3.8% NIIT) on qualified dividends. The combined effective rate on short-term gains through a C-Corp can reach approximately 39.8% (21% corporate plus 23.8% on dividends), which is comparable to the top personal rate of 40.8%.

Where C-Corps gain an advantage is in retaining earnings. If your business does not need to distribute profits immediately, the 21% corporate rate defers the second layer of tax. This retained capital can be reinvested into additional crypto or other business assets. However, the accumulated earnings tax under IRC Section 531 imposes a 20% penalty tax on earnings retained beyond the reasonable needs of the business, so this strategy requires careful planning and documentation.

Mining Income: Taxation and Business Deductions

Cryptocurrency mining income is classified as ordinary income, not capital gains, at the time the coins are received. The amount of income equals the fair market value of the mined coins on the date they appear in your wallet. For business owners who mine as part of their operations, this income is reported on the applicable business return and is subject to self-employment tax if you operate as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC.

The good news for miners is that the IRS allows deduction of ordinary and necessary business expenses associated with mining operations. These include electricity costs (often the largest expense), mining hardware depreciation under MACRS, cooling and ventilation systems, facility lease payments, internet service, mining pool fees, and software costs. Under current law, mining hardware qualifies for 100% bonus depreciation in the year placed in service (extended permanently by the OBBBA), allowing you to write off the full cost of GPUs, ASICs, and other equipment immediately rather than depreciating over multiple years.

Running mining operations through an S-Corp can reduce self-employment tax by splitting income between a reasonable salary and S-Corp distributions. The salary portion is subject to the full 15.3% FICA tax (up to the Social Security wage base), while distributions are exempt from self-employment tax. This strategy can save thousands of dollars annually for profitable mining operations.

Staking Rewards and Their Tax Treatment

Staking rewards present a nuanced tax question that the IRS has not fully resolved through formal guidance. Under current interpretation, staking rewards are taxed as ordinary income at the fair market value when received, similar to mining income. This treatment was affirmed in the Jarrett v. United States case, though the case was ultimately dismissed without a definitive ruling on the merits, leaving some ambiguity.

For business owners who stake crypto through their entities, the income is reported on the applicable business return. If staking constitutes a trade or business activity, the income may be subject to self-employment tax for sole proprietors. Staking through an S-Corp or C-Corp avoids self-employment tax but introduces the entity-level considerations discussed above.

The cost basis of staked tokens is the fair market value at the time of receipt. When you later sell staked tokens, the gain or loss is measured from that basis. Business owners should maintain detailed records of the exact date and fair market value of every staking reward received, as exchange-provided records may not capture this information with sufficient precision.

DeFi Transactions: Swaps, Liquidity Pools, and Yield Farming

Decentralized finance (DeFi) creates some of the most complex tax scenarios in the crypto space. Every token swap on a decentralized exchange (such as Uniswap or SushiSwap) is a taxable disposal, even though no fiat currency changes hands. Swapping ETH for USDC, for example, triggers recognition of any gain or loss on the ETH relative to your cost basis.

Providing liquidity to a pool introduces additional complexity. When you deposit tokens into a liquidity pool, you receive LP (liquidity provider) tokens in return. The IRS has not issued definitive guidance on whether this deposit constitutes a taxable exchange. Conservative practitioners treat the deposit as a taxable event, while others argue it is more analogous to a contribution to a partnership or an exchange under IRC Section 1031 (which does not apply to personal property after the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act). Until the IRS provides clarity, business owners should document their position and be prepared to defend it.

Yield farming rewards, governance token airdrops, and protocol incentive distributions are generally treated as ordinary income at the fair market value when received. The challenge lies in record-keeping: DeFi transactions occur on-chain without centralized reporting, meaning no 1099 form is issued. Business owners must use blockchain analytics tools or specialized crypto tax software to reconstruct their transaction history accurately.

NFT Creation, Sales, and Collectible Tax Rates

For business owners who create and sell NFTs, the income is treated as ordinary business income, just like selling any other product or service. The revenue is reported on your business return, and you can deduct the costs of creation (design, minting fees, platform commissions, marketing).

Buyers of NFTs face a different question: are NFTs "collectibles" under IRC Section 408(m)? If so, long-term capital gains on NFTs would be taxed at a maximum rate of 28% rather than the standard 20% long-term rate. In March 2023, the IRS issued Notice 2023-27 requesting comments on this issue and proposed that NFTs representing underlying collectible assets (such as digital art or rare items) would be taxed at the collectible rate. Business owners who purchase and resell NFTs should plan for the 28% rate on long-term gains until the IRS finalizes its position.

Cost Basis Methods: FIFO, LIFO, and Specific Identification

Choosing the right cost basis method can significantly impact your tax liability. The IRS allows several methods for identifying which units of crypto you are selling.

FIFO (First-In, First-Out) assumes you sell the oldest units first. This is the default method and is straightforward to apply, but it often results in larger gains (since older units typically have a lower cost basis due to crypto's long-term appreciation trend).

LIFO (Last-In, First-Out) assumes you sell the most recently acquired units first. This can reduce gains in a rising market because newer units tend to have a higher cost basis closer to the current selling price.

Specific Identification allows you to choose exactly which units you are selling, giving you maximum flexibility to manage your tax liability. To use this method, you must be able to identify the specific units sold (typically through wallet addresses, transaction IDs, or exchange lot records) and maintain documentation showing which units were disposed of in each transaction.

For business owners, specific identification is generally the most powerful method because it allows you to strategically select high-basis units to minimize gains or low-basis units to maximize losses. However, it requires meticulous record-keeping. Starting in 2026, brokers are required to use FIFO as the default method on Form 1099-DA unless the taxpayer specifies an alternative before the sale.

Wash Sale Rules and Tax-Loss Harvesting

One of the most valuable tax planning opportunities for crypto investors in 2026 is tax-loss harvesting, which is enhanced by the current inapplicability of wash sale rules to digital assets.

Under IRC Section 1091, the wash sale rule prevents taxpayers from claiming a loss on a security if they purchase a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale. This rule applies to stocks, bonds, options, and other securities, but because cryptocurrency is classified as property (not a security), the wash sale rule does not currently apply.

This means a business owner can sell Bitcoin at a loss on December 30, claim the capital loss on that year's tax return, and immediately repurchase Bitcoin on December 31 without any waiting period. The loss is fully deductible (subject to the $3,000 annual capital loss limitation against ordinary income for individuals, with excess losses carried forward).

However, business owners should be aware of pending legislative proposals that would extend wash sale rules to digital assets. The Lummis-Gillibrand Responsible Financial Innovation Act and the Digital Asset Market Structure and Investor Protection Act both contain provisions that would subject crypto to the same 30-day wash sale window as securities. If enacted, this change would eliminate the ability to harvest losses and immediately repurchase, fundamentally altering crypto tax planning strategy.

Proactive business owners should consider accelerating their tax-loss harvesting activities while the current rules remain in effect. Consult with your tax advisor to determine the optimal timing and approach for your portfolio.

IRS Enforcement Trends and the Digital Asset Question

The IRS has made cryptocurrency enforcement a top priority, and the trend is toward more aggressive action, not less. Several key enforcement mechanisms are now in place.

John Doe Summonses

The IRS has issued John Doe summonses to major exchanges including Coinbase (2016), Kraken (2021), Circle (2021), and SFOX (2022), compelling these platforms to turn over customer records for account holders meeting certain transaction thresholds. These summonses provide the IRS with names, addresses, Social Security numbers, transaction histories, and account balances, enabling the agency to cross-reference exchange data against filed tax returns.

The Form 1040 Digital Asset Question

Since 2019, every individual tax return has included a question about digital asset activity. For 2026 returns, the question appears on the first page of Form 1040 and asks: "At any time during 2026, did you: (a) receive (as a reward, award, or payment for property or services); or (b) sell, exchange, gift, or otherwise dispose of a digital asset (or a financial interest in a digital asset)?" Answering "No" when you have engaged in crypto transactions constitutes a false statement under penalties of perjury.

Increased Audit Rates

The IRS Criminal Investigation division has established a dedicated Cyber Crimes Unit focused on cryptocurrency tax evasion. With increased funding from the Inflation Reduction Act, the IRS has hired additional agents specializing in blockchain analysis and digital asset forensics. Business owners with significant crypto activity should expect higher audit scrutiny and maintain comprehensive documentation of all transactions.

Common Mistakes Business Owners Make with Crypto Taxes

After advising hundreds of business owners on crypto tax compliance, we consistently see several recurring mistakes that lead to penalties, interest, and unnecessary tax liability.

  1. Failing to track cost basis: Many business owners buy crypto on multiple exchanges over time without recording purchase prices, dates, and quantities. When they eventually sell, they cannot prove their cost basis, resulting in the IRS treating the entire proceeds as gain.
  2. Ignoring crypto-to-crypto trades: Swapping one cryptocurrency for another (such as trading Bitcoin for Ethereum) is a taxable event. Many business owners believe that only conversion to dollars triggers tax liability, which is incorrect.
  3. Not reporting mining and staking income: Income from mining and staking is taxable when received, not when converted to fiat. Failing to report this income is increasingly risky as the IRS expands its on-chain analytics capabilities.
  4. Overlooking DeFi transactions: Yield farming, liquidity provision, and token swaps on decentralized exchanges all create taxable events. The absence of 1099 forms from DeFi protocols does not eliminate reporting obligations.
  5. Misclassifying the entity holding structure: Placing crypto in the wrong entity type can result in unfavorable tax rates, double taxation, or unnecessary self-employment tax. Entity selection should be reviewed with a qualified tax advisor before significant crypto activity begins.
  6. Missing FBAR and Form 8938 filings: Business owners who use foreign exchanges often overlook international reporting requirements. The penalties for non-filing are severe and can exceed the value of the unreported accounts.
  7. Using the wrong cost basis method: Defaulting to FIFO when specific identification would produce a better tax result is a common oversight that can cost thousands of dollars in unnecessary taxes each year.

Strategic Planning: Putting It All Together

Effective crypto tax planning for business owners requires a coordinated approach that considers entity structure, holding period management, cost basis optimization, and compliance with evolving reporting requirements. Here are the key principles we recommend.

First, establish a clear separation between business operations involving crypto (accepting payments, mining, staking) and personal investment holdings. Business operations should flow through the appropriate entity, while long-term investment positions may be better held personally to access favorable capital gains rates.

Second, implement robust record-keeping from day one. Use crypto tax software such as CoinTracker, Koinly, or TokenTax to automatically import transactions from exchanges and wallets, calculate gains and losses, and generate the required tax forms. The cost of this software is a deductible business expense and is far less than the cost of reconstructing transaction history during an audit.

Third, harvest losses strategically while the wash sale exemption remains available. Review your portfolio quarterly, identify positions with unrealized losses, and consider selling to realize those losses for tax purposes. You can immediately repurchase if you want to maintain your position.

Fourth, plan for estimated tax payments. Crypto gains, mining income, and staking rewards are all subject to estimated tax requirements. Failing to make quarterly estimated payments can result in underpayment penalties under IRC Section 6654.

Finally, stay informed about legislative changes. The regulatory environment for cryptocurrency is evolving rapidly, and strategies that work today may not work next year. Work with a tax advisor who specializes in digital assets and actively monitors pending legislation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is cryptocurrency taxed for business owners?

The IRS classifies cryptocurrency as property under Notice 2014-21. For business owners, this means every disposal (sale, exchange, or use as payment) triggers a taxable event. If your business accepts crypto as payment, the fair market value at the time of receipt is recorded as ordinary income. Any subsequent gain or loss when you sell or exchange that crypto is treated as a capital gain or loss, with the holding period determining short-term (ordinary income rates) or long-term (0%, 15%, or 20%) treatment.

Should I hold cryptocurrency in my S-Corp, C-Corp, or personally?

The optimal holding structure depends on your goals. Holding crypto personally preserves access to long-term capital gains rates (0% to 20%). S-Corps pass gains through to your personal return at those same rates, but reasonable compensation requirements and distribution rules add complexity. C-Corps pay a flat 21% corporate tax on gains, which can be advantageous for short-term gains that would otherwise be taxed at 37% personally, but distributions to shareholders trigger double taxation. Many business owners use a hybrid approach: holding long-term investments personally while running crypto-related business operations through an entity.

What forms do I need to report cryptocurrency on my tax return?

Individual taxpayers report crypto disposals on Form 8949 (Sales and Other Dispositions of Capital Assets) and summarize them on Schedule D. If your business accepts crypto as payment, the income appears on Schedule C (sole proprietors), Form 1120-S (S-Corps), or Form 1120 (C-Corps). Mining and staking income is reported as ordinary income on the applicable business return. Starting in 2026, exchanges must issue Form 1099-DA to report digital asset transactions. If you hold crypto on foreign exchanges with aggregate balances exceeding $10,000, you must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114), and Form 8938 may also apply depending on your total foreign financial asset thresholds.

Are wash sale rules applicable to cryptocurrency?

As of 2026, the traditional wash sale rule under IRC Section 1091 does not apply to cryptocurrency because crypto is classified as property, not a security. This means you can sell crypto at a loss and immediately repurchase the same asset to harvest the tax loss without waiting 30 days. However, pending legislation could extend wash sale rules to digital assets. Business owners should monitor the Lummis-Gillibrand Responsible Financial Innovation Act and similar proposals. If enacted, the 30-day wash sale window would apply to crypto, eliminating this planning opportunity.

How is crypto mining income taxed for business owners?

Crypto mining income is treated as ordinary income equal to the fair market value of the coins on the date they are received. If mining is conducted as a business (which it typically is for business owners), the income is subject to self-employment tax (15.3% up to the Social Security wage base, then 2.9% on amounts above). However, business miners can deduct expenses including electricity, hardware depreciation, cooling systems, facility rent, and internet costs. Running mining operations through an S-Corp can reduce self-employment tax exposure by splitting income between reasonable salary and distributions.

What are the penalties for failing to report cryptocurrency transactions?

The IRS has significantly increased crypto enforcement. Penalties for failing to report include accuracy-related penalties of 20% of the underpayment, civil fraud penalties of 75% of the underpayment, and potential criminal prosecution for willful evasion (up to $250,000 in fines and 5 years imprisonment). The IRS has issued John Doe summonses to major exchanges including Coinbase, Kraken, and Circle, obtaining customer records for account holders with transactions exceeding $20,000. Every Form 1040 now includes a digital asset question on the first page, and answering it incorrectly constitutes a false statement under penalty of perjury.
