Nonqualified Deferred Compensation: Elections, Payments and Cash Timing

Employer plan records, Section 409A boundaries, and a worked cash-timing exercise.

Book a Call

Review the plan and the cash flow

Published and reviewed November 22, 2026 · AE Tax Advisors

Nonqualified deferred compensation is a question about an employer's promise to pay compensation later, the plan's election and distribution rules, and the value of receiving money at a different time. A current income-tax deferral is not the same as a permanent tax saving.

Start by identifying the arrangement. Qualified retirement plans and certain other arrangements have separate rules or exclusions. This guide focuses on employer nonqualified deferred compensation and Section 409A. It does not establish that a particular plan qualifies for deferral or that changing an election is permitted. See Section 409A, including its definitions and exclusions.

  • Payment events and specified employees

Confirm the election deadline before making a decision

The general initial-election rule looks to the year before the services are performed. Special rules can apply, including rules for initial eligibility and certain performance-based compensation. Ask the administrator to identify the applicable provision, service period, irrevocability date, and permitted payment form for the actual plan. A calendar-year example does not establish everyone's deadline.

Changing a payment election later involves separate restrictions. A new form or a preferred retirement date does not by itself make a subsequent deferral valid. Review the plan and Treasury Regulation 1.409A-2 before changing the time or form of payment.

Match the payment event to the plan

The permitted payment categories include separation from service, disability, death, a specified time or fixed schedule, a qualifying change in control, and an unforeseeable emergency. Those terms have defined requirements. A personal preference for an earlier payment does not create a qualifying event, and accelerated payments are restricted subject to regulatory exceptions.

The six-month separation-payment delay concerns specified employees of publicly traded corporations under the applicable rules; it is not a general six-month delay for every participant or every payment. Confirm whether that status and payment rule apply. See Treasury Regulation 1.409A-3 on permissible payments.

A plan amendment does not automatically cure a failure

A Section 409A failure can cause affected vested deferred amounts, including prior-year deferrals not already included, to become currently taxable. The statute also provides a 20% additional federal tax and an interest-based additional amount. The affected compensation and participants must be analyzed; this is not simply an extra tax on the latest distribution. Section 409A(a)(1) states the inclusion and additional-tax rules.

Document failures and operational failures require different review. IRS correction guidance has eligibility conditions, timing limits, and reporting requirements. Do not promise that signing an amendment removes prior tax consequences. Notice 2010-6 addresses certain document failures and discusses its relationship to the operational correction guidance in Notice 2008-113.

Separate income-tax timing, payroll tax, and employer risk

An unfunded arrangement can leave the participant exposed to the employer's creditors. Earmarking assets or using a rabbi trust does not automatically make the promise equivalent to a protected personal retirement account. The funding terms matter.

Income-tax and employment-tax timing can differ. NQDC amounts generally enter the FICA analysis at the later of service performance or lapse of a substantial risk of forfeiture, subject to the applicable rules. Confirm what payroll has already reported before assuming distribution-year treatment. The IRS NQDC Audit Technique Guide, Publication 5528, discusses funding, creditor exposure, and employment-tax timing. It is an examination guide, not binding authority.

Reducing excess withholding changes prepayments and current cash flow; it does not by itself reduce the final income-tax liability. Keep withholding adjustments separate from the plan's actual tax treatment.

Fictional example: compare the whole after-tax cash flow

Assume a plan validly permits a $40,000 deferral. For this arithmetic exercise, taking that portion now would incur an assumed 35% incremental income tax: $14,000 tax and $26,000 after-tax cash today. The deferred promise pays exactly $40,000 in five years with no credited earnings. Use a selected 5% annual discount assumption to express future cash in today's dollars.

The model assumes no employer default and leaves out payroll taxes, state taxes, fees, deductions, and other tax interactions. The three future tax rates are hypothetical assumptions, not actual brackets, forecasts, or client results. The model does not decide whether a real election is available or suitable.

For the 32% row, the nominal income-tax difference is $14,000 − $12,800 = $1,200. The cash arrives five years later: $27,200 ÷ 1.055 = $21,311.91. Comparing that present value with $26,000 now gives −$4,688.09. A smaller nominal tax amount therefore does not establish that the delayed payment has greater value under these assumptions.

The other rows illustrate the same calculation with a different future tax assumption. Actual credited earnings, payment schedules, taxes, liquidity needs, employer credit exposure, and discount assumptions can change the comparison. Do not call the $14,000 current deferral a permanent saving or evaluate only the discounted future tax while ignoring the compensation received later.

Build a record of the actual plan

Download the blank plan-review record (CSV). Use it to organize questions; do not treat a completed sheet as a compliance opinion.

  • Plan and amendments, employer, participant classification, and responsible administrator.
  • Compensation service year, eligibility date, election date, irrevocability deadline, and the provision supporting that deadline.
  • Vesting terms, payment event or schedule, permitted changes, and any specified-employee delay analysis.
  • Credited earnings, funding arrangement, employer credit exposure, and distribution records.
  • Payroll and income-tax reporting already completed, state questions, and any suspected document or operational failure.
  • Named owner, missing evidence, and next decision before an election, amendment, or payment.

A plan review should connect the employer documents, payroll treatment, participant return, and payment records. An S-corp owner-wage analysis answers a different compensation question; see the reasonable-compensation service scope for that assignment.
