Disregarded Entity: Definition, Taxes and Single-Member LLC Rules

Published on September 4, 2026 | Entity Structuring | AE Tax Advisors

What is a disregarded entity for tax purposes?

A disregarded entity is not treated as separate from its owner for federal income tax. A domestic single-member LLC commonly has this default status unless it elects corporate treatment. State-law existence continues.

A disregarded entity is a legal entity that exists under state law but is not treated as separate from its owner for federal income tax purposes. The most common example is a domestic single-member LLC that has not elected corporate tax treatment. Its income and deductions are reported on the owner's return, even though the LLC remains legally separate under state law.

The Default Classification

Under the federal entity-classification rules, a domestic single-member LLC generally defaults to disregarded status unless it elects corporate treatment. The LLC remains a state-law entity, while its income tax items are generally reported by its owner. The owner may be an individual or another entity, so the destination return depends on who owns the LLC.

For a U.S. individual owner, an active sole-proprietor business is commonly reported on Schedule C and rental real estate is commonly reported on Schedule E, subject to the facts and applicable exceptions. A corporation or partnership owner generally includes the LLC activity on its own return. Disregarded status does not by itself create a separate federal income tax return for the LLC; other filings may still be required.

What "Disregarded" Does Not Mean

Being disregarded for income tax purposes does not mean the entity is ignored for all federal tax purposes. This is a critical distinction that trips up many business owners and even some preparers. A disregarded entity is treated as a separate entity for employment tax and certain excise tax purposes under Treasury Regulation 301.7701-2(c)(2)(iv).

This means a single-member LLC with employees must obtain its own EIN and file employment tax returns, Forms 941, 940, and W-2, under the LLC's name and EIN, not the owner's. The LLC reports and pays its employment taxes under its own name and EIN; certain excise tax obligations also use separate entity treatment. Ownership and liability questions require facts beyond the income tax classification, so do not assume that the classification alone allocates all tax or legal liability.

The IRS confirms these distinctions in its guidance for single-member limited liability companies.

Where the Income Appears: Two Owner Examples

Example A, individual owner: A single-member LLC runs a consulting business and has made no corporate election. Its owner generally includes the business income and expenses on Schedule C of Form 1040. If the LLC has employees, it uses its own name and EIN for federal payroll filings. An LLC wrapper alone does not remove self-employment tax on the owner's business earnings.

Example B, entity owner: A corporation owns a single-member property LLC that has made no corporate election. The property activity generally flows into the corporate owner's federal income tax return rather than a separate corporate return for the LLC. The property's rental or operating treatment still depends on its actual use and services. See the IRS single-member LLC guide.

State Tax Treatment Varies

While the federal government disregards single-member LLCs for income tax purposes, states do not uniformly follow this treatment. Some states impose LLC fees, franchise taxes or filing duties even when federal income tax is reported by the owner. State rules and thresholds change, so check each state where the LLC operates or is registered before using the federal classification as a filing checklist.

When a Disregarded Entity Stops Being Disregarded

Adding another member generally changes the LLC's default federal classification to a partnership, unless a corporate election applies. The transaction and the members' status determine the tax consequences and filing obligations; review the transfer before assuming that only an additional Form 1065 is needed.

Conversely, when a multi-member LLC loses all but one member, it converts from a partnership to a disregarded entity. The tax consequences depend on how the interest changes hands and the partnership's assets and liabilities. Review the transaction under the relevant partnership rules before updating returns and basis schedules.

A disregarded entity can also elect to be treated as a corporation by filing Form 8832 (Entity Classification Election). Once the corporate election is made, the entity files its own corporate tax return and is no longer disregarded. An eligible LLC may instead make an S corporation election using Form 2553 under the applicable rules. Payroll, reasonable compensation, state treatment and the cost of maintaining the election all matter. See entity structuring services.

Liability Protection Remains

A disregarded LLC remains a state-law entity, with liability treatment governed by that law. The scope of liability protection depends on state law, how the entity is operated and the specific claim. Federal income tax classification does not itself decide whether a creditor can reach an asset; obtain legal advice for those questions.

Practical Considerations

Business owners operating through disregarded entities should maintain separate bank accounts, keep thorough records of entity-level transactions, and execute contracts in the LLC's name rather than their personal name. These practices reinforce the legal separateness of the entity and protect the liability shield. Keep the ownership and tax reporting records consistent, and ask local counsel about state-specific liability requirements.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a single-member LLC file its own federal income tax return?

Generally no if it is disregarded: its income tax items appear on its owner’s return. Payroll, certain excise and other information filings may still apply.

Is a disregarded LLC the same as a sole proprietorship?

For an individual owner, income tax reporting can resemble a sole proprietorship, but the LLC remains a separate legal entity under state law and may have separate employment or excise tax obligations.

Does a disregarded LLC need its own EIN?

An LLC with employees generally needs its own EIN for employment tax filings. Other EIN needs depend on the circumstances; confirm the applicable IRS requirements.

What changes if I add a second LLC member?

The default federal classification generally changes to a partnership unless a corporate election applies. Review the transaction, tax basis and new filing obligations before the ownership change.
