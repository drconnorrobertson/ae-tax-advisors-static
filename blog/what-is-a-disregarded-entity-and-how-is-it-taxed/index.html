What Is a Disregarded Entity and How Is It Taxed?

The term "disregarded entity" is one of the most important concepts in business taxation, yet it is widely misunderstood. A disregarded entity is a legal entity that exists under state law but is not recognized as a separate taxpayer by the IRS for federal income tax purposes. Understanding how disregarded entities work, and when they stop being disregarded, is essential for any business owner operating through an LLC.

The Default Classification

Under the check-the-box regulations in Treasury Regulation 301.7701-3, a domestic entity with a single owner that does not elect corporate treatment is classified as a disregarded entity. The most common example is a single-member LLC. The LLC exists as a separate legal entity under state law, providing its owner with limited liability protection, but for federal tax purposes, the IRS treats the LLC as if it does not exist, the owner reports all income and expenses directly on their personal tax return.

This means a single-member LLC does not file its own Form 1120, 1120-S, or 1065. If the SMLLC operates a trade or business, the owner reports income and expenses on Schedule C of Form 1040. If it holds rental property, the activity is reported on Schedule E. If another entity owns the SMLLC, the disregarded entity's activities are reported on the parent entity's return.

What "Disregarded" Does Not Mean

Being disregarded for income tax purposes does not mean the entity is ignored for all federal tax purposes. This is a critical distinction that trips up many business owners and even some preparers. A disregarded entity is treated as a separate entity for employment tax and certain excise tax purposes under Treasury Regulation 301.7701-2(c)(2)(iv).

This means a single-member LLC with employees must obtain its own EIN and file employment tax returns, Forms 941, 940, and W-2, under the LLC's name and EIN, not the owner's. The LLC is responsible for withholding and remitting payroll taxes, and the employment tax liability belongs to the LLC as the employer of record. Failing to maintain this distinction can create personal liability for the owner. For excise tax purposes, the disregarded entity is similarly treated as separate, those obligations belong to the LLC, not the owner.

State Tax Treatment Varies

While the federal government disregards single-member LLCs for income tax purposes, states do not uniformly follow this treatment. Some states impose entity-level taxes on LLCs regardless of their federal classification. California's $800 annual franchise tax applies to every LLC registered in the state, including disregarded entities. Texas imposes its franchise (margin) tax on LLCs with revenue above the threshold. These state-level taxes apply to the entity itself, not to the owner, creating an entity-level obligation that exists despite the federal disregarded status.

When a Disregarded Entity Stops Being Disregarded

A disregarded entity's classification changes automatically when a second member is added. The moment a single-member LLC admits a second member, it converts from a disregarded entity to a partnership for federal tax purposes. This conversion is treated as a deemed contribution of assets to a newly formed partnership under Revenue Ruling 99-5, and the entity must begin filing Form 1065 and issuing K-1s to the members.

Conversely, when a multi-member LLC loses all but one member, it converts from a partnership to a disregarded entity. This transition is treated as a deemed distribution of all partnership assets to the remaining member under Revenue Ruling 99-6, followed by a deemed contribution of those assets to a new disregarded entity.

A disregarded entity can also elect to be treated as a corporation by filing Form 8832 (Entity Classification Election). Once the corporate election is made, the entity files its own corporate tax return and is no longer disregarded. This election can be combined with Form 2553 to achieve S corporation status, which is a common strategy for single-member LLC owners seeking to reduce self-employment tax.

Liability Protection Remains

Regardless of tax classification, the LLC's state-law liability protection remains intact. A disregarded entity is still a separate legal entity for purposes of contract law, tort liability, and asset protection. The owner's personal assets are generally protected from business liabilities, and the business assets are generally protected from the owner's personal creditors. The disregarded entity status affects only how the IRS treats the entity for income tax reporting, it does not diminish the legal separateness of the LLC.

Practical Considerations

Business owners operating through disregarded entities should maintain separate bank accounts, keep thorough records of entity-level transactions, and execute contracts in the LLC's name rather than their personal name. These practices reinforce the legal separateness of the entity and protect the liability shield. State courts evaluating veil-piercing claims will look at whether the owner treated the LLC as a truly separate entity in practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I change my entity structure later?

Often, but not always cheaply. Converting an LLC to S-Corp taxation is straightforward, while unwinding a C corporation or removing appreciated real estate from one can be expensive. The cost of reversing a structure should be weighed before adopting it.

Which entity structure is right for my business?

It depends on profit level, asset type, ownership, and exit plans. An LLC taxed as a partnership suits real estate and businesses needing special allocations; an S election suits profitable operating businesses above roughly $80,000 of profit per owner; a C corporation suits capital-intensive growth aiming at a Section 1202 exit.

What is a pass-through entity tax election?

It allows a partnership or S corporation to pay state income tax at the entity level rather than passing it to owners. The entity-level tax is deductible federally and is not subject to the individual state and local tax cap, restoring a deduction that would otherwise be lost.

Should real estate be held in a separate entity from operations?

Usually yes. It isolates liability, creates a clean platform for depreciation and cost segregation, and produces rent taxed once without payroll tax. The tradeoff is the self-rental rule of Reg. 1.469-2(f)(6), which is generally addressed with a grouping election under Reg. 1.469-4.
