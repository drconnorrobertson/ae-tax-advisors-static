Why Can't I Deduct My Rental Property Losses?

A rental property can show a legitimate tax loss and still produce no current deduction. That usually means the loss was limited at one of three gates: tax basis, amount at risk, or the passive activity loss rules. Most long-term rentals are passive by default, so the usable loss may stop on Form 8582 instead of reducing W-2 wages or business income this year.

The loss is generally carried forward rather than erased. Whether you can use it now depends on the source of the loss, ownership and financing, passive income elsewhere on the return, modified adjusted gross income, participation, the rental period and services provided, and whether you sold your entire interest in a taxable transaction. The order matters: qualifying for a passive-loss exception does not repair a basis or at-risk limitation.

Why Can't I Deduct My Rental Property Losses? Start With the Return Sequence

Do not begin with a strategy label. Begin with the return. The right question is: at which gate did the loss stop? Review the limitation sequence before deciding that real estate professional status, a cost segregation study, or an amendment is the answer.

GateQuestion to answerWhere to lookWhat happens if limited
1. Economic and tax resultIs there actually a rental loss after capitalization, personal-use allocation, and depreciation?Schedule E, depreciation schedule, repairs and closing recordsThe loss may be smaller than bookkeeping reports suggest.
2. BasisDo you have enough adjusted basis in the property or pass-through interest?Fixed-asset ledger, partner/shareholder basis schedule, K-1 footnotesThe excess carries forward under the applicable basis rule.
3. At riskHow much could you actually lose, considering debt and guarantees?Form 6198, loan documents, guarantees, capital recordsThe disallowed amount carries forward for that activity.
4. Passive activityIs the activity passive, and is there passive income or an exception?Form 8582, Schedule E, participation records, prior-year returnsThe loss becomes a prior-year unallowed passive loss.
5. Other individual limitsDoes another return-level rule apply after the first four gates?Complete Form 1040 and supporting schedulesThe timing or amount of the deduction may be limited again.

The IRS Publication 925 ordering rule confirms that basis and at-risk limits are applied before the passive activity loss limit. That is why a return review must reconcile all three; reviewing only Form 8582 can miss the actual cause.

The General Rule: Passive Losses Cannot Offset Active Income

Under IRC Section 469(a), losses from passive activities, which include most rental real estate, generally cannot be used to offset nonpassive income such as W-2 wages or a business in which you materially participate. Portfolio income such as interest and dividends generally does not count as passive activity income either. Instead, passive losses are suspended and carried forward to future years, where they can offset passive income or may be released after a qualifying disposition.

This rule exists because Congress wanted to prevent high-income taxpayers from using paper losses from real estate to shelter their earned income. However, there are two major exceptions that allow many investors to deduct rental losses against their W-2 income.

Exception 1: The $25,000 Special Allowance

Under IRC Section 469(i), taxpayers who actively participate in rental real estate activities can deduct up to $25,000 in rental losses against non-passive income each year. Active participation is a lower threshold than material participation, it essentially means you make management decisions such as approving tenants, setting rental terms, and authorizing repairs. Most hands-on landlords meet this standard easily.

However, this allowance generally phases out for taxpayers with modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) between $100,000 and $150,000. For every two dollars of MAGI above $100,000, the maximum allowance is reduced by one dollar. At $150,000 MAGI, the general allowance is eliminated. Special filing-status rules apply: a married taxpayer filing separately who lived with a spouse at any time during the year generally receives no allowance, while a qualifying spouse who lived apart all year may be subject to half-sized thresholds and limits. Limited partners also do not meet active participation merely by holding the interest.

Worked example: the allowance is not always $25,000

Assume a married couple filing jointly actively participates in a long-term rental, has a $30,000 rental loss, no other passive income, and $120,000 of MAGI for this purpose. Their preliminary $25,000 special allowance is reduced by $10,000—50% of the $20,000 excess over $100,000—leaving a maximum allowance of $15,000. Subject to basis and at-risk limits, $15,000 may offset nonpassive income and the remaining $15,000 becomes a passive-loss carryforward. This is a simplified federal example; the precise MAGI calculation and state treatment must be checked on the actual return.

Four Paths That Can Make a Rental Loss Usable

1. Passive income elsewhere on the return

A passive loss does not need to offset wages to be valuable. It may offset passive income from another rental or pass-through activity, subject to activity-specific rules. Interest, dividends, wages, and income from a business in which you materially participate are not substitutes for passive income. Publicly traded partnership losses also stay in their own separate basket and generally cannot be netted freely with other passive activities.

2. The active-participation special allowance

The allowance described above may let a qualifying individual use up to $25,000 against nonpassive income before the MAGI phaseout. Active participation is a lower standard than material participation, but ownership, management decisions, filing status, income, and how the interest is held all matter. A property manager does not automatically defeat active participation if the owner still makes meaningful management decisions.

3. Real estate professional status plus material participation

Real estate professional status removes the rule that rental real estate is automatically passive, but it does not by itself make every rental loss nonpassive. The taxpayer must also materially participate in the relevant rental activity. If spouses file jointly, one spouse can satisfy the more-than-750-hour and more-than-half-of-personal-services real estate professional tests; both spouses' participation can count when testing material participation. Investor-type work and hours performed as an employee may be excluded under the rules.

A qualifying taxpayer with multiple rentals may consider an election to treat rental real estate interests as one activity. That election is not an automatic recommendation. It can change whether material participation is achievable and how a future sale releases former passive losses. Model the current-year and exit consequences before attaching an election statement.

4. A rental that is not a “rental activity” for Section 469

Certain average-customer-use and service patterns can cause an activity not to be treated as a rental activity under the passive rules. A short average stay is not enough by itself to create a wage-offset deduction: the activity must then be a trade or business and the taxpayer must materially participate. Personal use, mixed-use vacation-home limits, and how services are delivered can change the analysis. See AE's focused guide to material participation and the short-term-rental rules before applying an STR conclusion to a long-term rental.

Exception 2: Real Estate Professional Status (REPS)

The more powerful exception is qualifying as a Real Estate Professional under IRC Section 469(c)(7). If you meet the REPS requirements and materially participate in the rental activity, that activity is not passive. Its otherwise allowable loss can generally offset W-2 income or other nonpassive income, but basis, at-risk, excess business loss, and other return-level limits can still apply.

To qualify for REPS, you must satisfy two tests. First, more than 50% of the personal services you perform during the tax year must be in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate. Second, you must perform more than 750 hours of services in real property trades or businesses during the year. Additionally, you must materially participate in each rental activity, or make a grouping election under Treas. Reg. Section 1.469-9(g) to treat all rentals as a single activity.

The Impact of Cost Segregation

For investors whose losses can be used, combining that classification with a defensible cost segregation study can accelerate depreciation. A study may reclassify qualifying building components into shorter recovery periods, often 5, 7, and 15 years instead of 27.5 or 39 years. Bonus-depreciation eligibility depends on the asset, acquisition and placed-in-service dates, and current law. The study creates timing deductions—not permission to cross the basis, at-risk, or passive-loss gates. Read when cost segregation actually works before treating a projected deduction as current tax savings.

What Happens to the Loss If You Cannot Use It Now?

A passive loss disallowed this year is generally carried forward on Form 8582. It may become usable against future passive income, under the special allowance in a later qualifying year, or when the disposition rules permit release. Keep the carryforward by activity and reconcile it to each filed return; the number should not live only in tax software.

Worked example: passive income uses the loss before a sale

An investor has a $42,000 prior-year unallowed loss from Rental A. In the current year, Rental A produces $8,000 of income and another passive activity produces $12,000 of income. Assuming no separate basket or other limitation changes the result, up to $20,000 of the passive loss may be absorbed, leaving $22,000 carried forward. The exact allocation is completed through Form 8582.

Worked example: selling one property does not always release everything

An investor sells all of a direct interest in Rental A to an unrelated buyer in a fully taxable sale. Subject to the detailed ordering rules, the activity's remaining suspended passive loss may be deductible after being applied against gain or net income. A partial sale, installment structure, related-party transfer, gift, transfer at death, or sale of one property inside a grouped activity can produce a different answer. Review the suspended-loss schedule before closing so the sale reporting and carryforward do not contradict each other.

The IRS summarizes the general disposition rule in Topic 425. For a return that already contains an incorrect carryforward, use AE's passive-loss carryforward correction guide to determine whether the issue belongs on an amended return, a current-year return, or a broader method review.

Documents to Gather Before a Rental Loss Review

  • The current return and at least two prior-year federal and state returns, including every Schedule E, Form 8582, Form 6198, Form 4562, and K-1.
  • The depreciation schedule and fixed-asset ledger for each property, including land allocation, improvements, dispositions, and any cost segregation report.
  • Closing statements, refinance documents, loan agreements, guarantees, capital contribution records, and ownership changes.
  • A property-by-property schedule of current and prior-year unallowed losses; do not rely on one portfolio-level total.
  • Rental agreements, booking reports, average-stay data, and records of services provided if a short-term-rental exception is being considered.
  • Contemporaneous participation records, calendars, emails, invoices, and property-management communications if material participation or real estate professional status matters.
  • Sale, exchange, casualty, gift, or conversion documents for any property that left the portfolio.

Common Reasons a Return Gets the Rental Loss Wrong

  • Starting with Form 8582 but ignoring basis or at-risk limits. A passive-loss exception cannot free a loss that was already stopped earlier.
  • Calling all Schedule E income “passive income.” Self-rental recharacterization, publicly traded partnership rules, and other classifications can prevent the expected netting.
  • Assuming active participation means real estate professional status. They are different standards with different consequences.
  • Claiming real estate professional status without testing material participation. Both steps matter.
  • Counting investor work or unsupported estimates as participation hours. The character, timing, and evidence for the work matter.
  • Treating every short-term rental as nonpassive. The average-use and service facts determine whether the activity escapes rental classification; material participation must still be tested.
  • Dropping carryforwards during a preparer or software change. Compare the prior Form 8582 worksheets to the new return activity by activity.
  • Releasing losses after the wrong kind of transfer. A gift, related-party sale, partial disposition, or grouped-activity sale can differ from a fully taxable disposition of the entire interest to an unrelated party.

Documentation and Compliance

The IRS scrutinizes rental loss deductions aggressively, particularly for REPS claims. Maintain a contemporaneous time log documenting all hours spent on real estate activities, including the date, activity performed, property involved, and time spent. The Tax Court has denied REPS status in numerous cases, such as Truskowsky v. Commissioner, where taxpayers could not produce adequate records. Your CPA should review your qualification annually and ensure proper reporting on Schedule E and Form 8582.

If you earn W-2 income and own rental properties, the useful output is not a generic yes or no. It is a reconciliation showing the loss created, the amount allowed at each gate, the activity classification, the current deduction, and the carryforward that should appear next year. Frequently Asked Questions

Why can't I deduct my rental property losses?

A rental loss can be real but not currently usable. The return applies basis limits, at-risk limits, and then the passive activity loss rules. Most long-term rentals are passive, so a loss that exceeds passive income is generally suspended unless the active-participation special allowance, real estate professional rules, or another exception applies.

What are the requirements for real estate professional status?

Two tests must be met in the same year under IRC Section 469(c)(7): more than half of all personal services performed in all trades or businesses must be in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate, and you must perform more than 750 hours in those businesses.

What happens to suspended passive losses?

They carry forward indefinitely and become deductible when the activity produces income, when other passive income is available, or when the activity's character changes. They are also released in full on a qualifying disposition of the entire interest.

What documentation does the IRS accept for hours?

The regulation allows any reasonable means, but contemporaneous dated logs with specific task descriptions, corroborated by emails, invoices, calendars, and receipts, are what survive. Reconstructed summaries prepared after an examination begins are consistently given little weight by the Tax Court.

Do I need a real estate professional grouping election?

It depends on the facts. A qualifying real estate professional may elect under Treasury Regulation 1.469-9(g) to treat all rental real estate interests as one activity for material-participation purposes. The election can help in some portfolios but can also affect suspended-loss and disposition results, so it should be modeled before filing.
