MACRS Depreciation Tables: 5, 7, 15, 27.5 and 39 Years

Published August 13, 2026 | Depreciation | AE Tax Advisors

MACRS, the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System, is the depreciation method required for nearly all tangible business and rental property placed in service after 1986. It assigns every asset a recovery period, a depreciation method, and a convention that determines how much of the first year you get. Those three inputs produce the percentage tables in IRS Publication 946 that drive the number on your Form 4562.

The tables below reproduce the most-used half-year MACRS percentages and summarize the residential and nonresidential real-property schedules. For the full tables, exceptions, and worksheets, use the current IRS Publication 946.

The Three Inputs That Determine Your Deduction

Every MACRS calculation comes down to the same three decisions, made once when the asset is placed in service:

  1. Recovery period. How many years the asset is written off over, determined by asset class under Rev. Proc. 87-56.
  2. Method. 200 percent declining balance, 150 percent declining balance, or straight line. Declining-balance methods switch to straight line when it gives an equal or larger deduction.
  3. Convention. Half year, mid quarter, or mid month, which fixes how much of the first and last year you are allowed to claim.

These are common GDS classifications, not an exhaustive asset-class determination. The half-year entries assume the mid-quarter test does not apply. Industry-specific classes, required ADS, elections and statutory exceptions can change the method or life.

For a fictional residential rental building with $1,000,000 of depreciable basis and December service, the displayed table rate gives approximately $1,520 for that year; January service gives approximately $34,850. These are separate assumed service dates, not alternative deductions to choose after the fact. Land is excluded. Closing alone does not establish that a property is ready and available for rental use. Separately classified components need their own method, convention and bonus-eligibility review; a fourth-quarter purchase does not by itself establish a larger deduction.

Conventions: Half Year, Mid Quarter, Mid Month

Half year applies when neither mid-month nor mid-quarter applies. Under this convention, covered property is treated as placed in service or disposed of at the midpoint of the tax year.

Mid quarter generally applies when the tested depreciable basis placed in service in the final three months exceeds 40% of the full-year tested basis. Exclude residential rental and nonresidential real property, railroad grading or tunnel bores, same-year placements and dispositions, and property depreciated outside MACRS. Reduce tested basis for Section 179 and personal use, not for bonus depreciation. If triggered, it applies to covered additions throughout the year, not just final-quarter purchases. Use the table for each asset’s service quarter.

Mid month applies to residential rental property, nonresidential real property, and railroad grading or tunnel bores. It does not apply to every asset described casually as real estate: a separately classified land improvement needs its own convention review.

Worked convention test: the building does not dilute the equipment ratio

This is an original fictional illustration, not a client result. Assume a calendar-year taxpayer has $60,000 of tested MACRS basis placed in service in the first nine months and $40,000 in the final three months. These figures already reflect any Section 179 and personal-use reductions, with no same-year disposals or other exclusions. The final-quarter ratio is $40,000 ÷ $100,000 = 40%; exactly 40% does not exceed the threshold. If a separate $1,000 covered addition is placed in service in December, the ratio becomes $41,000 ÷ $101,000 ≈ 40.59%, triggering mid-quarter for covered additions. A $900,000 residential rental building is excluded from both versions of the test and retains its own mid-month convention. A bonus deduction does not reduce this test’s basis.

When You Must Use ADS Instead

The Alternative Depreciation System uses straight line over longer recovery periods and is required in several situations:

  • Listed property used 50 percent or less in a qualified business use
  • Tax exempt use property and tax exempt bond financed property
  • Property used predominantly outside the United States
  • Electing real property trades or businesses that opted out of the Section 163(j) business interest limitation

The real-property trade or business election under Section 163(j) requires ADS for specified residential rental, nonresidential real property and QIP; it does not move every asset into ADS or guarantee that all interest is deductible. Common ADS lives are 30, 40 and 20 years respectively; older residential property requires its own transition review. Property required to use ADS is not eligible for bonus depreciation, including QIP required to use ADS. A recovery period of 20 years alone does not establish eligibility. Model both paths before electing.

Where MACRS Shows Up on the Return

Depreciation is reported on Form 4562, then flows to Schedule E for rentals, Schedule C for sole proprietors, or the relevant business return. Assets placed in service in the current year are listed by class in Part III. Prior year assets are reported in aggregate on line 17. Listed property, including vehicles, goes in Part V with its own substantiation requirements.

Fixing a Depreciation Error

The correction route depends on the error, return history, elections, deadlines and whether an accounting method was adopted. Consistent use of an impermissible depreciation method can require an accounting-method change, often Form 3115 with a Section 481(a) adjustment. Mathematical or posting errors, basis facts and certain elections are not resolved by a universal “two years means Form 3115” rule. Review Publication 946’s correction instructions before choosing an amended return or method change. See recovering missed depreciation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does MACRS stand for?

MACRS stands for the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System. It is the depreciation system required under IRC Section 168 for most tangible property placed in service after 1986. MACRS assigns each asset a recovery period, a depreciation method, and a convention, which together determine the annual deduction.

What is the MACRS depreciation rate for 5 year property?

Under the half year convention and 200 percent declining balance method, 5 year property is depreciated at 20.00 percent in year one, 32.00 percent in year two, 19.20 percent in year three, 11.52 percent in years four and five, and 5.76 percent in year six. The percentages apply to unadjusted depreciable basis after Section 179, bonus and other applicable reductions, without subtracting prior regular MACRS deductions. Under these assumptions, with no early disposition, the schedule spans six tax years.

Why does 5 year property take six years to depreciate?

When the half-year convention applies, it treats covered property as placed in service at the midpoint of the tax year. Under the displayed assumptions, five-year property spans six tax years, seven-year property eight, and 15-year property sixteen. Mid-quarter, short-year and disposition rules can change the amounts.

What is the difference between MACRS and ADS?

MACRS includes GDS and ADS. GDS generally permits accelerated methods; ADS uses straight-line methods and often longer recovery periods. Mandatory ADS can apply to listed property used 50 percent or less in qualified business use, specified tax-exempt or foreign-use property, and specified real-property categories held by an electing real property trade or business. The election does not move every asset into ADS.

When does the mid quarter convention apply?

It generally applies when final-three-month tested basis exceeds 40% of the full-year tested basis. Exclude specified buildings, railroad grading or tunnel bores, same-year placements and dispositions, and non-MACRS property. Reduce basis for Section 179 and personal use, not bonus. The convention then applies to covered additions throughout the year; exactly 40% does not trigger it.

What depreciation life applies to a residential rental property?

Under the usual GDS treatment, a residential rental building uses 27.5-year straight-line depreciation and mid-month; nonresidential real property generally uses 39 years. Required ADS and older property can differ. Land is not depreciable. Separately classified components need supported lives and methods; QIP generally uses 15-year straight line under GDS, not the 15-year declining-balance column above.

Worked example: $10,000 of five-year property

This is an original fictional illustration, not an actual client deduction. A $10,000 purchase used 80% for business starts with $8,000 of business-use basis. Assume a valid $3,000 Section 179 deduction, no bonus deduction, no other basis adjustments and eligibility for the displayed five-year half-year schedule. Regular MACRS basis is $8,000 − $3,000 = $5,000. Year-one regular depreciation is $5,000 × 20% = $1,000, and year two is $5,000 × 32% = $1,600. First-year Section 179 plus regular depreciation totals $4,000 under those assumptions; that is a deduction illustration, not $4,000 of tax savings. The remaining paragraph isolates a different case with no basis reductions.

Assume $10,000 of depreciable basis, GDS, 200% declining balance, half-year convention, no Section 179 and no bonus deduction. Year one is $10,000 × 20% = $2,000; year two is $10,000 × 32% = $3,200. Apply each table percentage to the original depreciable basis, not the prior year’s remaining balance. If bonus or Section 179 reduces basis first, this example no longer describes that asset’s schedule.

Check the convention and asset class before choosing a table. The table is not an election to use a shorter life.
