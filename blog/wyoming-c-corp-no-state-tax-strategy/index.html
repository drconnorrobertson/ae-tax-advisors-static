The Wyoming C-Corp: When the No-State-Tax Strategy Actually Works

Published on May 7, 2026 | Entity Structuring and Planning | AE Tax Advisors

A C corporation is a separate taxpayer subject to a flat 21% federal rate under IRC Section 11. Income retained in the corporation is taxed once at that rate, while distributions to shareholders as dividends add a second layer of tax, which is why the structure suits businesses that reinvest rather than distribute.

Wyoming imposes no corporate income tax, no personal income tax, no gross receipts tax on most activity, and no franchise tax measured by income. Filing fees are modest and the annual report requirement is light.

That combination has made Wyoming a popular incorporation state, and it has also produced a great deal of bad advice. The core misunderstanding is simple: where you incorporate does not determine where you are taxed.

How States Actually Tax Corporations

A state's authority to tax a corporation rests on nexus, the connection between the business and the state. Nexus is created by physical presence, employees, property, inventory, and, since South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc., 585 U.S. 162 (2018), by economic activity exceeding statutory thresholds even without physical presence.

Once nexus exists, the state applies apportionment, dividing income among states based on factors such as sales, payroll, and property. Most states now use single-sales-factor apportionment, sourcing income to where customers are.

Nothing in that framework references the state of incorporation.

So an owner living in California, working from a California home office, serving California customers, who forms a Wyoming C-Corporation, has a California-taxable corporation. California will assess corporate income tax on apportioned income, will require foreign qualification as an out-of-state corporation doing business in California, and will impose its minimum franchise tax. The Wyoming certificate adds a registered agent fee and an annual report to the pile.

See Wyoming vs. Delaware.

When It Genuinely Works

Four situations where a Wyoming C-Corp produces real savings.

1. You Actually Move

The most reliable version. If you relocate to Wyoming and establish genuine domicile, your personal income has no state tax and a corporation operated from Wyoming with Wyoming-sourced activity has no state corporate tax.

Domicile is a facts-and-circumstances question, and high-tax states audit departures aggressively. The evidence that matters is where you actually live: days present, home ownership or lease, driver's license, voter registration, vehicle registration, physician and dentist, banking, and where your family lives. Several states apply a statutory day-count test in addition to common-law domicile.

A corporation formed in Wyoming while the owner remains in a taxing state does not accomplish this. Only the move does.

2. Holding Company With No Operations

A Wyoming corporation that holds stock of subsidiaries, or investment assets, and conducts no business activity in another state generally has no nexus elsewhere.

The subsidiaries are taxed where they operate, but income at the holding level, such as intercompany dividends, may escape state taxation. Note that many states impose combined or unitary reporting, which can pull a holding company into the group return regardless of its own nexus. This works in some state regimes and not in others, and requires state-specific analysis rather than a general rule.

See whether to use a holding company.

3. Wyoming-Situs Assets

Real property, mineral interests, or equipment physically located in Wyoming and generating Wyoming-sourced income. Income from real property is sourced to where the property sits, so a Wyoming corporation holding Wyoming property has genuinely untaxed state income.

4. Properly Structured IP Holding

A Wyoming corporation owns trademarks or software and licenses them to operating entities elsewhere. Historically this was a common state tax planning technique.

It has become considerably harder. Many states enacted addback statutes disallowing deductions for intangible expenses paid to related parties, economic nexus rules reach licensors whose IP is used in-state, and combined reporting regimes eliminate the separation. It still works in certain multistate footprints, but it is a specialist structure requiring genuine substance, arm's-length royalty pricing supportable under IRC Sec. 482, and state-by-state analysis. See C-Corp income shifting.

The Federal Layer Does Not Change

Worth stating plainly, because it gets lost: Wyoming affects state tax only. The corporation pays the same flat 21% federal rate under IRC Sec. 11(b) that a corporation formed anywhere else pays. Dividends carry the same federal treatment. Every federal strategy, the closely held passive loss rule under IRC Sec. 469(e)(2), a MERP under Sec. 105(b), accumulated earnings exposure under Sec. 531, applies identically.

If someone is selling a Wyoming entity as a federal tax strategy, that is a signal to stop the conversation. See the C-Corp tax strategy guide.

The Non-Tax Benefits Are Real

Wyoming does offer genuine advantages that have nothing to do with income tax.

Privacy. Wyoming does not require officer or director names on the public annual report for corporations in the way many states do, and shareholder information is not public. Note that federal beneficial ownership reporting obligations operate separately from state law and should be evaluated on their own.

Charging order protection. Wyoming's LLC statute provides strong charging order protection, limiting a judgment creditor's remedy against a member's interest. This is an LLC feature rather than a corporate one, and it is a common reason to use a Wyoming LLC specifically.

Low cost and light administration. Modest filing fees and a simple annual report.

Favorable statutes. Wyoming has been early on series LLCs and digital asset legislation.

These are legitimate reasons to choose Wyoming. They are asset protection and administrative reasons, not income tax reasons, and they should be presented as such.

The Costs of Getting It Wrong

An owner who forms a Wyoming corporation while operating in a taxing state typically ends up with:

  • Foreign qualification requirements in the home state, plus penalties if registration is late
  • Two sets of annual filings and two registered agents
  • Home state corporate tax anyway, on apportioned income
  • Minimum franchise or entity taxes in the home state regardless of profit
  • Potential penalties for operating unregistered, and in some states loss of standing to sue in state courts

Roughly $1,500 to $4,000 a year of additional cost for zero tax benefit.

How to Decide

Three questions, in order.

Where do your customers, employees, and property actually sit? That is where you will be taxed. If the answer is a high-tax state, incorporation elsewhere does not change it.

Are you willing to relocate? If yes, the strategy is real and worth planning properly, including the departure audit exposure. If no, treat Wyoming as an asset-protection choice.

Is there a genuine holding, IP, or Wyoming-situs structure available? These work, but they require substance and state-specific analysis, not a template.

For most owners, the honest answer is that the state where you live and work is the state that taxes you, and the meaningful savings come from federal structure rather than from a certificate of incorporation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does forming a Wyoming C-Corp eliminate my state income tax?

Only if the income is genuinely sourced to Wyoming. States tax based on nexus and apportionment, not the state of incorporation. If your employees, property, and customers are in another state, that state taxes the apportioned income regardless of where the entity was formed, and you have added foreign qualification and registered agent costs for no benefit.

Do I have to live in Wyoming for this to work?

For the personal income tax benefit, yes. Establishing genuine Wyoming domicile eliminates state tax on your personal income, and a corporation operated from Wyoming with Wyoming-sourced activity avoids state corporate tax. High-tax states audit departures closely, so the evidence of domicile has to be real: days present, residence, licenses, registrations, banking, and family location.

Does a Wyoming corporation reduce my federal taxes?

No. Federal tax treatment is identical regardless of the state of incorporation. The corporation pays the same flat 21% rate under IRC Sec. 11(b), and every federal strategy applies the same way. Anyone presenting a Wyoming entity as a federal tax strategy is describing something that does not exist.

What are the real benefits of a Wyoming entity?

Privacy, since ownership information is generally not public at the state level; strong charging order protection under Wyoming's LLC statute, which limits a judgment creditor's remedy; low filing fees and light annual administration; and modern statutes on series LLCs and digital assets. These are asset protection and administrative benefits rather than income tax benefits.

What does it cost if I set this up incorrectly?

Typically $1,500 to $4,000 per year in unnecessary cost: two registered agents, two sets of annual filings, foreign qualification in your home state, and your home state's minimum franchise or entity tax anyway. Operating unregistered in your home state can also produce penalties and, in some states, loss of standing to bring suit in state courts.
