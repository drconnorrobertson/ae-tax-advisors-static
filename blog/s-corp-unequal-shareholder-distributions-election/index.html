Our S corporation paid one owner more. Did that end our S election?

Published September 25, 2026 | S Corporation Tax Planning | AE Tax Advisors

Usually not from the unequal payment alone. An S corporation's one-class-of-stock test principally asks whether every outstanding share has identical rights to distributions and liquidation proceeds under the company's governing provisions. IRS Revenue Procedure 2022-19 says a disproportionate distribution by itself does not violate that test when those rights are identical. The extra cash still needs the correct tax treatment. If an operating agreement, charter, or binding side agreement instead gives one owner preferential economic rights, the S election may be at risk and a different correction analysis is needed. Do not reallocate K-1 profit merely to match the bank transfers.

If your company is about to sign Form 1120-S, admit a new owner, or sell the business, reconcile this now. A cash imbalance, an invalid share class, and a mistaken payroll or loan entry can lead to different returns and remedies.

First separate an unequal payment from an unequal legal right

Review the articles, bylaws or LLC operating agreement, shareholder agreements, redemption terms, and any binding side letter. The IRS one-class rule looks to rights under those governing provisions, not just the checks written this quarter. Voting rights may differ; rights to distribution and liquidation proceeds generally must remain identical. An arrangement that functions as a second class of equity can also matter even when the cap table says there is only common stock. The IRS revenue procedure explains both the governing-provision test and the distinction between it and an actual disproportionate distribution.

That is why neither blanket answer is sound: “one late catch-up check automatically revokes S status” overstates the rule, while “unequal draws never matter” ignores tax characterization and possibly preferential rights. Treat the payment record as evidence to investigate, not as a substitute for reading the legal documents and the stock ledger.

What the numbers change—and what they do not

Illustration: Assume A owns 60% and B owns 40% of an S corporation for the entire year, the governing documents give every share identical economic rights, and the company has $1 million of taxable income. It transfers $240,000 to A and $60,000 to B, each labeled “distribution.” A simple pro rata split of that $300,000 cash would have been $180,000 and $120,000: the payment imbalance is $60,000. Under the stated facts, the unequal transfers alone do not create a second class of stock. They also do not change the ordinary 60/40 allocation of the $1 million of income to $600,000 and $400,000 on the owners' K-1s. The Form 1120-S instructions govern that shareholder reporting.

The $60,000 comparison is not a tax bill and does not prove B must receive an immediate $60,000 check. Investigate why A received more: a temporary advance, repayment of bona fide shareholder debt, compensation for services, a redemption, an authorized timing difference, or an actual distribution can have different legal and tax consequences. Do not backdate a loan note, invent wages, or relabel a transfer merely to make the spreadsheet balance. If the payments were distributions, compute each owner's stock basis independently; the IRS stock-and-debt-basis guidance and Form 7203 help determine whether any amount exceeds basis. AE's distribution-basis guide covers that separate owner-level calculation.

Use the correct branch before attempting a fix

  1. Rights are identical; payments differ. Document the governing rights and reconstruct each transfer from the bank ledger, board approvals, payroll, loan accounts, and stock basis. Apply the proper tax treatment to the actual facts. Rev. Proc. 2022-19 says the disproportionate payment itself does not end the S election in this branch, but it does not waive the tax effect of the payments.
  2. A document grants preferential rights. Have tax and corporate counsel identify when the nonidentical provision began and whether it created a second class of stock. Do not assume a later equalizing check retroactively repairs the election. Section 3.06 of Rev. Proc. 2022-19 provides a limited retroactive relief process for certain nonidentical governing provisions, but requires that no actual or deemed disproportionate distribution was made to an applicable shareholder, among other conditions. An owner who already received unequal payments cannot automatically use that streamlined route.
  3. Facts fall outside streamlined relief. Review whether inadvertent-termination relief under Section 1362(f), potentially through a private letter ruling, or another applicable procedure is required. This is not the same as filing a routine late Form 2553. Coordinate the company's return position and the owners' returns with counsel before representing that S status continued.

A company can have both a rights defect and mislabeled payments. Resolve the legal status and cash characterization separately; a journal entry is not a universal cure.

Records and failure points for a return review

Bring the original and amended charter or operating agreement, shareholder and buy-sell agreements, stock ledger, distribution resolutions, payroll registers, shareholder-loan notes and repayment schedules, bank statements, prior Forms 1120-S and K-1s, accumulated adjustments account workpapers, and each owner's stock-and-debt-basis schedule. If an owner changed during the year, bring the dated transfer and redemption documents as well; AE's midyear shareholder guide addresses income allocation when ownership actually changes.

  • Changing K-1 profit to follow cash. An unequal withdrawal does not, by itself, change ownership percentages or the income-allocation rule.
  • Assuming every “draw” is tax-free. A true distribution can exceed an owner's stock basis; corporate earnings and profits, if any, introduce another ordering analysis.
  • Ignoring a side letter. An economic preference in a binding agreement can be more consequential than the year's check pattern.
  • Claiming the streamlined fix without checking eligibility. Rev. Proc. 2022-19 section 3.06 has conditions, including the absence of disproportionate distributions for the affected period.
  • Calling compensation a distribution. Payments for a shareholder-employee's services require separate reasonable-compensation and employment-tax review under IRS compensation guidance.

This is a federal framework, not a determination about a particular company's election. State entity law, agreements, actual payment purpose, shareholder eligibility, and prior filings can change the answer.
