Can I seller-finance a rental sale to my child and spread the gain?

Published on August 10, 2026 | Real Estate Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Often, yes: a genuine sale of a rental to your child as an individual can qualify for installment reporting when you receive at least one payment after the sale year. But a family note does not defer every tax item. Ordinary depreciation recapture is generally reported in the sale year, note interest is separate, and a child's resale within two years can accelerate your remaining installment gain. Selling depreciable property to an entity you control raises a different, stricter eligibility rule.

Before signing the deed and note, have the buyer identity, fair-market-value support, debt, asset-level depreciation, payment schedule, and likely resale plans reviewed together. AE can reconcile the proposed terms with the sale-year return and the child's intended use.

Two related-party rules that are easy to confuse

IRS Publication 537 separates the initial sale of depreciable property to certain related persons from a later disposition by a related buyer. The initial-sale restriction generally denies installment treatment for depreciable property sold to a controlled entity or another person in its narrower Section 453(g) list, absent a qualifying exception. A child buying directly as an individual is not automatically in that narrower list. Do not assume the same result if the buyer is a corporation, partnership, trust, or LLC: identify the actual federal tax owner and test the control and attribution rules first.

The later-sale rule, Section 453(e), uses a broader family definition that does include a child. If your child sells, exchanges, or gives away the property within two years while your original installment price remains unpaid, part or all of the child's amount realized can be treated as a payment to you in that year. Publication 537 explains the calculation and exceptions, including a demonstrated nontax-avoidance purpose. A promise that the child will “just keep it” is not a substitute for modeling the financing and possible exit.

These rules are separate from the ordinary-income recapture question. A cost-segregated rental may contain Section 1245 assets; a building can have Section 1250 ordinary recapture if additional depreciation is involved. By contrast, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain from straight-line building depreciation is a different tax-rate category, not automatically an up-front ordinary recapture item. See AE's asset-character guide and installment-recognition guide before quoting a down payment.

Worked example: parent finances a child's rental purchase

Assume a parent sells a rental to an adult child individually for its supported $600,000 value. The parent's adjusted basis is $300,000, with no debt, selling expenses, or ordinary depreciation recapture in this simplified example. The child pays $120,000 at closing and gives a bona fide $480,000 note with adequate stated interest. The $300,000 installment gross profit divided by the $600,000 contract price gives a 50% gross-profit percentage: $60,000 of the $120,000 principal paid at closing is installment gain. Stated interest is reported separately. The property's actual asset mix may change the character of that gain, including any unrecaptured Section 1250 portion.

If the child resells the property for $650,000 the next year after only the down payment, Section 453(e) can treat up to the remaining $480,000 contract price as received by the parent, even without a matching cash payment from the child. Applying the illustrative 50% ratio would bring up to $240,000 of remaining installment gain into that resale year, subject to the actual payment history and statutory exceptions. The parent's later principal collections are tracked so gain already reported is not counted twice. If instead the buyer is a controlled entity, the initial Section 453(g) eligibility test must be resolved before using this installment example at all.

Decide before the family closes

  1. Identify the seller and buyer for federal tax purposes. A deed to your child personally, a controlled company, and a trust can trigger different related-person tests.
  2. Reconcile price, basis, debt, and components. Obtain a defensible valuation, the closing statement, land/building allocation, cost-segregation schedule, improvements, and any mortgage payoff or assumption. A below-market price can add a separate gift-tax question.
  3. Model immediate tax and note economics. Separate ordinary recapture, installment-eligible gain, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, and interest or original issue discount. Do not set the down payment solely from the buyer's financing capacity.
  4. Document a real note and resale plan. Keep signed payment terms, security, actual transfers, an interest-rate analysis, and any planned refinance or sale; revisit the parent's Form 6252 if the child disposes of the property.

Bring the prior returns, Form 4562 and asset ledger, proposed deed and note, valuation, loan payoff, buyer-entity documents, and any draft closing statement to the review. Common failures are treating the note as proof of deferral, applying the controlled-entity restriction to every family sale, ignoring the child's two-year resale, treating straight-line building depreciation as ordinary recapture, or forgetting that a mortgage can change the installment contract price. This is a federal framework; ownership, state law, gift tax, and the final return facts can change the result.

The Installment Method Mechanics

For a qualifying sale, Publication 537 generally applies a gross-profit percentage to principal payments received or treated as received. Selling costs, ordinary recapture, asset allocations, and assumed debt affect that percentage; interest is separate.

For a simple debt-free asset sold for $1,400,000 with a $600,000 adjusted basis and no selling costs or ordinary recapture, installment gross profit is $800,000 and the percentage is about 57.14%. A $200,000 principal down payment would carry about $114,286 of gain. A real rental sale needs an asset-by-asset calculation before using that shortcut.

Stated interest, unstated interest, or original issue discount is separate from installment gain and is reported under the applicable interest and accounting-method rules.

The installment method generally applies unless a qualifying seller elects out under Section 453(d). An election out may be worth comparing when the sale year has usable losses or a lower expected rate, but the timing and return-reporting rules matter; Publication 537 describes the election deadline and limited relief for a timely filed return.

Depreciation Recapture Is Not Deferred

Under Section 453(i), ordinary-income depreciation recapture under Sections 1245 and 1250 is reported in the year of sale, regardless of cash received. Section 1250 ordinary recapture is generally the additional-depreciation amount, if any; straight-line building depreciation more commonly produces unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, a separate character category that can be recognized as installment payments arrive. IRS Publication 544 and Publication 537 explain the character and timing rules.

For a seller with a cost-segregation study, a small down payment can be inadequate to fund tax on Section 1245 recapture. The actual tax is not equal to the recapture amount; calculate it using the seller's complete year-of-sale return, asset allocation, and other available cash.

The planning answer is to size the down payment against the recapture liability rather than against a rule of thumb. A seller with heavy recapture needs more cash at closing, and that is a negotiating point that should be raised early.

For an eligible installment sale, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain may be recognized across payments. It generally is counted before remaining lower-rate long-term gain, and “up to 25%” is a maximum federal rate category, not a guaranteed 25% tax bill. Model each year's character and applicable rate.

Interest Must Be Adequate

If the note does not charge adequate stated interest, the IRS will impute it under IRC Sec. 483 or the original issue discount rules of IRC Sec. 1274, depending on the transaction. The effect is to recharacterize part of what the parties called principal as interest.

That can reduce the amount treated as sale price and increase ordinary interest income. A low stated rate on a large family note deserves a current-rate calculation, not a presumption that all stated principal is sale proceeds.

Publication 537 describes the applicable test rate, contract and sale dates, term, and statutory exceptions under Sections 1274 and 483. The current IRS applicable federal rates are a starting point; confirm the rate and compounding convention for the actual note rather than applying a single blanket AFR rule.

What Happens If the Seller Disposes of the Note

Selling or gifting an installment obligation can trigger gain under Section 453B; a pledge is analyzed under a separate Section 453A(d) rule. Neither should be treated as an ordinary, tax-neutral family transfer without reviewing the obligation's basis, value, and facts.

If a company buyer has already stopped paying, the next question is different from a planned sale or gift of the note: review default, settlement, and repossession before deciding whether any return needs amendment. A missed payment alone is not the same as a legal cancellation or recovery of collateral.

If the pledge rule applies—for example, to a nonexempt property sale above its $150,000 selling-price threshold—net loan proceeds secured by the installment obligation may be treated as a payment, subject to the statutory limit. Publication 537 lists exceptions and explains how later principal payments avoid double counting.

Section 453A can also impose an interest charge on certain large installment obligations when the statutory threshold and other conditions are met. Test the combined outstanding obligations, excluded property, and applicable year rather than assuming every note above $5 million is treated alike.

Death and Basis

Unreported gain embedded in an installment obligation is generally income in respect of a decedent; a death does not simply erase the installment gain. Publication 537 explains how the estate or beneficiary reports later payments.

That differs from the potential basis adjustment on property still owned at death, but estate value, existing debt, liquidity needs, expected sale, and state taxes affect the comparison. Neither path is automatically superior.

For a family transfer, compare keeping the rental, a fair-value sale with a note, and any gift component using the actual estate and income-tax facts. Do not promise heirs a tax-free sale or assume a note eliminates the parent's deferred gain.

Worked Example: Seller-Financed Commercial Sale

For a separate unrelated-buyer scenario, assume the allocated sale price for a commercial property is $1,850,000 and the aggregate adjusted basis is $740,000. An asset-by-asset sale analysis, supported by an earlier cost-segregation study, produces $180,000 of actual Section 1245 ordinary recapture. The study's historical deduction alone would not establish that recapture amount.

Terms are $370,000 down and a $1,480,000 note at 7.25% over 15 years with a balloon at year seven.

The $180,000 of assumed Section 1245 ordinary recapture is reported in the sale year under Section 453(i), even if the note is otherwise eligible for installment treatment. The seller must calculate the actual federal and state tax from the full return; this example cannot support a fixed $67,000 tax estimate. A smaller down payment could make that immediate liability harder to fund.

The remaining $930,000 of aggregate gain requires an asset-by-asset installment calculation. If the building analysis separately identifies $280,000 of unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, its character is generally recognized first as installment gain is reported; that does not make every dollar of that category taxable at exactly 25%. Debt assumption, selling expenses, and other components would change the computation.

The balloon may bunch substantial gain into year seven, so compare a payment-by-payment federal and state model rather than promising a lower capital-gain bracket or net investment income tax result. For return reporting, review the IRS's Form 6252 overview and Form 4797 instructions alongside the note schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sell a rental to my child on an installment note?

Often, yes, if it is a genuine sale to the child individually and the general installment requirements are met. The narrower rule for depreciable property sold to certain controlled related persons is different from the two-year resale rule that includes children. Ordinary depreciation recapture may still be due in the sale year.

Does seller financing defer all of my tax?

No. Ordinary-income recapture under Sections 1245 and 1250 is generally reported in the sale year under Section 453(i). Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain is different from ordinary recapture and may be recognized with installment payments. Interest is also separate; size the down payment against the actual sale-year tax.

What interest rate do I have to charge?

Use the applicable test rate and compounding convention for the contract, sale date, and note term. Sections 483 or 1274 can recharacterize part of stated principal as interest when adequate interest is missing, but exceptions and special rules mean a single AFR slogan is not enough.

Can I borrow against my installment note?

Possibly, but test the Section 453A(d) pledge rule before doing so. For a covered sale, net loan proceeds secured by the note may count as an installment payment, subject to limits and exceptions. Selling or gifting the obligation raises a separate Section 453B disposition issue.

What happens to an installment note when I die?

Remaining unreported installment gain is generally income in respect of a decedent and is not simply wiped out at death. The estate or beneficiary may report gain as payments arrive. Compare that with retaining the underlying property using the actual estate, debt, liquidity, and income-tax facts.

Can I choose not to use the installment method?

Yes, for a qualifying sale you can generally elect out under Section 453(d) and report the gain in the sale year. Model usable losses and rates first, then follow Publication 537's election and filing deadlines. An election out cannot make an otherwise ineligible family or controlled-entity sale eligible.
