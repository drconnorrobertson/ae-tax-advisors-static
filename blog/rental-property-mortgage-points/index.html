I sold or refinanced my rental. Can I deduct the remaining mortgage points?

Published September 22, 2026 | Real Estate Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Possibly. Rental mortgage points that are prepaid interest generally are recovered over the loan term, not deducted all at closing. If the old loan ends because you sell, pay it off, or refinance, the remaining eligible points may be deductible in that year. A refinance with the same lender is an important exception: the old balance generally is recovered over the new loan term instead. The answer also depends on what the charge actually paid for and how the borrowed proceeds were used.

If you are selling or signing a new rental loan, bring both closing disclosures, the old loan payoff, and your points schedule. AE can reconcile the remaining balance and new-loan costs before a return or amendment is prepared.

First separate points from other closing charges

The lender may call a charge discount points, origination fees, or loan charges. Under IRS Publication 527, a charge solely for the use of money is prepaid interest and follows the original-issue-discount (OID) rules. That is not the same thing as an escrow deposit, property tax, title cost, appraisal, prepaid insurance, or fee for a separate service. Build a line-by-line schedule from the loan disclosure; do not assume every cost on the settlement statement follows the points rule. Our loan-origination fee guide explains the broader financing-cost classification.

OID is not always a simple equal annual deduction. Publication 527 uses a de minimis test. If total OID is de minimis, certain methods including straight-line recovery may be available if applied consistently on the timely filed first-year return; otherwise the constant-yield method generally applies. A lender's Form 1098 amount is not by itself permission to deduct all points in the payment year.

What happens when the old loan ends?

  • Property sale or full payoff. A payoff can end the old debt. Publication 527 says any remaining eligible points may be deductible in the tax year the loan ends. Confirm the payoff date, unused balance, rental-use allocation, and whether another rule limits current use of the resulting rental deduction.
  • Refinance with a different lender. The original loan ordinarily ends. The remaining eligible old-loan points may be deductible then; new points start a separate schedule. Do not combine the two balances as though they were paid on one note.
  • Refinance with the same lender. Publication 527 says the remaining old points generally are not deducted immediately in the refinance year and may instead be recovered over the new term. Ask whether the transaction truly ended the old obligation and document the lender identity.
  • Cash-out for personal spending. The new loan's points and interest must be allocated to the use of the proceeds. Points allocable to cash used for a car or other personal purpose generally are not a rental expense merely because the loan is secured by a rental. See the cash-out refinance guide for tracing examples.

Worked example: $2,800 remains when the loan is paid off

Assume a $400,000, ten-year rental loan carried $4,000 of eligible points, there is no other OID, and the owner validly used straight-line recovery under the de minimis rule. After three complete years, $1,200 has been deducted and $2,800 remains. If the owner sells the rental and fully pays the loan at the beginning of year four, that $2,800 may be deductible in the payoff year, subject to the actual loan facts and rental-loss rules. If the owner instead refinances with the same lender, do not automatically deduct the $2,800: the old balance generally carries into recovery over the new loan term. A different-lender refinance may produce the payoff-year result, while points on the new note require their own OID calculation. The example isolates points; other financing fees and gain on sale are separate issues.

Return-review packet and common errors

Gather the purchase and refinance closing disclosures, promissory notes, lender names, Form 1098s, payoff statement, old points/OID workpaper, filed Schedule E returns, and bank records showing how extra cash was spent. Reconcile opening unamortized points plus new charges, less amounts already deducted, to the closing balance for each loan. Check whether an earlier return immediately deducted points, omitted them, used straight-line recovery without meeting the de minimis conditions, or put financing costs into building basis. A sale does not make those errors disappear; correct the relevant return and carryforward schedules before claiming a leftover amount.

This is a federal framework, not a guaranteed deduction. Same-lender continuity, mixed-use debt, passive-loss limitations, entity ownership, accounting method, and state treatment can change the answer. Keep the old-loan points schedule separate from the new-loan schedule and the property's depreciation basis.

The tax treatment

Points on a loan for rental property are generally spread over the loan term, unlike the limited immediate deduction sometimes available for a principal residence. This is a federal income tax starting point; the contract, ownership, accounting method, and actual use can change the result. State and local treatment should be checked separately.

The decision to make before filing

Determine the term and whether the payment truly represents prepaid interest. The useful planning step is to resolve the classification while the underlying documents are still available, then reconcile it to the books and the prior-year return. If the transaction spans more than one year, track the opening balance and what happened to it afterward.

Illustrative example

An investor pays points to lower the rate on a loan secured by a rental condo. Even if the closing statement calls them discount points, the rental-property timing rules differ from the familiar principal-residence exception. The lender documents and loan term control the schedule.

Records that support the position

Retain the settlement statement, note, amortization worksheet, and payoff records. Tie amounts on the return to bank activity and the agreement. When several assets, people, or uses are involved, write down the allocation method and apply it consistently. A short dated workpaper is easier to defend than a reconstructed explanation years later.

A common reporting error

Applying the personal-home point rule to an investment property can produce an early deduction. Review both sides of the entry: a payment can affect income, basis, liability, or an expense at different times. A correct cash total alone does not establish the correct tax character.

Where to verify the rule

Start with IRS Publication 527: Residential Rental Property, especially its points, OID, and loan-ending sections. Its examples explain the de minimis and constant-yield calculations, the same-lender exception, and allocation when cash-out proceeds have nonrental use. Check the version governing the year at issue. For a coordinated review of related deductions and limitations, see Real estate tax planning.
