Our S Corporation Used Company Cash to Buy Out a Shareholder. Was It a Redemption?

Published September 24, 2026 | S-Corp Planning | AE Tax Advisors

Not necessarily. If the corporation legally acquired and retired or held the departing owner's shares, the transaction may be a corporate redemption. If a continuing owner acquired those shares, it is a cross-purchase even if corporate money moved around the same closing. Company cash used to fund that personal purchase may also create a separate distribution, bona fide loan, or other tax issue for the buyer. The signed agreement, stock ledger, payment instructions, and actual obligations—not a bookkeeping label—must be reconciled before the Form 1120-S, K-1s, and shareholder returns are filed.

First identify who actually bought the stock

In a cross-purchase, the continuing shareholder buys stock from the departing shareholder. The buyer generally takes cost basis in the newly acquired shares; the corporation has not itself purchased an asset or obtained a new tax basis in its operating assets. The IRS Form 7203 instructions say purchased stock's basis is usually its cost and distinguish stock acquired through purchase from other basis changes. The departing owner's sale proceeds and adjusted basis must be computed on that owner's return.

In a redemption, the S corporation acquires its own stock from the departing shareholder. The continuing owner's percentage rises because fewer shares remain outstanding, but the corporation's payment does not by itself add the redemption price to that continuing owner's stock basis. The departing owner's characterization depends on the redemption rules—including Section 302's sale-or-exchange tests, constructive ownership, and whether the corporation has accumulated earnings and profits from C-corporation years. Do not assume every redemption is automatically a capital-gain stock sale or an ordinary dividend. See Section 302's statutory tests and the Form 1120-S instructions before choosing a reporting path.

A wire from the corporate bank account is important evidence, but not the whole answer. The corporation might have paid its own redemption obligation, distributed funds to the continuing owner who then bought stock, or advanced a documented loan to that owner. A payment booked to “treasury stock” or “shareholder loan” without matching legal documents, repayment terms, and stock records does not resolve the substance.

What a continuing owner's basis can look like

Assume two shareholders each own 50% of an operating S corporation. One agrees to exit for $1 million. The continuing shareholder's existing stock basis is $300,000 before current-year pass-through adjustments. These figures are illustrative; they do not establish a tax result for any actual transaction.

  • Personal cross-purchase: if the continuing shareholder personally owes and pays $1 million for the other's shares, the acquired block generally has $1 million of cost basis. The owner's original block retains its separately tracked basis, subject to normal S-corporation adjustments. There is no automatic $1 million increase to the corporation's basis in its equipment, goodwill, or real estate.
  • Corporate redemption: if the corporation itself owes and pays $1 million to redeem the departing owner's shares, the continuing owner does not receive a $1 million purchased-stock block. Their basis continues to change through their own share of corporate income, distributions, and other applicable adjustments—not simply because they now own 100%.
  • Corporation pays a personal purchase obligation: if the agreement names the continuing owner as buyer but the corporation wires the $1 million, determine why the company paid. A documented, genuine loan to the buyer is different from a distribution or a corporate redemption. If it was a distribution, the buyer's stock basis and possible tax on an excess distribution must be tested; the seller's stock sale is a separate leg. Do not make offsetting journal entries and assume the legs disappear.

For the buyer, the economic distinction can matter years later when another distribution, loss, or sale tests stock basis. For the seller, a partial sale or redemption can require allocating basis among share blocks. Form 7203's instructions expressly address partial stock sales and redemptions. The Form 1120-S and K-1 review is separate from the stock price

A share transfer during the year changes which owner is allocated corporate income and deductions. Under the general rule, the Form 1120-S instructions weight ownership by days held. When a shareholder's entire interest terminates, a consent-based Section 1377(a)(2) election may permit a closing-of-the-books allocation; a qualifying disposition can have a separate election under Regulations section 1.1368-1(g)(2). Neither election happens automatically just because the owners chose a closing date. The corporation and affected shareholders should model the result and satisfy the applicable statement and consent rules.

The corporation should reconcile its stock ledger, Schedules K-1, Schedule L, and any distribution or shareholder-loan entries to the signed purchase or redemption documents. The departing owner's K-1 still reports their share of pass-through items for the relevant ownership period; it is not a substitute for reporting the separate disposition of their stock. If an already-filed 1120-S used the wrong ownership percentages or distribution boxes, review the S-corporation amendment and corrected-K-1 workflow before owners independently amend personal returns.

Documents to gather before filing or amending

  • Signed buy-sell agreement, board and shareholder approvals, stock certificates or ledger, cap table before and after closing, and any redemption or cancellation documents.
  • Closing statement, wire instructions, bank records, escrow ledger, promissory notes, guarantees, and evidence of who is legally liable for each payment.
  • Corporate general ledger entries for treasury stock, distributions, shareholder loans, interest, and transaction costs, with supporting invoices.
  • Each owner's prior Form 7203 or basis workpapers, K-1s, capital-contribution and loan history, plus any C-corporation earnings-and-profits history.
  • Draft or filed Form 1120-S, both owners' K-1s, and the seller's stock-disposition calculation. Include state returns and any installment-payment schedule.

Failure points that commonly change the result

  • Giving the continuing owner basis for a corporate redemption: acquiring 100% control is not the same as personally paying for another share block.
  • Calling a company-funded cross-purchase a redemption after filing: the agreement, stock records, and legal payment obligation may contradict that label. Correct the underlying documentation and returns based on facts; do not relabel a closed deal to select a preferred tax result.
  • Ignoring a possible distribution or shareholder loan: corporate money used for an owner's personal obligation needs its own basis, repayment, and reporting analysis.
  • Allocating all annual profit to whoever owns the shares on December 31: the default ownership-by-days rule and any valid election must be tested before K-1s are issued.
  • Assuming the seller's proceeds are all capital gain: stock basis, Section 302 characterization, inherited C-corporation earnings and profits, and any installment terms can change the return.

Before either owner files, ask for one reconciled schedule showing the legal stock buyer, source and destination of every payment, pre- and post-closing ownership, each shareholder's adjusted basis, K-1 allocation method, and the seller's proceeds. That is the useful scope for a return review—not a generic “S-corp buyout deduction” estimate.
