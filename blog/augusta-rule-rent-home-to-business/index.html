The Augusta Rule: How to Rent Your Home to Your Business Tax-Free

The Augusta Rule is one of the simplest and most underutilized tax strategies available to business owners. Named after the city of Augusta, Georgia, where homeowners have long rented their residences during the Masters golf tournament, the rule under IRC Section 280A(g) allows any homeowner to rent out their personal residence for up to 14 days per year and exclude the rental income entirely from their tax return. For S-Corp and C-Corp owners, this creates a straightforward mechanism to extract tax-free income from their business while generating a deductible expense for the company.

The Legal Basis: IRC Section 280A(g)

IRC Section 280A generally restricts deductions related to business use of a personal residence. However, subsection (g) provides a carve-out: if a dwelling unit is used as a residence and is rented for fewer than 15 days during the tax year, the rental income is not included in gross income and no deductions attributable to the rental use are allowed (other than those that would be allowed regardless of rental use, such as mortgage interest and property taxes).

The key elements of Section 280A(g) are: the property must be a "dwelling unit" used as a personal residence, the total number of rental days must be 14 or fewer during the calendar year, the rental income is completely excluded from gross income (it does not even need to be reported), and rental-specific expenses cannot be deducted (but this limitation is irrelevant when the income itself is tax-free).

The exclusion is absolute. The rental income does not appear on Schedule E, Form 1040, or any other tax form. It is not subject to income tax, self-employment tax, or the Net Investment Income Tax. For a business owner in the 37% federal bracket plus state taxes, every dollar received under the Augusta Rule is worth significantly more than a dollar received as salary, distributions, or other taxable income.

How Business Owners Use the Augusta Rule

The most common application of the Augusta Rule for business owners involves renting their personal residence to their own business entity (S-Corp, C-Corp, LLC, or partnership) for legitimate business purposes. The business pays fair market rent for use of the home on specific days, and the homeowner receives that rent tax-free.

Legitimate business uses include: quarterly board of directors meetings, annual strategic planning retreats, team meetings or training sessions, client appreciation events, company holiday parties, product launch planning sessions, investor meetings, and any other bona fide business event that requires a meeting venue.

The business deducts the rental payment as an ordinary and necessary business expense under IRC Section 162, reducing the company's taxable income. The homeowner receives the payment and excludes it from income under Section 280A(g). The net effect: a deduction on one side and tax-free income on the other.

Determining Fair Market Value

The rental rate charged must reflect the fair market value (FMV) of comparable rental space. This is the most important compliance step. The IRS will not accept inflated or unreasonable rates, and overpaying rent can result in reclassification of the excess as a constructive distribution, dividend, or compensation (depending on entity type).

Methods for establishing fair market value include: researching comparable event venue rentals in the same geographic area (hotels, conference centers, estate rentals), obtaining quotes from online rental platforms (Peerspace, VRBO, Airbnb) for daily rates on similar properties, documenting the property's features (square footage, number of rooms, outdoor space, kitchen facilities, parking) and comparing to available rental comparables, and in some cases, obtaining a formal FMV appraisal from a licensed appraiser.

Daily rental rates for the Augusta Rule typically range from $500 to $5,000 depending on property size, location, and local market comparables. A 3,000-square-foot home in a suburban area might command $1,000 to $2,000 per day for event rental, while a larger property in a high-cost market could justify $3,000 to $5,000 per day.

At 14 days and $2,000 per day, the total annual tax-free income under the Augusta Rule would be $28,000. In the 37% federal bracket, that is equivalent to approximately $44,400 in pre-tax income. Adding state tax savings makes the value even higher.

The S-Corp Owner's Application

The Augusta Rule is particularly powerful for S-Corp owners because it creates a mechanism to extract value from the business without payroll taxes, income taxes, or the complexities of distribution planning.

Here is how a typical arrangement works. The S-Corp schedules a quarterly board meeting at the owner's home. A written rental agreement is executed between the homeowner (in their personal capacity) and the S-Corp, specifying the date, rental rate, and business purpose. The S-Corp pays $2,500 for the day's use of the residence. The S-Corp deducts the $2,500 as rent expense on its tax return. The homeowner receives $2,500 and does not report it as income.

Repeating this process for 12 legitimate business events throughout the year at an average rate of $2,000 per day generates $24,000 in tax-free income. The S-Corp reduces its taxable pass-through income by the same $24,000, saving the owner additional income taxes on the reduced K-1 amount.

Business Purpose Requirement

The IRS requires that the rental arrangement serve a legitimate business purpose. Simply labeling a personal dinner as a "board meeting" does not satisfy this requirement. Each rental day must involve genuine business activity. Best practices include:

  • Preparing a written agenda for each meeting or event
  • Taking meeting minutes or notes documenting topics discussed and decisions made
  • Inviting participants other than just the owner (employees, advisors, board members, clients)
  • Maintaining sign-in sheets or attendance records
  • Keeping photos of the event setup (meeting room, presentation materials, catering)
  • Retaining receipts for any catering, supplies, or other event-related expenses

The business purpose does not need to be elaborate. A half-day strategic planning session, a quarterly financial review with the company's accountant, or a team training event all constitute legitimate business purposes. What matters is that the event is genuine, documented, and would reasonably require a meeting venue.

Documentation Checklist

Proper documentation is the foundation of a defensible Augusta Rule arrangement. AE Tax Advisors recommends maintaining the following for each rental event:

  • Written rental agreement: Signed by the homeowner and the business entity, specifying dates, rental rate, and permitted use
  • Fair market value analysis: Comparable rental data supporting the daily rate charged
  • Business purpose documentation: Meeting agenda, minutes, or event description
  • Payment records: Bank statements showing the business payment to the homeowner (not a cash transaction)
  • Attendance records: List of participants, sign-in sheets, or email invitations
  • Calendar tracking: A log of all rental days to ensure the 14-day limit is not exceeded

Limitations and Audit Risk Factors

While the Augusta Rule is well-established in the tax code, certain practices increase audit risk:

Exceeding 14 days. If total rental days reach 15 or more, the entire exclusion is lost. All rental income for the year becomes taxable, and the property is treated as a rental for tax purposes. There is no partial exclusion. This makes careful tracking essential.

Inflated rental rates. Charging $10,000 per day for a modest home in a market where comparable venues rent for $1,500 will not survive scrutiny. The IRS can reclassify the excess as a constructive distribution (S-Corp) or constructive dividend (C-Corp), eliminating the tax-free treatment and potentially triggering penalties.

Lack of business substance. If every "board meeting" is just the owner sitting alone at the kitchen table, the IRS may argue there was no genuine business use of the residence. Real business activity with documentation provides the necessary substance.

Related-party scrutiny. Because the homeowner and the business entity are related parties, the IRS applies heightened scrutiny under IRC Section 267. The arrangement must reflect arm's-length terms, meaning the business would pay a similar rate to an unrelated venue for the same purpose.

Combining the Augusta Rule with Other Strategies

The Augusta Rule works well alongside other high-income tax strategies. The $24,000 to $35,000 in annual tax-free income supplements S-Corp payroll tax savings, retirement plan contributions, and real estate depreciation strategies without competing for the same deduction buckets or running into phase-out limitations.

For business owners who also own short-term rental properties, the Augusta Rule applies only to the personal residence, not to investment properties. STR properties have their own separate tax framework under the 7-day rule and material participation tests.

Common Misconceptions

Misconception: The Augusta Rule is a "loophole" that could be closed. Section 280A(g) has been in the tax code since 1976. It is not a temporary provision, not tied to any sunset legislation, and not subject to the OBBBA or other recent tax reform. It is established law with nearly 50 years of precedent.

Misconception: Only S-Corp owners can use it. Any homeowner with any type of business entity, including sole proprietorships, can use the Augusta Rule. The entity type affects the deduction side (how the business expense is reported), not the income exclusion side.

Misconception: The home must be a luxury property. The Augusta Rule applies to any dwelling unit used as a personal residence, regardless of size, value, or location. A modest three-bedroom home hosting a legitimate quarterly board meeting qualifies just as readily as a lakeside estate hosting a corporate retreat.

Misconception: Using the Augusta Rule triggers a home office deduction. The 14-day rental exclusion is entirely separate from the home office deduction under IRC 280A(c). Renting the home for business events does not require claiming a home office, and the two provisions can be used independently or together without conflict.

Getting Started

Implementing the Augusta Rule is straightforward, but getting the details right matters. The fair market value analysis, rental agreement, and event documentation are the three pillars that support the arrangement. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Augusta Rule?

The Augusta Rule, codified in IRC Section 280A(g), allows homeowners to rent out their personal residence for up to 14 days per year without reporting the rental income on their tax return. The income is completely excluded from gross income. Originally designed for homeowners renting during events like the Masters Tournament in Augusta, Georgia, the rule applies to any personal residence nationwide.

Can I rent my home to my own S-Corp?

Yes. An S-Corp owner can rent their personal residence to their S-Corp for legitimate business purposes such as board meetings, strategic planning sessions, team retreats, or client meetings. The S-Corp pays fair market rent and deducts the payment as an ordinary business expense. The homeowner receives the rental income tax-free under Section 280A(g), provided total rental days do not exceed 14 per year.

How much rent can I charge under the Augusta Rule?

The rental rate must reflect fair market value for comparable event or meeting space in your area. This is determined by researching daily rental rates for similar-sized homes, event venues, or meeting spaces in the same geographic market. Rates typically range from $500 to $5,000 per day depending on location, property size, and local market comparables. A documented fair market value analysis is essential.

What documentation is required for an Augusta Rule rental?

Proper documentation includes a written rental agreement between the homeowner and the business entity, a fair market value analysis with comparable rental data, meeting agendas or business purpose documentation for each rental day, payment records showing the business paid the homeowner, and minutes or notes from the business events held at the residence. The IRS may challenge the arrangement if documentation is thin.

Does the Augusta Rule work with C-Corps and LLCs?

Yes. The Augusta Rule applies regardless of entity type. The business entity (S-Corp, C-Corp, LLC, or partnership) rents the residence for a legitimate business purpose, deducts the rental payment, and the homeowner excludes the income. The entity type affects only how the business deduction flows through to the owner's tax return, not whether the exclusion applies.

What happens if I rent my home for more than 14 days?

If total rental days exceed 14, the Section 280A(g) exclusion does not apply, and ALL rental income for the year becomes taxable (not just the income from days beyond 14). The property is then treated as a rental property, and rental income must be reported on Schedule E. This makes the 14-day limit a hard ceiling that must be carefully tracked.
