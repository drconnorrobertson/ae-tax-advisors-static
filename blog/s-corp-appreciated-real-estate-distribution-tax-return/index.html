My S Corporation Distributed Appreciated Real Estate: What Goes on Form 1120-S and My Return?

Published September 24, 2026 | Business Return Corrections | AE Tax Advisors

Short answer: transferring appreciated real estate from an S corporation to a shareholder is generally not a tax-free change of title. In a nonliquidating distribution, the corporation normally recognizes gain as if it sold the property for fair market value; that gain passes through to shareholders. The recipient also has a property distribution measured under the distribution rules and must calculate stock basis. The entity return, every affected K-1, and the recipient's individual return need to agree before filing.

Book a Return Review Call for an S Corp Property Transfer

First identify the transaction, not just the deed

Confirm who held title immediately before and after the transfer, the effective transfer date, whether the corporation received consideration, and whether the corporation is continuing. An outright nonliquidating distribution is different from a sale, a contribution to a disregarded entity still owned by the S corporation, a shareholder's purchase of stock, or a liquidating distribution. A sale or liquidation requires its own analysis; do not copy the nonliquidating steps below into a final-return transaction.

The branch that changes the answer is the asset's tax basis versus its fair market value on the distribution date. Under the IRS Schedule D (Form 1120-S) instructions, a nonliquidating distribution of appreciated property generally produces corporate gain to the extent fair market value exceeds adjusted basis, but generally does not recognize a loss when value is lower. An appraisal or defensible contemporaneous valuation matters even if no cash changes hands.

Work the entity return before the owner's return

  1. Rebuild adjusted basis. Start with acquisition records, capital improvements, depreciation allowed or allowable, and any cost-segregation components. Separate land from depreciable building and shorter-life assets. Book value is not tax basis.
  2. Classify the deemed disposition. IRS instructions direct a distribution of trade-or-business real estate or depreciable property to Form 4797. Capital assets follow the capital-gain reporting path. Depreciation recapture, Section 1231 treatment, and any unrecaptured Section 1250 gain must be analyzed rather than calling the entire difference “capital gain.”
  3. Allocate the gain. The S corporation's recognized gain appears in the appropriate Form 1120-S and Schedule K categories and is passed through to shareholders under the normal allocation rules. If one of several owners receives the building, do not assume only that owner recognizes the entity-level gain.
  4. Report the distribution separately. The recipient's nondividend distribution is reported on Schedule K-1, box 16, code D, to the extent applicable. The Form 1120-S instructions value distributed appreciated property at fair market value and require a statement with the acquisition date, distribution date, fair market value, and corporation's basis. Liabilities can affect the distribution amount and deemed sale computation, so review the mortgage documents instead of netting debt informally.

Then reconcile the shareholder return and property basis

Pass-through gain can increase the owner's stock basis before the nondividend distribution reduces it. The recipient should reconcile beginning stock basis, the year's K-1 income and other adjustments, and the distribution on Form 7203 when required. Debt basis from a direct shareholder loan is not a substitute for stock basis when testing a distribution. If the applicable nondividend distribution exceeds stock basis, the excess may be reported as stock gain on Form 8949 and Schedule D; old C-corporation accumulated earnings and profits can change the ordering and may create a dividend layer. The shareholder's basis in the real estate generally starts at its fair market value when distributed, subject to the transaction's actual facts and liabilities. See IRS Publication 542.

Those are potentially two tax effects: the owner's share of S-corporation property gain and possible gain or dividend from receiving a distribution. They are not a single tax calculated on the cash received. A later sale, rental, or personal use of the property will use the recipient's new basis and requires its own depreciation and holding-period analysis.

A simplified no-debt example

Suppose a one-owner S corporation distributes a warehouse worth $900,000 with $400,000 adjusted tax basis. Assume no mortgage, no old C-corporation earnings and profits, no other current-year items, and $100,000 of the owner's beginning stock basis. The corporation generally recognizes $500,000 of property gain; the owner's pro rata share increases stock basis to $600,000 before the distribution. A $900,000 nondividend distribution can exhaust that $600,000 stock basis and leave $300,000 of potential shareholder-level gain. The warehouse's basis in the recipient's hands is generally $900,000. This deliberately simplified arithmetic does not determine the character of the $500,000 entity gain: depreciation history and the asset's business use must be reviewed.

With two or more shareholders, property debt, a prior C-corporation period, a sale in partial consideration, or a liquidation, this arithmetic is not a filing template. In particular, gain allocation and distribution reporting can affect different shareholders differently.

What to gather before the 1120-S is filed or corrected

  • Deed, board or shareholder approval, transfer date, title-company and closing records, and a valuation tied to that date.
  • Original purchase allocation, fixed-asset and depreciation schedules, cost-segregation study if any, improvement invoices, and prior Form 4797 workpapers.
  • Loan statements and assumption or release agreements showing which liabilities followed the property.
  • Prior Forms 1120-S and K-1, each shareholder's stock-basis schedule, Form 7203, and any accumulated-earnings-and-profits or built-in-gain workpapers from a former C corporation.
  • Draft current-year entity return, all K-1s, and the recipient's proposed individual reporting so the same transaction is not counted twice or omitted.

Three filing mistakes to stop before they spread

Using book value as the distribution amount: the IRS requires fair-market-value reporting for appreciated property. Leaving gain off the 1120-S because no cash was received: a deed transfer can trigger a deemed taxable disposition. Fixing only the recipient's 1040: if the corporation omitted gain or the K-1 distribution, first determine the proper entity-return correction and issue consistent corrected K-1s. Shareholders should not invent an entity gain on an individual return without reconciling the corporation's filing.

A former C corporation may also face built-in-gains tax within the applicable recognition period. State income and transfer-tax treatment can differ from federal treatment. Have a qualified adviser review the transaction before filing, refinancing, or retitling again.
