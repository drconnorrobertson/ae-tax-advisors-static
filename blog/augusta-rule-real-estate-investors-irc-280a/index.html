The Augusta Rule for Real Estate Investors (IRC 280A(g))

What Is the Augusta Rule?

The Augusta Rule under IRC Section 280A(g) excludes rental income from a dwelling used as a residence and actually rented for fewer than 15 days during the tax year. The homeowner takes no deductions attributable to that rental use. Qualifying excluded rent is not reported on Schedule E, but the payer's information-return obligations need a separate review.

You do not need to own a business to rent a qualifying home to unrelated guests. Renting to your own S corporation, C corporation or partnership adds a separate business-deduction analysis. A property's label as a vacation home or investment property does not establish eligibility.

The 14-Day Exclusion Under IRC 280A(g)

Under Section 280A(d)(1), personal use must be more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days rented at a fair rental. Under Section 280A(g), actual rental use must total fewer than 15 days for the dwelling in the tax year. Count all renters and related companies together.

The exclusion has no dollar cap or income phase-out, but the residence and rental-day tests still apply. It disallows rental-use deductions, including depreciation, utilities and maintenance. Qualified mortgage interest and real estate taxes may remain deductible as personal itemized deductions, subject to their ordinary limits. There is no unlimited personal deduction.

How Business Owners Use the Augusta Rule

A separately taxed business may rent an owner's qualifying home for a genuine board meeting, planning session or other business use. The business must independently establish an ordinary and necessary expense under Section 162, reasonable rent and a documented purpose. The homeowner's exclusion does not guarantee the entity's deduction.

Document why the business needs the space and what an unrelated business would pay for the same use. Section 274 can disallow entertainment and entertainment-facility costs; calling a client party a meeting does not make it deductible. Evaluate meals and other event expenses separately.

Dollar Example: Model the Deduction and QBI Together

Assume a home meets the residence test and a wholly owned S corporation rents it for 12 actual days, with no other rentals that year. A hypothetical $1,500 daily rate supported by comparable venues for the actual meetings produces 12 days x $1,500 = $18,000 of rent. This is an illustration, not a current quote or an expected client outcome.

Assume the full $18,000 is currently deductible by the company, the shareholder can use the resulting income reduction, and all affected taxable income remains in the 37% federal bracket. If the payment does not change the shareholder's Section 199A QBI deduction, the simplified regular federal income-tax reduction is $18,000 x 37% = $6,660.

If instead the payment reduces QBI by $18,000 and an otherwise fully available 20% QBI deduction by $3,600, taxable income falls by $14,400. At the same 37% marginal rate, the simplified regular federal income-tax reduction is $5,328. These alternatives assume no binding wage, property, taxable-income or other QBI limitations, phase-in changes, or other return interactions. They are not savings guarantees.

Do not automatically add 3.8% NIIT to an active S corporation owner's rate. Ordinary operating income from a nonpassive business is generally outside NIIT. Any effect on NIIT from other investment income and modified adjusted gross income requires a separate calculation. State tax, other deduction limitations and implementation costs also affect the net result. The owner excludes qualifying $18,000 rent but cannot also deduct the home's rental-use expenses.

Fair Market Value: The Critical Requirement

A proposed $5,000 daily business rent is not supported merely by owning a home where comparable rentals cost $800. For the business deduction, the rate must reflect what an unrelated renter would pay for the actual space, hours, date, capacity and services.

For meetings, obtain comparable conference-room or venue quotes. Airbnb, VRBO and property-manager listings may help when the use is genuinely comparable, but a whole-home overnight quote is not automatically a daytime meeting price. Save dated evidence and explain adjustments. For a higher-value property, consider a rental-rate opinion addressing the actual proposed use.

Documentation Requirements

Maintain a file that the business and individual preparers can both review before the transaction and at filing time.

1. Written Rental Agreement

Identify the property, parties, dates, spaces available, rate, permitted use and payment terms. Prepare the agreement before the rental rather than reconstructing an arrangement afterward.

2. Corporate Minutes or Meeting Documentation

Retain agendas, attendees, meeting minutes or other records showing what business work actually occurred. A document titled board meeting is not enough without a real event and business purpose.

3. Comparable Rental Analysis and Calendar

Maintain dated comparable quotes and one annual calendar of all personal and rental use. Include rentals to unrelated guests and each related company. Review special personal-use rules for family use, below-market use and repair days.

4. Payment and Reporting Records

Retain invoices and evidence of actual payment, keeping rent separate from wages and distributions. Have the payer's and recipient's preparers determine information-return requirements for the applicable tax year and address any Form 1099 received. The Section 280A(g) income exclusion is not a blanket promise that no Form 1099 is required. Use the current IRS instructions rather than assuming an old reporting threshold still applies.

Common Mistakes and IRS Red Flags

Exceeding 14 days. At 15 actual rental days, the Section 280A(g) exclusion is unavailable for the entire year. Income reporting, expense allocation and deduction limitations must then be reviewed. There is no partial 14-day exclusion.

Unsupported rental rates. Retain evidence for the actual rental terms rather than choosing a rate to produce a desired deduction.

No business purpose. A personal or invented event does not establish an ordinary and necessary business expense. Entertainment limitations need separate attention.

Missing documentation. A calendar, written agreement, rate analysis and payment records support the facts. None guarantees eligibility by itself.

Renting to your own sole proprietorship. A sole proprietor or an LLC disregarded as separate from that owner cannot create a federal rent deduction by paying the owner for the owner's property. A separately taxed payer is needed for this own-business arrangement. The homeowner exclusion can still apply to qualifying rentals to unrelated guests without the owner forming a business.

Relationship to IRC 280A(d) Personal Use Rules

Section 280A(d) supplies the residence test needed for Section 280A(g); it is not switched off by staying below 15 rental days. The owner cannot claim rental-use deductions under the exclusion, and personal mortgage interest and real estate tax deductions remain subject to their usual rules.

If rental use reaches 15 days, determine whether the home is still treated as a residence, allocate personal and rental expenses, and apply the relevant deduction limits. Losing the exclusion does not automatically allow every expense or depreciation deduction.

Interaction with Self-Rental Rules Under IRC 469

The self-rental rules can recharacterize net rental income from property rented to a business in which the taxpayer materially participates. Qualifying Section 280A(g) rent is excluded from gross income, so that excluded amount is not passive rental income to recharacterize. If the exclusion fails, the self-rental and passive-activity analysis needs to be revisited. On the business side, Section 162 and other deduction restrictions still apply.

Who Benefits Most from the Augusta Rule?

S corporation owners with a qualifying residence and a genuine business rental may reduce pass-through income, subject to deduction limits and QBI effects. Active ordinary business income is not automatically subject to NIIT, and this is not a substitute for reasonable compensation.

C corporation owners may have a company-level rent deduction and an owner-level exclusion when both sets of requirements are met. Neither outcome follows merely from making a payment. Excessive or nonbusiness payments require separate treatment.

Partnership and multi-member LLC owners should confirm federal tax classification, ownership, related-party rules and the business purpose. A separate legal name by itself does not establish the deduction.

Real estate investors and other homeowners may use the exclusion for a qualifying residence rented to unrelated guests. A pure investment rental that fails the residence test does not qualify. Keep its depreciation and rental reporting separate from the qualifying residence.
