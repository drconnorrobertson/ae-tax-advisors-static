Tax Strategy for Dentists: Practice Structure, Equipment, and Retirement

Published on August 10, 2026 | Updated November 28, 2026 | Professional Tax Strategy | AE Tax Advisors

Tax planning for dentists starts with three records: the practice’s year-to-date results, the equipment installation schedule, and the ownership or lease documents for the office. Use each to decide what belongs on the practice return and what affects the owner’s return.

Compare the entity election with a defensible salary

Model business and owner taxes, payroll and administration under the current and proposed structures. For an S corporation, document clinical and management duties, time, and market comparisons. Check applicable state professional-entity rules before restructuring. See IRS compensation guidance.

Time dental equipment around installation and business use

Track the signed quote, invoice, financing, delivery, installation, acceptance testing and date the equipment is ready and available for use. For example, if a $150,000 scanner requires a $30,000 down payment, that is the initial purchase cash outflow before fees. The depreciable basis and any Section 179 or bonus deduction require a separate review of ownership, eligibility, business use and limits. Compare the remaining principal and projected cash costs before choosing financing.

Use the equipment lease-versus-buy calculator for the quotes and IRS Publication 946 for depreciation rules.

Identify who paid for the office improvements

Separate building ownership, tenant improvements and movable equipment. Reconcile the lease, landlord allowance, invoices and fixed-asset schedules. Specialized utilities and other components require property-specific classification support. See the cost segregation guide for medical and dental practices.

Model retirement funding and owner-level QBI together

Ask for a retirement-plan proposal that includes staff costs and an adverse cash-flow case. Review owner taxable income and the applicable health-services limits before assuming a QBI deduction. See the IRS QBI guidance.

What to bring to a dentist tax planning review

Bring federal and state returns, payroll and the staff census, ownership and lease documents, equipment quotes and service records, and the depreciation schedules for the practice and building. See the dental practice advisory scope.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a dentist deduct a new CBCT machine in full?

It depends on tax ownership, equipment eligibility, use, acquisition and service dates, and the applicable deduction limits. Confirm financing and readiness for patient use before claiming the deduction.

What salary should a dental practice owner take?

For an S corporation, document the owner’s clinical and management duties, time, experience, market comparisons and other compensation. The recommendation depends on the services provided and supporting facts.

Do dentists qualify for the QBI deduction?

Dentistry generally falls within health services. Check the owner’s taxable income, filing status, and applicable Section 199A limits before projecting a deduction.

How much does cost segregation recover on a dental building?

Use the property’s depreciable basis and an evidence-based asset schedule. Estimate usable deductions and sale consequences separately, and account for assets already recorded by the practice.

Is a cash balance plan worth it with fifteen employees?

Request an actuarial proposal showing eligibility, required contributions for owners and staff, fees and funding in a weaker year. Evaluate the total commitment against practice cash flow.

Discuss the practice, equipment and building together

Bring the equipment quotes, accounting records and building documents. Ask which decisions are included in the advisory engagement and how the study or filing work would be scoped.
