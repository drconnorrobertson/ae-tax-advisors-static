Tax Strategy for Professional Athletes: Jock Taxes, Signing Bonuses, and a Short Career

Published on August 11, 2026 | Tax Strategy | AE Tax Advisors

Multi-state tax planning addresses where a business owes tax, covering nexus, apportionment, sourcing, and residency, and it has become substantially more complex as remote employees and economic nexus thresholds create filing obligations in states where a business has no physical location.

A professional athlete has an income profile almost nobody else has: very high earnings compressed into a short window, taxed by every state the team travels to, followed by decades of retirement funded from that window.

Three things move the number more than anything else: where you establish residency, how your signing bonus is structured, and how much of the earning window goes into deferred and protected vehicles.

Duty Day Allocation and the Jock Tax

States tax nonresident athletes on income allocated to services performed in the state, generally using a duty day formula: total compensation multiplied by duty days in the state divided by total duty days.

Duty days typically include games, practices, team meetings, travel days, and preseason and postseason activity, though the definition varies by state. Some states include training camp days and some do not, which changes the denominator and therefore every state's allocation.

An athlete on a team playing in 20 states files roughly 20 nonresident returns plus a resident return, with credits claimed in the resident state for taxes paid elsewhere. Several cities impose their own taxes on top.

The resident state credit generally prevents true double taxation, but it does not eliminate the cost. Where a nonresident state's rate exceeds the resident state's rate, the excess is not recovered by the credit. And a resident of a no-tax state receives no credit benefit at all, since there is no resident tax to offset, which means every nonresident state's tax is a real cost rather than a wash.

Accurate duty day tracking is the foundation. Errors compound across every return, and states audit these allocations.

Residency Is the Largest Single Decision

For an athlete on a team in a no-tax state, or one who can establish residency in Florida, Texas, Nevada, Tennessee, or Washington, the savings on home games, off-season income, endorsement income, and investment income are substantial.

Endorsement and licensing income sourcing is more complex than salary. Income for personal appearances is generally sourced where the appearance occurred. Royalty income for the use of name, image, and likeness is often sourced to the residence state, which makes residency planning worth considerably more for an athlete with large endorsement revenue.

High-tax states audit departures aggressively. Domicile turns on where you actually live: family location, home ownership, vehicle and voter registration, physical presence, and where personal and professional connections sit. Day counting is verifiable through travel records and team schedules.

An athlete relocating should document the move thoroughly from the beginning and understand that the departure state will examine it.

Signing Bonus Structure

A properly structured signing bonus can be sourced entirely to the athlete's state of residence rather than allocated across duty days.

The generally accepted requirements are that the bonus is paid separately from salary, is not conditioned on the athlete performing subsequent services, and is nonrefundable.

Where those conditions are satisfied, the bonus is compensation for signing rather than for services performed in any state, and it is generally sourced to residence.

For an athlete residing in a no-tax state, that can mean a multi-million dollar bonus entirely free of state tax rather than allocated across a dozen taxing jurisdictions.

This is a contract drafting matter that must be handled at signing. It cannot be fixed afterward, and it is worth involving tax counsel alongside the agent before the deal is executed.

The Compressed Earning Window

An athlete earning $4,000,000 annually for six years must fund a lifetime from that window, and the tax planning should reflect the compression.

Deferred compensation arrangements under IRC Sec. 409A, where the league and collective bargaining agreement permit, can shift income into lower-rate years after the playing career ends. The deferral must comply strictly with Sec. 409A election timing and distribution rules, and violations trigger immediate inclusion plus a 20% additional tax.

A business entity for endorsement and appearance income, separate from team salary, creates earned income that supports a retirement plan. Team salary generally cannot fund an individual plan, but endorsement income through an S corporation or LLC can support a solo 401(k) and, for an athlete with substantial and sustained endorsement revenue, a cash balance plan.

Real estate is common and the mechanics matter. An athlete cannot qualify as a real estate professional under IRC Sec. 469(c)(7) during a playing career. Short-term rentals under the seven-day rule are the accessible route, since only material participation applies and an athlete has concentrated off-season availability.

Agent Fees, Training, and Deductions

Agent commissions, union dues, training and conditioning costs, and equipment are business expenses for an athlete treated as self-employed, but employee athletes cannot currently deduct unreimbursed expenses.

That distinction matters. Where an athlete is a team employee receiving a W-2, agent fees and training costs are not deductible under current law. Where endorsement and appearance income is earned through a business entity, expenses attributable to that activity are deductible against it.

This is another reason to separate endorsement income into an entity rather than reporting it alongside team compensation.

Worked Example: Mid-Career Contract

An athlete signs a four-year contract with $3,600,000 of annual salary and a $6,000,000 signing bonus, plus $900,000 of annual endorsement income. The team plays in a high-tax state.

The athlete establishes residency in Florida, documented through home purchase, family relocation, vehicle and voter registration, and day counting supported by team travel records.

The signing bonus is drafted as separate from salary, nonrefundable, and not conditioned on subsequent services, sourcing it to Florida. State tax avoided on the bonus alone approaches $500,000 relative to duty day allocation across taxing states.

Salary is allocated by duty day across the states played in, with nonresident returns filed in each. Because Florida imposes no tax, no resident credit offsets those amounts, making accurate duty day tracking directly valuable.

Endorsement income flows through an S corporation with a $300,000 salary, funding a solo 401(k) at $70,000 and allowing deduction of agent commissions and training costs attributable to that activity.

A short-term rental acquired in the off-season, self-managed with documented material participation, produces a cost segregation deduction that offsets endorsement income.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the jock tax?

State taxation of nonresident athletes on income allocated to services performed in the state, generally using a duty day formula. An athlete on a team playing in 20 states files roughly 20 nonresident returns plus a resident return, with several cities imposing their own taxes.

Can a signing bonus avoid state allocation?

Often yes, where it is paid separately from salary, is nonrefundable, and is not conditioned on performing subsequent services. It is then compensation for signing rather than for services in any state, and is generally sourced to residence. This must be drafted at signing.

Does moving to a no-tax state help?

Substantially, for home games, off-season income, endorsement royalties, and investment income. But it also means no resident credit offsets nonresident state taxes, so duty day accuracy becomes more important, not less. Departure states audit these moves aggressively.

Can an athlete deduct agent fees?

Not against W-2 team compensation, since unreimbursed employee expenses are not currently deductible. Where endorsement and appearance income is earned through a business entity, expenses attributable to that activity are deductible against it, which is a reason to separate the income.

How can an athlete fund a retirement plan?

Team salary generally cannot fund an individual plan. Endorsement and appearance income earned through an S corporation or LLC creates the earned income needed to fund a solo 401(k), and with sustained revenue, a cash balance plan.
