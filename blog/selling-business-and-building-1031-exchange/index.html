Selling Your Business and Building Together: Can You 1031 the Real Estate?

Selling your business and its building together? Separate ownership, asset allocation, exchange funds and tax reserves before agreeing to the closing terms.

By AE Tax Advisors Team · Published November 6, 2026

The short answer: separate the property exchange from the business sale

You may be able to sell your operating business and exchange qualifying real estate in the same overall transaction. The full combined price does not automatically qualify for Section 1031. Start with the tax owner of each asset, what the buyer is actually purchasing, and a supportable allocation. Then coordinate the exchange documents and funds flow before closing.

Section 1031 requires qualifying real property held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment. Property held primarily for sale does not qualify. Review the intended use and holding facts for the relinquished and replacement property, not just their commercial zoning.

This guide is for owners of an established business who also own its premises, such as a practice building, workshop or commercial facility. A single offer, buyer or closing date does not merge the assets into one tax item. The IRS explains that a business asset sale generally requires separate gain or loss calculations for the assets transferred. Read the IRS sale-of-business guidance.

The useful pre-signing deliverable is a transaction map: who sells each asset, which portion is taxable, which property may be exchanged, and which cash remains available after debt, costs and tax reserves. If the offer is already signed, provide the current documents and closing date so the team can identify choices that remain open.

1. Confirm who owns the building and what is being sold

Put the deed beside the entity returns and ownership chart. A property LLC, an operating S corporation and the individual owner may be different taxpayers. The business seller cannot simply direct another taxpayer’s gain into its exchange. Identify the taxpayer disposing of the real estate and the intended tax owner of the replacement property before selecting the titleholder.

An ordinary corporate stock sale transfers shares, not the shareholder’s real estate. A sale of a partnership interest generally is not an exchange of the partnership’s building. A disregarded single-member LLC is different: a transfer of its interests can be treated as an asset transaction for income-tax purposes. Even then, the exchange conditions must be met. IRS Publication 3402 also illustrates why the seller’s and buyer’s tax treatment can differ when partnership ownership changes. A buyer receiving asset treatment does not by itself establish exchange eligibility for the seller.

Have the tax team evaluate any deemed-asset election or narrow exception rather than assuming the contract’s label controls. If retaining or separating a corporate building is being considered, review the C-corporation distribution issues or S-corporation distribution reporting before moving title.

2. Support the allocation before treating any amount as exchange proceeds

Use a valuation-supported schedule, not a percentage selected to minimize the seller’s tax. The buyer and seller can have different preferences because the allocation affects the buyer’s basis and the seller’s income character. For an applicable asset acquisition, the residual-method rules and fair-value limits govern the allocation. See the Form 8594 allocation instructions and AE’s business purchase-price allocation guide.

The mixed transaction has a specific reporting rule: the Form 8594 instructions say that when Section 1031 does not apply to all transferred business assets, Form 8594 is required for the part to which the exchange rules do not apply. The return preparers must determine the actual filing parties and covered asset group. A separately sold building alone does not automatically trigger Form 8594.

Assign every asset to one owner and one schedule. Tenant-owned improvements, removable equipment and landlord-owned building systems need particular attention so the same asset is not counted in both the business sale and the property exchange. Reconcile later price adjustments with the original allocation and determine whether supplemental reporting is required.

3. Arrange the exchange and closing funds before the property transfers

A business-sale escrow and a qualified-intermediary arrangement serve different purposes. Have deal counsel, the tax adviser, the qualified intermediary (QI) and the closing agent agree on written instructions covering the intended exchange property, contract assignments, payment recipients and restrictions on access to funds. The QI must satisfy the applicable rules; a familiar adviser is not automatically eligible.

  • Before the transfer: resolve the exchanger’s identity, qualified property, allocation, exchange agreement and funds flow. Keep the intended exchange proceeds from becoming available to the seller outside the permitted arrangement.
  • After transfer: identify replacement property in the required signed writing within 45 days.
  • Completion deadline: receive the replacement property by the earlier of 180 days after transfer or the due date of the exchanger’s return, including extensions. A late-year exchange can require attention to the return extension.

Receiving the intended exchange money actually or constructively can make the transaction taxable. Receiving cash first and then depositing it with a QI is not a reliable way to turn a completed cash sale into an exchange. The QI’s involvement also does not excuse a missed deadline. Publication 544 explains receipt restrictions and QI safe harbors; the Form 8824 instructions explain the deadlines.

Keep a separate forecast for cash or other non-like-kind property received and for liabilities relieved or assumed. Net debt relief can affect recognized gain. Do not reduce the decision to “reinvest the profit”: proceeds, gain, adjusted basis, debt and replacement-property basis are different figures. Use the debt-relief and boot guide for that calculation, and calendar the transaction with the team before approving closing instructions.

4. Check old cost segregation and current recapture exposure

Bring the original acquisition records, every depreciation schedule, improvement invoices, cost-segregation report and any Form 3115 adjustment. A combined sale requires asset-level basis and valuation, not just a building balance from the financial statements.

Section 1031 real-property eligibility and depreciation recapture are separate tests. Some Section 1245 property can qualify as real property under the exchange regulations. The Form 8824 instructions for line 21 expressly discuss Section 1245 real property and give examples of ordinary-income recapture in an exchange. Prior cost segregation therefore neither automatically disqualifies the property nor guarantees that all gain can be deferred.

Have the model identify eligible real property, any nonqualifying assets, ordinary-income recapture, other recognized gain and deferred gain. Retain the replacement-property basis and recapture records for later years. A study’s old classification or a single estimated capital-gains rate is not a substitute for this disposition analysis.

If part of the business price will be paid over time, analyze the seller note separately. Installment treatment does not automatically defer depreciation recapture; Publication 537 requires applicable recapture income to be reported in the sale year. Keep that tax reserve separate from cash expected in later years.

5. Separate a $5 million offer from cash available to the owner

Hypothetical illustration, not a valuation, tax computation or client result. Assume an all-cash offer allocates $3,000,000 to operating assets and $2,000,000 to real estate, subject to a defensible final allocation. Assume the real-estate closing pays $600,000 of debt and $100,000 of costs from its $2,000,000 gross amount. The following arithmetic is only a funds-flow starting point.

The closing team must determine whether and how the property cash moves into the exchange arrangement. Do not treat the $1,300,000 as both a replacement-property funding source and money available to the owner. The $600,000 debt relief and the treatment of the $100,000 costs still belong in the exchange calculation, together with replacement-property value and financing, any cash retained, adjusted basis and recapture.

Prepare a separate tax-reserve schedule for each seller. A corporation’s sale proceeds are not automatically the shareholder’s spendable cash; a subsequent distribution can have its own consequences. The combined offer is useful for negotiation, but the owner needs a reconciliation from each entity’s closing funds to after-tax amounts actually available.

Run a downside case alongside the expected outcome. A weak replacement investment can outweigh a deferral benefit. A retained building can produce less usable cash than its gross rent suggests. The retained-building decision guide covers that separate lease and ownership choice.

Use the pre-LOI review agenda while terms remain negotiable. State income taxes, transfer taxes, lender consents and legal restrictions also need separate review; a federal exchange analysis does not settle them.

7. Bring a decision file to the advisory review

The first discussion should establish the transaction stage, entities, open choices and work still needed. Gather:

  • The current LOI, purchase-agreement drafts, asset exhibits and proposed closing dates.
  • Deeds, ownership percentages, entity tax classifications, prior reorganizations and any proposed tax elections.
  • Recent entity and owner returns, asset-level basis and depreciation schedules, cost-segregation reports and Form 3115 history.
  • Appraisals or other valuation support, debt statements, guarantees, closing-cost estimates and contingent-payment terms.
  • The proposed QI agreement, replacement-property plan and a draft closing funds flow if an exchange is being considered.

Ask for the engagement to identify its written deliverables: an ownership and asset map, supported allocation questions, a sale/exchange tax projection, cash-reserve assumptions and a responsibility list. Confirm who handles legal documents, the QI arrangement, valuations, entity returns, owner returns and post-closing adjustments. These are scope questions, not a representation that every service is included in one advisory engagement.

Use the agreed secure channel for detailed returns and transaction documents. For the initial conversation, the property and business type, entity structure, expected timing and decision you need to make are a practical starting point.

8. Questions before a combined closing

Can the entire combined business and property sale price go into a 1031 exchange?

No. Section 1031 applies to qualifying real property held for business or investment, not automatically to the operating business or every asset sold with the building. Identify each tax owner and asset, support the allocation, and separately calculate the taxable portion and exchange portion.

Do we still need Form 8594 if the building is exchanged?

For a covered transfer of business assets, yes for the portion to which Section 1031 does not apply. The Form 8594 instructions expressly address a group of assets that is only partly exchanged. Your preparers must determine the filing obligations for the actual sellers and buyers; selling a building alone does not automatically make every transaction a Form 8594 acquisition.

Does prior cost segregation prevent the building from qualifying for an exchange?

Not automatically. Apply the Section 1031 definition of real property to the assets, then separately test depreciation recapture. Some Section 1245 property can be real property for exchange purposes, and an exchange can still produce ordinary-income recapture. Bring the study and asset-level depreciation schedules to the review.

Can I receive the real estate proceeds and set up the exchange afterward?

Receiving the intended exchange proceeds, actually or constructively, can make the transaction taxable. Arrange the exchange documents and restrictions on funds before the relevant transfer. The 45-day identification period does not provide a window to receive the cash first and turn a completed cash sale into an exchange.

If my LLC owns the building, can I exchange the sale of my LLC interest?

The LLC label does not decide the answer. A partnership-interest sale generally does not qualify, even though the partnership owns real estate. A disregarded single-member LLC can have different asset-sale treatment. Confirm federal classification, the seller-side treatment and any proposed election before relying on a buyer’s asset treatment or making exchange commitments.
