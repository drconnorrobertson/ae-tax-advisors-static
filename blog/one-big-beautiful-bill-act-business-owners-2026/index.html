What the One Big Beautiful Bill Act Means for Business Owners in 2026

Published on June 23, 2026 | Business Tax Strategy | AE Tax Advisors

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law in 2025, is the most significant overhaul of the U.S. tax code since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017. For business owners, real estate investors, and high-income professionals, it changes nearly every major planning lever, bonus depreciation, the QBI deduction, SALT limits, estate planning, and more.

The challenge is that the changes are not uniform. Some provisions are immediately favorable. Others require you to rethink strategies you may have built around prior law. And a number of planning opportunities that were time-sensitive are now permanent, which changes the urgency calculus entirely.

Here is what you need to understand and act on.

100% Bonus Depreciation Is Back, Permanently

Under the TCJA, 100% bonus depreciation was set to phase down beginning in 2023. By 2025, the rate had dropped to 40%, with 20% scheduled for 2026 and complete elimination in 2027. The OBBBA reversed that phase-down entirely.

The restored 100% bonus rate generally applies to eligible property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, subject to acquisition, binding-contract, and other qualification rules. There is no scheduled sunset for this provision under current law. Older acquisitions can remain subject to prior-law percentages, so the in-service year alone is not enough. See IRS Publication 946.

For real estate investors, cost segregation studies identify components that may qualify for 5-, 7-, or 15-year depreciation instead of the building's 27.5- or 39-year schedule. Eligible components can qualify for bonus depreciation when the acquisition and other requirements are met. Land and the building shell do not become bonus-eligible merely because a study is performed.

Illustration, not a client result: a study of a $1.5 million rental acquisition might support $400,000 to $500,000 of eligible shorter-life basis after land and other allocations. If that basis meets the restored 100% bonus rules, the same $400,000 to $500,000 could be accelerated before owner-level limits. A passive rental loss generally cannot offset wages or active business income simply because the deduction is large; basis, at-risk, passive-activity, and other loss rules determine whether it can be used now or carried forward.

Before buying a study, compare its fee and implementation cost with the timing value of usable deductions, expected holding period, state conformity, and later sale or recapture taxes. For an older property, the preparer must determine the correct correction method and historical bonus rate. A new study does not automatically convert an old acquisition into 100% bonus property.

The 20% QBI Deduction Is Permanent; Phase-In Ranges Widen

The OBBBA made the qualified business income deduction under IRC Section 199A permanent. The percentage remains up to 20%; there is no 23% QBI rate in the enacted rules. The IRS QBI guidance explains the calculation and taxable-income limit, along with the minimum deduction available to qualifying active businesses for tax years beginning after 2025.

For a hypothetical owner with $500,000 of QBI, 20% produces a preliminary $100,000 deduction before wage/property, SSTB, loss-netting, and taxable-income limits. It does not produce a $115,000 deduction or an automatic additional $15,000 benefit. A deduction reduces taxable income; it is not a dollar-for-dollar tax credit.

The 2026 wage/property limitation phases in over taxable income, before the QBI deduction, from $201,750 to $276,750 for single filers and other non-joint/non-separate returns, or $403,500 to $553,500 for joint filers. Married filing separately uses $201,775 to $276,775. These are $75,000 and $150,000 phase-in widths, respectively. SSTB eligibility phases out across the applicable range. The existing 50%-of-wages or 25%-of-wages-plus-2.5%-of-UBIA formulas still matter. See Revenue Procedure 2025-32, section 4.26.

For S-Corp owners, the work is to model reasonable compensation, payroll taxes, qualified income, and the phased-in wage limit together. Salary must reflect services performed; it is not a free variable chosen only for a tax result. Qualified retirement-plan deductions generally can reduce QBI as well as taxable income, so a threshold strategy needs both calculations.

The 2026 SALT Cap Is $40,400 Before the Income Phase-Down

The OBBBA raised the individual state and local tax (SALT) deduction cap to $40,000 for 2025, with a $500,000 MAGI threshold. Those are the historical first-year figures. For 2026, the cap is $40,400 and the MAGI phase-down threshold is $505,000. Married filing separately uses a $20,200 cap and $252,500 threshold. The higher-cap regime is temporary through 2029 under current law; do not treat it as a permanent $40,000 allowance.

A non-separate filer who itemizes, has $35,000 of otherwise deductible SALT, and stays below the applicable MAGI threshold could deduct that $35,000 rather than being restricted by the old $10,000 cap. The deduction is still limited by taxes actually eligible and paid. Compare total itemized deductions with the standard deduction and consider AMT and the overall itemized-deduction limitation where applicable.

For a joint or other non-separate filer above $505,000 MAGI, reduce the $40,400 cap by 30% of the excess, but not below $10,000. For married filing separately, use the $252,500 threshold in that calculation and then halve the resulting cap and floor: start with $40,400, subtract 30% of excess MAGI, apply the $10,000 floor, and divide by two. This produces the $20,200 maximum and $5,000 floor; do not apply the 30% reduction directly to an already-halved cap. For example, a joint filer at $550,000 MAGI has $45,000 of excess income: the $13,500 reduction leaves a $26,900 cap before considering actual eligible taxes and other limits. For comparison, a separate filer at $275,000 MAGI has $22,500 of excess: ($40,400 − $6,750) ÷ 2 = a $16,825 cap. These are cap calculations, not the amount of taxes actually deductible. The IRS 2026 Form 1040-ES correction confirms the current caps, thresholds, and floors.

The increase also affects pass-through entity (PTE) tax elections, which many states adopted as SALT workarounds under prior law. With the individual SALT cap now higher, the benefit calculation for PTE elections shifts. In some cases, the PTE election may still be the better option; in others, it may no longer be necessary. This is state-specific and depends on your income level and entity structure.

The Estate Tax Exemption Is Permanently Doubled

The TCJA temporarily doubled the estate and gift tax exemption from approximately $5 million to $10 million (adjusted for inflation). That temporary increase was set to sunset after 2025, which would have cut the exemption roughly in half. The OBBBA made the doubled exemption permanent.

For 2026, the federal estate and gift tax basic exclusion is $15 million per individual. A married couple may be able to use a combined $30 million, but portability of a deceased spouse's unused exclusion requires the applicable estate-return election; it is not automatic. Prior taxable gifts, citizenship and residency, and state estate or inheritance taxes also affect the plan.

This does not mean estate planning is less urgent. It means it is more plannable. Business owners with significant assets should now focus on strategies that take advantage of the permanent high exemption, including gifting programs, grantor trusts, family limited partnerships, and life insurance structures. The goal shifts from exemption preservation to efficient wealth transfer at the lowest tax cost.

Section 179 Expensing Enhanced for Equipment Purchases

For 2026, the Section 179 deduction limit is $2,560,000 and its investment phaseout begins above $4,090,000 of qualifying property placed in service. The dollar ceiling reaches zero at $6,650,000, before considering the separate active-business-income limit. The 2026 covered-SUV cap is $32,000. These figures come from Revenue Procedure 2025-32; a qualifying purchase is not by itself a currently usable deduction.

Section 179 provides asset-by-asset control and may differ from bonus depreciation under state law. It does not override passenger-auto limits or the covered-SUV cap. Review tax ownership, business use above 50%, vehicle specifications, acquisition and in-service dates, and the remaining basis available for bonus depreciation before choosing either method. See the 2026 Section 179 guide for worked examples.

What to Do Right Now

The OBBBA creates both immediate opportunities and longer-term planning implications. Here is how to approach it systematically.

If you own investment real estate: Gather acquisition contracts, closing allocations, depreciation schedules, use records, and expected sale timing. Ask your advisor to estimate usable deductions and total study/filing cost before commissioning work. Have the preparer determine whether Form 3115, an amended return, or no change is appropriate; this guide is not an instruction to file.

If you operate a pass-through business: Request a 2026 QBI calculation using the 20% rate, current phase-in ranges, qualified income after business deductions, wage/property limits, and SSTB status. Compare reasonable salary and retirement scenarios on total tax and cash cost, not the QBI deduction alone.

If you are in a high-tax state: Model the $40,400 cap, $505,000 MAGI threshold, 30% phase-down, and $10,000 floor for 2026. For separate filers, use the $252,500 threshold and halve the resulting cap after the phase-down. Compare state PTE elections, credits, QBI effects, payment timing, and itemization before changing an existing election strategy.

If your estate exceeds $5 million: The permanent doubled exemption is your signal to move from reactive planning to proactive wealth transfer. Work with your advisors to implement a gifting strategy, review any existing trusts, and ensure your business succession plan is aligned with your estate plan.

Timing still matters, but a larger deduction does not make an unnecessary purchase or an unaffordable commitment worthwhile. Ask for a written projection that states assumptions, documents eligibility, and compares current cash needs with later tax consequences.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the One Big Beautiful Bill Act permanently restore 100% bonus depreciation?

Yes, current law permanently restores the 100% rate for eligible property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. The acquisition and binding-contract rules, property qualification, and elections still matter. Older acquisitions may remain under prior-law percentages; a new cost segregation study does not reset the acquisition date.

How does the OBBBA change the QBI deduction for business owners?

The deduction is permanent at up to 20%, not 23%. For 2026, wage/property limitations phase in from $201,750 to $276,750 for single filers, $403,500 to $553,500 for joint filers, and $201,775 to $276,775 for married filing separately. SSTB eligibility phases out across those ranges. Taxable-income and other limits still apply, and qualifying active businesses may be eligible for the new minimum deduction.

What happened to the SALT deduction cap under the new law?

The 2025 cap was $40,000 with a $500,000 MAGI threshold. For 2026, the cap is $40,400 and phase-down begins above $505,000 MAGI; married filing separately uses $20,200 and $252,500. For non-separate filers, reduce $40,400 by 30% of excess MAGI with a $10,000 floor. For separate filers, use the $252,500 threshold in that full-cap calculation and then halve the result, yielding a $20,200 maximum and $5,000 floor. Actual eligible taxes, itemization, and other limits determine the deduction; the higher-cap regime lasts through 2029 under current law.

Should I still pursue a cost segregation study if bonus depreciation is now 100%?

Possibly. A cost segregation study can identify eligible shorter-life components, but acquisition dates, loss limits, study and filing fees, state treatment, and expected sale taxes determine whether it is worthwhile. Confirm that the accelerated deduction can actually be used and compare the after-tax timing benefit before ordering a study.

How does the OBBBA affect estate planning for high-net-worth business owners?

The 2026 federal basic exclusion is $15 million per individual. A couple may be able to use $30 million combined, but portability requires the applicable estate-return election and is not automatic. Prior gifts and state taxes matter. Review succession and estate documents with legal and tax advisors before making gifts or changing trusts.
