How Do I Maximize Retirement Contributions as an S-Corp Owner?

Published on August 16, 2026 | Retirement Plans | AE Tax Advisors

S-Corporation owners face a unique planning challenge when it comes to retirement contributions, your W-2 salary directly determines how much you can contribute to employer-sponsored retirement plans, but it also determines how much you pay in payroll taxes. Setting the salary too low reduces retirement plan capacity. Setting it too high increases payroll tax costs. Maximizing retirement contributions requires finding the optimal balance between salary, profit distributions, and plan design.

The W-2 Salary Foundation

As an S-Corp owner-employee, you must pay yourself a "reasonable salary" that is subject to FICA taxes (Social Security and Medicare) under IRC Section 3121. The IRS has been aggressive about challenging S-Corp owners who pay themselves unreasonably low salaries to avoid payroll taxes. While there is no statutory formula for "reasonable compensation," it should reflect the fair market value of the services you provide to the business.

S corporation distributions are not retirement-plan compensation. Employee deferrals and employer profit-sharing contributions depend on eligible W-2 compensation and plan terms. For an owner-only illustration, 25% of an $80,000 salary is $20,000 and 25% of a $200,000 salary is $50,000, but those percentage calculations must still fit the annual-additions limit after employee deferrals. A solo 401(k) requires no eligible common-law employees other than a spouse; staffed businesses must cover eligible employees and satisfy applicable testing.

Optimizing Salary for Maximum Plan Contributions

Start with reasonable compensation for the work performed; there is no universal optimal salary range. For 2026, employee elective deferrals are limited to $24,500 and annual additions to a defined contribution plan to $72,000 before eligible catch-up contributions. The standard age-50 catch-up is $8,000, while participants attaining ages 60 through 63 may qualify for $11,250 instead. Social Security tax applies only up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500, while Medicare continues above it. Model the combined payroll-tax, income-tax, QBI and plan effect.

Assume an under-50 owner has $200,000 of eligible W-2 compensation, no deferrals to another employer's plan and no other annual additions. A $24,500 elective deferral leaves $47,500 of room under the $72,000 annual-additions limit, even though 25% of salary is $50,000. The supported total is therefore $72,000, not $74,500. Eligible catch-up can be added outside that cap: up to $80,000 with the standard catch-up or $83,250 for an eligible participant attaining age 60 through 63. Plan terms and testing can permit less.

Adding a Cash Balance Plan

A cash balance defined benefit plan may support additional funding, but an actuary must calculate it from plan terms, age, compensation, employee census and existing benefits. The 2026 defined-benefit annual benefit limit is $290,000; it is not an annual contribution allowance. Review cash commitments and employee costs before changing salary to fund a plan.

Combining a cash balance plan with a 401(k) does not automatically make the full contributions to both deductible. Section 404(a)(7), PBGC status and the interaction of employer contributions can limit the combined deduction. Obtain a coordinated calculation from the actuary and plan administrator rather than adding generic contribution estimates by age.

The Payroll Tax Trade-Off

Medicare tax generally applies to all W-2 wages at 1.45% each for the employee and employer. Social Security is 6.2% each up to the 2026 $184,500 wage base. Additional Medicare Tax is employee-only at 0.9%, using household thresholds of $200,000 for single/head-of-household filers, $250,000 for married filing jointly and $125,000 for married filing separately. Employer withholding above $200,000 of wages is a separate rule, not a universal final-tax threshold.

Illustration: assume a single owner has no other wages or self-employment income and compare $150,000 with $250,000 of salary in 2026. Combined employer/employee Social Security rises by $4,278 (12.4% of $34,500); regular Medicare rises by $2,900; and employee Additional Medicare Tax rises by $450 (0.9% of $50,000). The combined employment-tax increase is $7,628 before income-tax effects of employer deductions, QBI and plan funding. Do not compare this with a supposed extra retirement deduction until the annual-additions and combined-plan limits have been applied.

Employer Contributions as a Business Deduction

Employer contributions are deductible only within the qualified-plan and Section 404 limits. Allowed deductions generally reduce S corporation pass-through income and may also reduce QBI. Employee Roth deferrals and Roth catch-up do not provide a current income-tax deduction. Starting in 2026, catch-up generally must be Roth when prior-year wages under IRC Section 3121(a) from the sponsoring employer exceed $150,000, subject to the applicable rules. Do not assume all active S corporation income was otherwise subject to the 3.8% NIIT.

Timing Contributions

Arrange employee deferral elections and payroll withholding before the applicable compensation is paid; do not retroactively label shareholder distributions as deferrals. Coordinate timely deposits with the plan administrator. Employer contribution and deduction deadlines depend on the plan and return rules, including extensions where allowed. A December payroll adjustment must respect compensation, the $24,500 individual deferral limit, other plans and any Roth catch-up requirement.

Model reasonable compensation, eligible employee coverage, individual deferrals, the $72,000 annual-additions ceiling and any catch-up or combined-plan limits together. Contribute only an amount supported by the plan and the owner's cash flow. Salary and contribution targets should follow the actual services, records and calculation, not a blanket percentage or income threshold.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does S-Corp salary interact with the QBI deduction?

Above the Section 199A taxable income thresholds, the deduction for a non-service business is limited by W-2 wages. Reducing your salary to save payroll tax can therefore reduce the QBI deduction by more than the payroll tax saved, so both effects must be modeled together.

Is there a 60/40 rule for S-Corp salary?

No. There is no safe harbor percentage in the Code, regulations, or IRS guidance. Reasonable compensation is a facts-and-circumstances determination measured against what comparable businesses pay for comparable services, using the nine factors summarized in IRS Fact Sheet FS-2008-25.

What happens if my S-Corp salary is too low?

The IRS recharacterizes distributions as wages, producing back employment taxes for both halves, failure to deposit and failure to file penalties, accuracy-related penalties, and interest across all open years. Adjustments spanning three years frequently exceed six figures.

How much does an S-Corp election save?

There is no fixed percentage or income threshold that establishes net savings. Compare supported reasonable compensation, other wages, the Social Security wage base, Medicare and Additional Medicare Tax, employer payroll-tax deductions, QBI, retirement-plan effects and compliance costs. Distributions are not retirement-plan compensation, and retirement deductions do not automatically reduce payroll tax.

Does a higher S corporation salary increase my retirement contribution limit?

Often, because S corporation distributions are not earned compensation for retirement-plan purposes. A higher W-2 salary can increase an employer contribution limit, but also changes payroll tax, QBI, and plan testing. Model the net result rather than maximizing salary automatically.

For the underlying rules, see the official tax guidance. The relevant tax year, entity documents, actual transactions, and state filings determine the result.
