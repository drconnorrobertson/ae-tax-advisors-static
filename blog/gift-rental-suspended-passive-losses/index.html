If I gift my rental to my child, can I deduct its suspended passive losses first?

Published September 24, 2026 | Real Estate Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Generally, no. Giving away an interest in a passive rental activity does not turn its unused Section 469 passive losses into a deduction for the donor or a loss carryforward for the child. The federal gift rule instead increases the basis of the transferred interest by the unused passive losses allocated to it immediately before the gift.

That distinction can change whether a planned family transfer achieves the owner's tax objective. Before signing a deed or transferring an entity interest, AE can reconcile the activity-level loss ledger and basis schedule, then compare the actual proposed transfer with other bona fide options. The answer depends on the ownership and transaction documents, not merely the property's current value.

Identify the loss before applying the gift rule

A number carried on a rental workpaper is not automatically an unused passive loss. First reconcile the original Schedule E or pass-through K-1, the owner's tax basis and at-risk limits, any passive income already absorbed, applicable rental-real-estate special allowance, prior Form 8582 workpapers, and the current year up to the transfer date. The IRS Form 8582 instructions distinguish passive activity carryforwards from amounts limited by other rules. A basis- or at-risk-suspended loss should not be put into the Section 469 gift calculation merely because it appears on a spreadsheet labeled “suspended.”

The activity also matters. If the owner has grouped multiple rentals as one activity under a valid election, a single property's balance may not be the complete activity balance. If only a fractional interest is gifted, the unused losses allocable to that transferred interest must be established; do not add the entire portfolio carryforward to one deed. A pass-through interest may require both entity-level asset records and owner-level basis schedules.

What changes on the donor's and child's tax records?

IRS Publication 925, “Dispositions by gift,” states that unused passive activity losses allocable to an interest given away cannot be deducted in any tax year. Instead, the transferred interest's basis is increased by those losses. This is an income-tax basis adjustment, not cash paid by the child, a new depreciation study, or permission to list the donor's unused losses as the child's Form 8582 carryforward. The child's gain, loss, and depreciation basis still require the usual gifted-property analysis under IRS Publication 551, including the fair-market-value limitation for determining a later loss when value is below donor basis.

The adjustment has to be documented in the donor's final activity workpaper and handed to the recipient with the original acquisition allocation, improvements, depreciation allowed or allowable, gift-date value, and transfer documents. Land and depreciable assets cannot be merged into one unsupported number. If the child later rents the property, the child needs a defensible beginning depreciation schedule; the gift does not reset every asset to the property's appraised value. AE's gifted-rental basis guide covers that recipient-side question in more detail.

Worked example: a deduction becomes a basis adjustment

Assume a parent directly owns one passive rental activity, has an adjusted tax basis of $420,000 in the interest immediately before a proposed outright gift, and has $80,000 of unused losses solely limited under Section 469 and allocated to that interest. Assume no current-year passive income or special allowance uses any of the $80,000 before transfer, and no other basis or at-risk issue changes the number. Under the gift rule, the parent does not deduct $80,000 on the gift-year return. Instead, the transferred interest's basis is increased to $500,000 immediately before the gift. That increased basis enters the child's gifted-basis workpaper, subject to other applicable basis rules.

This example is not a promise that the child receives an $80,000 current depreciation deduction or that a later $80,000 tax benefit is guaranteed. The basis must be allocated correctly, later depreciation follows its own rules, and a gift-date fair-market value below adjusted basis can create separate gain and loss bases. If the parent's “$80,000” contains losses disallowed by basis or at-risk rules, losses already used against passive income, or losses belonging to another grouped activity, the example's starting figure is wrong.

Compare the transaction before transferring title

  • Outright gift. No fully taxable sale occurs merely because title changes. Apply the Section 469 gift basis adjustment to the transferred interest, then analyze the recipient's basis and gift-reporting obligations separately.
  • Taxable sale to an unrelated buyer. A disposition of the owner's entire passive activity in a transaction recognizing all realized gain or loss can release unused passive losses under different rules. It can also create taxable gain, depreciation recapture, and closing costs. This is a comparison, not an instruction to replace a family gift with a sale.
  • Sale or part-sale to a child. Related-party status, consideration, assumed debt, and a bargain-sale component can change both the disposition and gift analyses. Do not assume that writing “sale” on a family agreement satisfies the unrelated-buyer release condition.
  • Transfer of an LLC or partnership interest. Determine what legal interest changed hands, what liabilities moved, and how the entity and owner-level basis records reconcile before applying a direct-deed example.

For the separate taxable-sale release rules, see AE's rental-sale suspended-loss guide. Death has another rule and should not be substituted for the gift calculation; AE addresses that in the death and basis step-up guide.

Documents to gather before the gift and return review

  • Prior returns, Schedule E, Forms 8582, and the year-by-year loss ledger for each rental activity.
  • Tax-basis and at-risk schedules, including any pass-through K-1s and entity-interest basis worksheets.
  • Original settlement statement, land/building allocation, capital-improvement invoices, fixed-asset and depreciation schedules, and any cost-segregation report.
  • Grouping-election records, ownership percentages, and documents identifying the interest actually being transferred.
  • Proposed deed or transfer agreement, debt and consideration details, gift-date valuation, and proposed recipient accounting schedule.

Failure points to catch before the deed is signed

  • Deducting a passive carryforward because the property left the return. A gift is not the unrelated, fully taxable disposition required for the general full-release rule.
  • Carrying the parent's Form 8582 balance onto the child's return. The gift rule changes basis instead of transferring the deduction.
  • Using an unverified loss total. Basis, at-risk, passive, and grouped-activity balances are different ledgers.
  • Ignoring assumed debt or partial payment. A transfer that is not a pure gift can require a different transaction analysis.
  • Resetting depreciable basis to appraisal value. The basis adjustment must be reconciled with the gift and depreciation rules, not booked as a new fair-market-value purchase.
