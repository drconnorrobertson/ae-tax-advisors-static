S-Corp to C-Corp Conversion: Model Distributions and Exit First

By AE Tax Advisors · Published 2026-07-19

Updated November 8, 2026.

Changing from S corporation to C corporation taxation can alter when and where income is taxed. The 21% federal corporate rate is only one part of the comparison. Model cash paid to owners, benefit costs, state taxes, retained earnings and the eventual sale before revoking the election.

Compare complete cash flows

A C corporation generally pays tax on its taxable income. Shareholders may then owe tax on dividends or other distributions under the applicable rules. Wages have their own deduction, payroll and reasonable-compensation requirements.

An S corporation generally passes its tax items to shareholders whether or not cash is distributed. The comparison therefore depends on annual owner cash needs, business reinvestment, QBI, losses, benefits and exit structure—not simply a 37% personal rate minus 21%.

Check what happens to existing stock

Revoking S status does not retroactively turn existing S corporation shares into qualified small business stock. Section 1202 has original-issuance, issuer, active-business, shareholder, holding-period and other requirements. New issuances and reorganizations need their own analysis.

The 2025 law changed certain QSBS limits and holding-period rules, so the stock's acquisition and issuance dates matter. Do not rely on a universal five-year/$10 million description for every issuance or assume the exemption removes corporate tax on an asset sale.

Understand the revocation procedure

The IRS requires a revocation statement and consent from shareholders holding more than half of the issued and outstanding shares, including nonvoting shares. Confirm the required information, signatures, filing address and intended effective date.

A revocation filed by the applicable third-month deadline can generally take effect at the start of that tax year. A later filing generally has a different default effective date, and a valid prospective date can be specified. Read the current IRS instructions rather than assuming any requested retroactive date is allowed.

A midyear termination can create short S and C tax periods. A revocation effective on the first day of the year does not automatically require the same split-year treatment. Income allocation and filing requirements follow the actual effective date.

Preserve S-period records

Track shareholder stock and debt basis, accumulated adjustments, earnings and profits, loss carryforwards and distributions. Post-termination distributions can have special treatment and deadlines; the AAA balance alone is not an unlimited tax-free distribution allowance.

Retirement plans, medical benefits, payroll and accounting methods also require review. C status does not excuse benefit-plan nondiscrimination or make all owner expenses deductible.

Model a later reversal and sale

Re-electing S status is generally restricted for five tax years after termination unless IRS consent applies. A later C-to-S change can introduce built-in-gains and earnings-and-profits issues. Do not describe the original S-to-C conversion itself as the same built-in-gains tax event.

Compare stock sale, asset sale, liquidation and continued ownership. Corporate and shareholder taxes can produce materially different outcomes. State treatment may differ from the federal result.

Prepare the decision before signing

  • Forecast owner wages, distributions and retained business cash.
  • Reconcile basis, AAA, earnings and profits and carryforwards.
  • Identify stock history and any proposed new issuance.
  • Compare likely exit forms and holding periods.
  • Confirm revocation consent, effective date and filing obligations.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does revoking S status turn my old shares into QSBS?

No. Existing S shares do not automatically acquire Section 1202 eligibility through revocation.

Will I always file two short-year returns?

No. Split-year treatment depends on the effective date and applicable termination rules.

Can I freely switch back next year?

A later S election generally faces a five-tax-year restriction unless IRS consent applies.
