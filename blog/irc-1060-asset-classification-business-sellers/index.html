IRC 1060 Asset Classification: What Every Business Seller Needs to Know

Published on June 20, 2026 | Updated November 6, 2026 | Selling Your Business | AE Tax Advisors

IRC Section 1060 determines how a business asset purchase price is allocated. It does not, by itself, determine whether the seller has ordinary income, capital gain or Section 1231 gain. A useful exit plan pairs the seven asset classes with each asset's fair market value, adjusted tax basis, depreciation or amortization history, and legal owner. Review those numbers before signing the purchase agreement, when there is still time to resolve differences with the buyer.

When Section 1060 and Form 8594 Apply

The rules generally apply when a group of assets constituting a trade or business is transferred and the buyer's basis is determined wholly by the amount paid. An ordinary stock or partnership-interest sale needs a different analysis; certain transactions treated as asset acquisitions can still fall within these rules. The IRS instructions for Form 8594 explain the filing scope and exceptions.

Under the residual method, consideration is first reduced by Class I assets, then allocated through Classes II to VI in order, generally up to each class's fair market value. Within a class, allocation follows relative fair market values. Any residual goes to Class VII. Liabilities and transaction costs can affect the buyer's cost and the seller's amount realized, so the allocation workpaper must reconcile to the deal documents rather than just the headline cash price.

A written allocation agreement generally binds both parties unless the IRS determines it is inappropriate. Agreement alone does not make an unsupported value defensible. Keep the appraisals, receivable analysis, inventory records and intangible valuation support behind the signed schedule.

Classes I and II: Cash, Deposits and Traded Property

Class I covers cash and general deposit accounts, including checking and savings accounts, but not certificates of deposit. Class II includes actively traded personal property, certificates of deposit and foreign currency, subject to the IRS definitions and exclusions. Identify which accounts and investments actually transfer. Retaining cash may change the negotiated purchase price; it does not automatically create additional goodwill or a tax saving.

Class III: Receivables and Debt Instruments

Class III generally includes accounts receivable, debt instruments and assets marked to market annually for federal tax purposes, with specified exceptions. The seller's accounting method and basis matter. A cash-method business commonly has zero tax basis in service receivables, while an accrual-method seller may already have recognized that income.

For example, if $300,000 is allocated to receivables with zero adjusted tax basis, the simplified ordinary-income amount is $300,000 before applicable selling costs or other adjustments. A $300,000 face amount is not automatically a $300,000 fair market value. Collectibility, aging and the agreed assets transferred must support the allocation. Compare retaining and collecting receivables with selling them, including the cash-flow and tax timing effects.

Class IV: Inventory at Supportable Fair Market Value

Inventory and property held primarily for sale to customers fall in Class IV. Inventory is not a capital asset; gain on its sale generally produces ordinary income. The seller's tax basis is used to calculate gain, but it does not dictate the acquisition-date value used in the allocation.

Do not assign inventory its cost basis just to remove taxable gain. Document quantities, condition, obsolescence and valuation assumptions, then apply the residual allocation rules. Cost can be relevant evidence, but an allocation equal to cost needs support that it reflects the appropriate fair market value.

Class V: Equipment, Vehicles, Land and Buildings

Class V includes assets not in Classes I through IV, VI or VII. The IRS specifically lists furniture, fixtures, buildings, land, vehicles and equipment as generally belonging here. Separate land from buildings and distinguish building components where relevant. If the seller keeps the real estate and leases it to the buyer, that retained property is not part of the acquired asset allocation.

For depreciable personal property, Section 1245 generally treats gain as ordinary income up to depreciation allowed or allowable, including bonus depreciation and Section 179 deductions. Remaining gain on qualifying business property held more than one year may enter Section 1231. Netting and the five-year lookback for prior Section 1231 losses can still turn some of that gain into ordinary income.

Buildings and land require a separate calculation. Buildings may raise Section 1250, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain and corporate recapture issues; land is not depreciable. Do not apply an equipment-recapture formula or a single capital-gain rate to every Class V asset. See IRS Publication 544 for the gain-character and recapture rules.

Class VI: Identifiable Section 197 Intangibles

Class VI includes Section 197 intangibles other than goodwill and going-concern value, such as customer relationships, workforce in place, trade names and qualifying noncompete agreements. Whether a particular patent, license or separately acquired right belongs here depends on the statutory definitions and exceptions.

A payment for a seller's covenant not to compete generally produces ordinary income for that seller. Acquired amortizable intangibles may generate Section 1245 recapture and, for qualifying property held more than one year, Section 1231 treatment on remaining gain. Self-created intangibles need their own capital-asset analysis; being self-created does not automatically make them Section 1231 property.

A buyer's ability to amortize an intangible over 15 years does not settle the seller's treatment. As a simplified illustration only, shifting $100,000 from gain taxed at an assumed 25% combined rate to income taxed at an assumed 45% combined rate increases tax by $20,000. Those are hypothetical rates, not a forecast or permission to move value without valuation support.

Class VII: Goodwill Is the Residual, Not a Guaranteed Tax Rate

Class VII captures goodwill and going-concern value after the earlier classes have been allocated under the residual method. Self-created goodwill may be a capital asset, with long-term treatment depending on the facts and holding period. Acquired amortizable Section 197 goodwill can instead produce ordinary-income recapture under Section 1245. Remaining gain may enter Section 1231 when its requirements are met.

There is no universal 20% tax rate for goodwill. Entity-level tax, owner-level tax, holding periods, Section 1231 netting, the net investment income tax and state treatment can change the result. A C corporation's asset sale and a later shareholder distribution, for example, require separate modeling.

If a supported equipment valuation is $300,000 instead of $500,000, the $200,000 difference may increase the Class VII residual if the other values and total consideration stay unchanged. The tax effect still depends on the seller's equipment recapture and goodwill history. Start with defensible values, then calculate tax, rather than choosing values from a desired tax result.

Pre-Signing Allocation Workpaper

Build one row per asset or supportable asset group, and resolve these items with the transaction's tax and legal advisers before committing to the allocation:

  1. Asset description and owner: identify the asset, legal owner, applicable class, acquisition date and whether it transfers. Separate operating-company assets, owner-held assets and excluded property.
  2. Fair market value and evidence: record the valuation date, appraisal or other support, inventory condition, receivable collectibility and the allocation within each class.
  3. Adjusted tax basis: reconcile original cost, improvements, depreciation and amortization allowed or allowable, and other basis adjustments to the tax records.
  4. Recapture and gain character: model Sections 1245, 1250 and 1231 as applicable, including holding periods and prior Section 1231 losses. Show ordinary income separately from potential capital gain.
  5. Entity and owner taxes: model the actual seller, distributions to owners, federal and state treatment, and relevant limitations. Distinguish tax on proceeds from the debt payoff and cash available after closing.
  6. Retained real estate: identify land or buildings kept outside the sale, any separate property sale, and any lease to the buyer. Do not allocate the operating-business price to real estate that does not transfer.
  7. Buyer/seller Form 8594 handoff: assign responsibility for the signed allocation schedule, closing-statement reconciliation, each return attachment and later earnout or price adjustments. Confirm both preparers receive the final version.

Form 8594 After Closing

Generally, buyer and seller each attach Form 8594 to their income tax return for the year of sale. If an allocation changes in a later year, the affected party may need Parts I and III for the year the increase or decrease is taken into account. Track earnouts, escrow releases and purchase-price changes with the preparers rather than assuming the closing allocation never changes.

The allocation schedule and tax-character calculation serve different purposes. Keep both, together with the agreement and valuation evidence, so the reported gain can be reconciled asset by asset.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Class V include land and buildings?

Yes. Class V generally includes land, buildings, equipment, vehicles, furniture and fixtures transferred with the business. Land and buildings need separate values and basis schedules because their depreciation and gain rules differ. Real estate the seller retains is not an acquired asset in the business sale.

Is every dollar allocated to goodwill taxed as capital gain?

No. Class VII identifies goodwill and going-concern value for allocation, not the seller's tax rate. Self-created goodwill may qualify for capital gain, while acquired amortizable Section 197 goodwill can generate ordinary-income recapture under Section 1245. Remaining gain may enter Section 1231 if the holding-period and business-use rules are met. Entity type and state taxes also matter.

Can the buyer and seller allocate inventory at its tax basis?

Tax basis alone does not set the allocation. Apply the residual method using supportable acquisition-date fair market values. An inventory allocation equal to cost needs valuation support; it cannot be selected simply to eliminate the seller's ordinary gain.

When should we agree on the allocation and prepare Form 8594?

Model the allocation before signing a binding purchase agreement, then reconcile it to the final closing statement. Generally, buyer and seller each attach Form 8594 to the income tax return for the year of sale. A later price adjustment may require a supplemental Form 8594 for the year the change is taken into account.

What should I bring to an asset-sale allocation review?

Bring the draft agreement and asset list, proposed values and appraisal support, depreciation and amortization schedules, tax basis records, debt and transaction-cost details, entity and ownership records, and a list of retained assets. Identify who will reconcile the closing allocation and prepare each party's Form 8594.
