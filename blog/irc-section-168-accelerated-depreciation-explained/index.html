Section 168 Depreciation: MACRS and Bonus Rules

IRC Section 168 establishes MACRS depreciation rules; Section 168(k) adds a separate first-year allowance for qualifying property. A short recovery period is not proof of bonus eligibility, and a depreciation deduction is not automatically a currently usable loss or an equal amount of tax savings. This guide separates classification, method, convention and the bonus election decision.

The MACRS framework: identify the asset first

Start with the asset ledger, purchase allocation, business use and date the asset was ready and available for its assigned function. Land is not depreciable. Under usual GDS treatment, residential rental buildings use 27.5 years and nonresidential real property uses 39 years. Separately supported personal property and land improvements can have shorter lives; industry-specific classes, statutory exceptions and required ADS can change the answer.

  • Five-year property: computers and certain appliances or other personal property, depending on the asset and use.
  • Seven-year property: office furniture and other qualifying assets not assigned to a different class.
  • 15-year property: qualifying land improvements and separately qualifying improvement property; the same life does not mean the same depreciation method.

Use IRS Publication 946 and its asset-class guidance rather than assigning a recovery period from a vendor invoice label alone.

Method and convention are separate decisions

Many three-, five-, seven- and ten-year GDS assets use 200% declining balance. Many 15- and 20-year assets use 150% declining balance. Declining-balance methods switch to straight line when that provides an equal or larger deduction. Exceptions and elections matter: qualified improvement property placed in service after 2017 generally uses 15-year straight line under GDS, not the 15-year 150% declining-balance column.

Residential rental and nonresidential real property generally use straight line and mid-month. Other covered MACRS additions use half-year unless the mid-quarter rule applies. The mid-quarter test excludes specified building categories and other property; it reduces tested basis for Section 179 and personal use, but not bonus depreciation. Required ADS uses its applicable methods and recovery periods and can prevent bonus eligibility. A real-property trade or business interest-limitation election puts specified real-property categories into ADS, not every asset.

For the actual percentage rows and a worked convention test, use the MACRS table guide and selected IRS rate download. Do not use one table interchangeably for land improvements, QIP, ADS and a building.

Bonus depreciation under Section 168(k): eligibility before percentage

The restored 100% allowance generally applies to qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. Earlier acquisitions can follow transitional rates, including 20% for certain property placed in service in 2026. Binding contracts, self-construction, long-production-period property and special elections need their own timing review. Merely moving a service date from December to January does not establish a rate change.

A MACRS recovery period of 20 years or less is one common eligibility category, not a complete test. Check acquisition and service dates, use, prior use by the taxpayer, related-party and carryover-basis restrictions, required ADS and applicable elections. Certain used property can qualify; not every used purchase qualifies. A cost segregation study does not override these requirements. Ordinary 27.5- or 39-year building classification does not itself qualify the structure for Section 168(k); separate statutory provisions need separate analysis.

IRS Notice 2026-11 provides interim additional-first-year-depreciation guidance. The currently linked Publication 946 is the 2025 edition, with a section addressing 2026 changes; it is not a 2026 edition.

Bonus normally applies; the common election is to opt out

For qualifying property, bonus depreciation generally applies unless a valid election or exception changes the result. The election not to claim the allowance generally applies to all property in the relevant class placed in service during that tax year. It is not normally a free choice to skip bonus on one asset while taking it on another asset in the same elected class. The taxpayer owning the assets makes the election, so entity-level elections also matter.

Publication 946 describes the election statement, timely-return rules and limited relief. Do not treat a missing software checkbox or a late amended return as a valid election without checking the applicable procedure. Other special elections described in Notice 2026-11 are distinct from the general opt-out.

Fictional example: do not deduct the same basis twice

These are two alternative assumptions, not deductions to combine. Assume $50,000 of fully business-use depreciable basis in qualifying five-year equipment acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025, no Section 179, no other basis adjustments and a 12-month tax year. The opt-out alternative also assumes half-year treatment and a valid class-wide election.

The first row leaves no basis for an additional $10,000 regular deduction. The second row leaves basis for later recovery years; it is not a permanent $40,000 loss of deductions. Neither row establishes current tax savings. Entity basis, at-risk, passive-activity, excess-business-loss and state rules can limit or defer the use of a computed deduction. See IRS Publication 925 for at-risk and passive-activity rules.

How cost segregation fits

A cost segregation study supports allocation among building components with different classifications. Reconcile its asset list to the acquisition ledger and existing depreciation schedule so components are not deducted both separately and inside unreduced building basis. A report or a short recovery period is not IRS approval, proof of bonus eligibility, or proof that the owner can currently use the resulting loss.

Section 179 and Section 168(k) are different

Section 179 is generally elected for qualifying property, subject to dollar, purchase-phaseout and active-business-income limits. The federal maximum is $2,500,000 for tax years beginning in 2025 and $2,560,000 for tax years beginning in 2026, before applicable reductions and limits. It also covers certain specified nonresidential improvements under its own rules. Bonus has different eligibility and election rules and no comparable general dollar cap or Section 179 business-income limit; other loss limitations still apply.

Where elected and allowed, apply Section 179 first, then bonus to the appropriate remaining basis, then regular depreciation. There is no universal rule that one is better for most investors. Compare federal and state treatment, usable income, future deductions, disposition consequences and the entity making the election. Use the Section 179 versus bonus guide for that decision.

Prepare the review file before filing

  • Asset invoice, acquisition agreement and any binding-contract or construction dates.
  • Evidence of when the asset was ready and available, business use and supported land/component allocations.
  • Prior depreciation, Section 179, bonus and election statements, plus any ADS requirement.
  • A basis bridge showing each deduction once and separate federal/state schedules where needed.
  • The owner/entity’s loss-limit review and unresolved classification questions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Section 179 and bonus depreciation?

Section 179 generally is elected for qualifying property and has dollar, purchase-phaseout and active-business-income limits. Bonus generally applies to qualifying property unless a valid election or exception changes it; the common opt-out is by class for the year. Both require eligibility and basis review, and other loss limits can affect use of the deduction.

Do states follow federal bonus depreciation?

State treatment varies by jurisdiction and tax year. Check conformity, addbacks, state depreciation and election requirements separately rather than assuming that the federal deduction appears unchanged on the state return.

Is bonus depreciation still 100% in 2026?

Generally for qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. Earlier acquisitions can remain subject to transitional percentages. Acquisition, binding-contract, use, service-date and election requirements must be checked; not every asset placed in service in 2026 qualifies for 100%.

What property qualifies for bonus depreciation?

A MACRS recovery period of 20 years or less is one common eligibility category, not the full test. Acquisition and service dates, use, prior taxpayer use, related-party and carryover-basis restrictions, required ADS and elections can change eligibility. Certain new and used property qualify; a cost segregation classification alone does not settle the result.
