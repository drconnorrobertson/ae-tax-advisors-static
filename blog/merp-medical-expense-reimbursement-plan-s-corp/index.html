S-Corp Medical Reimbursements: Premiums vs Out-of-Pocket Costs

By AE Tax Advisors · Published 2026-07-01

Updated November 8, 2026.

A more-than-2% S corporation shareholder cannot assume that a medical reimbursement plan turns every personal medical bill into a tax-free business benefit. Health-insurance premiums and out-of-pocket treatment costs follow different rules. Classify the payment before choosing payroll or return treatment.

Start with ownership and family attribution

More-than-2% shareholders are treated differently from ordinary employees for fringe-benefit purposes. Family ownership attribution can matter, so holding a small direct percentage or employing a spouse does not automatically avoid the owner rules.

The IRS states that a more-than-2% shareholder is not eligible for tax-free HRA treatment under Section 105(b) or QSEHRA participation. A plan name, written document or payroll entry does not override those restrictions.

Health-insurance premiums have a specific pathway

When the corporation pays or reimburses eligible premiums and reports them correctly as shareholder wages, the shareholder may qualify for the self-employed health-insurance deduction if its conditions are met. Corporate establishment of the coverage, wage reporting, earned-income limits and eligibility for subsidized employer coverage must be reviewed.

The additional premium wages generally go in Box 1 of Form W-2. Exclusion from Social Security and Medicare wages depends on the applicable plan-or-system requirements. Do not apply that premium rule indiscriminately to every medical reimbursement.

Copays and dental bills are not insurance premiums

Paying a doctor's invoice, deductible or dental treatment does not make it a premium deductible under Section 162(l). Personal unreimbursed medical expenses may instead enter the itemized-medical-expense rules, subject to eligibility, itemizing and the applicable AGI floor. An HSA may offer another route when all HSA conditions are met.

For illustration, $10,000 of premiums and $5,000 of copays must not be combined into a $15,000 self-employed health-insurance deduction solely because the corporation reimbursed them. Determine the treatment of each category separately.

Employee plans need their own review

Benefits for eligible nonowner employees can involve different exclusions, nondiscrimination rules, coverage requirements and reporting. Review the entire employee census and family relationships before adopting a plan. An arrangement suitable for one employee group may not give the owner the same tax result.

Prepare a payment-by-payment reconciliation

  • Ownership and family-attribution schedule.
  • Insurance policies, premium receipts and corporate payments.
  • Separate out-of-pocket expenses and reimbursements.
  • Payroll reports and Forms W-2.
  • Eligibility for other employer coverage or HSA treatment.
  • Plan documents and employee eligibility records.

Use the setup review guide for next steps, and ask for a benefits and payroll review before treating reimbursements as tax-free.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a more-than-2% S shareholder use a tax-free HRA?

The IRS excludes these shareholders from the employee exclusion under Section 105(b); do not assume ordinary HRA benefits apply.

Are copays deductible as self-employed health insurance?

They are not insurance premiums merely because the company reimburses them. Review personal medical or HSA rules separately.

Can premiums still be deductible?

Potentially, through the specific S corporation and shareholder reporting pathway, if all self-employed health-insurance conditions are met.
