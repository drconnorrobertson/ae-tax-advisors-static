We're buying a business and the seller owes Form 941 payroll taxes. Will an asset deal protect us?

Published September 25, 2026 | Business Acquisition Tax | AE Tax Advisors

An asset purchase does not automatically make the buyer liable for every Form 941 balance owed by the seller, but it is not a universal shield. The buyer needs to separate four questions before closing: which legal entity owes the tax; whether an IRS tax lien already reaches assets being transferred; whether applicable state law, a merger-like transaction, or the contract creates successor or transferee liability; and whether any person who controls payroll-tax payments faces a separate trust fund recovery penalty. The IRS's third-party-liability manual expressly describes state-law-dependent successor theories. Transaction counsel must determine how those rules apply to the actual deal and states.

This is a buyer-side diligence decision, not simply a question of whether the seller promises to pay. A profitable operating business may have staff, accounts, licenses, equipment, and customer contracts the purchaser intends to keep; a payroll-tax surprise can affect the price, financing, liens, escrow, and closing date. The form of the purchase agreement is only one part of the risk analysis.

Separate the company debt, the assets, and individual exposure

Entity debt: If the buyer acquires the stock or membership interests of the same employer entity, that entity generally remains the Form 941 taxpayer; a change in owners is not payment of its old payroll-tax balance. In an asset purchase by a separate entity, the seller may remain the original taxpayer, but counsel must review assumed liabilities, transaction substance, and state successor rules. The IRS manual identifies express or implied assumption, merger, de facto merger or mere continuation, and fraudulent transfer as possible state-law paths. It does not say every asset buyer is liable.

Property liens: An assessed, unpaid federal tax can create a lien on the seller's property. A buyer must check whether assets being acquired are encumbered and whether a release or property-specific certificate of discharge is needed. An ordinary contractual indemnity against the seller does not itself discharge a lien. The IRS's federal tax lien guidance distinguishes paying the debt, releasing a lien, and discharging specific property.

People: The trust fund recovery penalty (TFRP) is a separate federal issue for a person who was responsible for collecting, accounting for, or paying over withheld taxes and willfully failed to do so. Title alone is not the test; authority and payment decisions matter. Buying assets does not by itself establish that a new owner caused the seller's earlier failure, but taking control of an employer with known unpaid withholding calls for immediate advice on current deposits and any proposed payments to other creditors. See the IRS TFRP explanation. Do not conflate TFRP with successor liability of the purchasing entity.

Decision tree before signing the purchase agreement

  1. Identify the transaction taxpayer. Is the buyer acquiring equity in the existing employer, merging entities, or buying specified assets through a distinct entity? Trace the EIN, payroll accounts, assumed contracts, and liabilities. A term sheet that says “asset deal” is not enough if the final structure changes.
  2. Verify the Form 941 balance and years. With seller authorization, reconcile each quarter's Form 941, payroll register, Forms W-2, deposit history, IRS notices and business tax transcripts. Distinguish unfiled returns, assessed liabilities, deposits in transit, and disputes. A seller-prepared spreadsheet alone cannot prove payment.
  3. Examine assets and state-law exposure. Search federal lien notices and obtain lien/discharge advice for each material asset. Have counsel test the states where assets, employees, and operations sit for successor liability, bulk-sale or tax-clearance procedures, and whether the buyer is assuming debt by contract or conduct. The IRS manual says successor liability depends on state law; a certificate available in one state is not a nationwide clearance.
  4. Set a closing mechanism. Discuss direct payoff, verified release or discharge, purchase-price escrow or holdback, seller representations, tax indemnities, and post-closing payroll controls with deal counsel and the lender. Determine which protections actually bind the IRS or state taxing agency and which merely create a claim against the seller if it later fails to pay.

Worked comparison: the same arrears, two proposed deals

Suppose a target operating company has two unpaid Form 941 quarters, and the buyer is considering either acquiring its shares or buying selected assets into a new entity. In the share deal, the target employer remains the taxpayer with its liabilities; changing shareholders does not erase them. The buyer must price and fund that entity exposure, then separately assess the powers its new managers will exercise over current deposits. In the asset deal, the seller may remain the original Form 941 taxpayer, but the buyer must determine whether transferred equipment is subject to a lien and whether state successor, assumed-liability, or de facto-merger rules could reach the new entity. No dollar figure or tax clearance can be inferred from the deal label alone.

If counsel instead negotiates a documented payoff at closing and a discharge for specified liened property, the asset risk may differ from an unprotected transfer. That does not automatically resolve unfiled returns, other assets, state payroll accounts, or individual TFRP issues. Records to gather and mistakes to avoid

  • Entity chart, proposed asset or equity purchase agreement, schedules of assumed liabilities, merger documents if any, and any lender conditions.
  • All relevant Forms 941, IRS account transcripts and balance notices, deposit confirmations, payroll registers, W-2 reconciliations, and records of any payment plan or audit.
  • Federal lien search results, asset-title and UCC records, state tax-account and clearance documents, escrow/payoff instructions, and written proof of any agency release or discharge.

Common failure points are assuming the words “asset purchase” eliminate every tax risk; assuming a stock purchase personally makes every new shareholder a TFRP responsible person; treating a seller indemnity as a tax-lien release; ignoring state-law successor rules; or letting current payroll deposits lapse while negotiating old balances. State law and deal facts control successor analysis, and TFRP requires a separate responsibility-and-willfulness test. This article is a federal tax diligence framework, not a legal opinion or state-specific clearance determination.
