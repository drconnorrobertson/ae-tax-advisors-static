Do I Need an Engineering-Based Cost Segregation Study?

Published on August 11, 2026 | Updated November 6, 2026 | Cost Segregation | AE Tax Advisors

Choose a study by the property facts and the evidence it produces. A higher fee, a longer report or an engineering label does not establish that an allocation is correct. A lower price or an online questionnaire does not establish that it is wrong. Before commissioning a study, identify the work needed to support the components, reconcile basis and implement the result on the return.

For a business owner with rental property, there are two decisions: whether the property analysis is adequately supported, and whether accelerating depreciation is useful on the owner's returns. An engineering report does not by itself resolve passive-loss limitations, entity basis, state differences or the economics of an expected sale.

Discuss Your Property and Study Scope

What the IRS audit guide actually addresses

The IRS Cost Segregation Audit Technique Guide, chapters 3 through 5, discusses several approaches and the documentation examiners review. Actual-cost records can provide stronger support than estimates. When estimates are necessary, their method and source matter. The guide describes a site visit as an element of a quality study and discusses preparer experience, asset classification and reconciliation.

The guide is examination guidance, not binding law or an IRS approval of any provider. It cautions against unsupported percentage allocations but directs examiners to assess the evidence on its merits. Do not turn that into a promise of acceptance or a prediction that a particular price tier will fail an audit.

Match the method to the records and the property

Choose the work from the property facts
Property factsWork to ask the provider to explain
New construction with invoices and drawingsHow actual costs are traced to components, indirect costs are allocated and the study reconciles to the project ledger.
Acquired rental without original construction invoicesHow existing components, quantities, condition and cost estimates are supported, and how the acquisition allocation is reconciled.
Renovated or mixed-use buildingHow separate service dates, removed assets, specialized systems and prior schedules are investigated without duplicating basis.
Portfolio of similar buildingsWhy the proposed sample represents the portfolio, how exceptions are identified and which properties need individual analysis.

Ask how the proposed method addresses uncertainty. An acquired building needs support for its acquisition-date allocation, not merely a list of replacement costs scaled to force a desired result. A portfolio model needs more than the statement that all properties have the same number of units.

Five checks before accepting the report

  1. Identify the assets. Request descriptions that connect the report to the actual property and explain material classification judgments.
  2. Trace quantities and costs. Ask which inputs come from records, inspection, measurement or estimates and who checks them.
  3. Explain the tax treatment. The report should show recovery periods and the support for classifications, rather than only a total deduction.
  4. Reconcile basis. Land, existing assets, improvements and indirect costs should reconcile without duplication. Compare the report with the fixed-asset ledger.
  5. Name the responsible parties. Identify who reviews the report, answers preparer questions, corrects errors and handles any separately scoped filing or representation work.

Ask whether audit support means supplying study records, answering technical questions or representing the taxpayer. Confirm exclusions, duration and additional charges in the engagement. Neither free support nor a site visit guarantees the tax result.

Software and professional studies can overlap

Product labels do not describe the entire engagement. For example, KBKG's CostSegregation.com FAQ describes component-level cost estimates, report schedules and included study audit support, while also explaining limits of self-service inputs and situations needing more detailed work. Those are the provider's published descriptions, not an independent assessment of a particular report.

Use the software-versus-professional decision guide to compare who verifies the facts, resolves exceptions and implements the result. Ask for a sample deliverable for a property like yours.

Illustrative comparison: two proposals for one medical office

This hypothetical example is not an AE client result or a market price survey. An owner acquires a $2,600,000 medical office building with $2,150,000 of depreciable basis. Proposal A costs $1,200 and supplies a two-page allocation summary showing 24% reclassification, without an asset listing. Proposal B costs $11,500 and includes site work, quantity and cost support, classification analysis, photographs and a basis reconciliation, showing 27% reclassification.

The stated percentages correspond to $516,000 and $580,500 of reclassified basis, a $64,500 difference. That difference is not automatically a current deduction. At a purely illustrative 37% rate, $64,500 multiplied by 37% is $23,865, but that multiplication is not a tax-savings estimate. Depreciation timing, incremental deductions compared with the existing schedule and loss limits still need to be calculated under Publication 946 and Publication 925.

Before selecting either proposal, ask A for the missing workpapers and ask B how its allocations were supported. Confirm filing and support scope for both. The $10,300 fee difference buys different stated work; it does not prove that B's percentage is correct or that A cannot supply adequate support. A report's page count is not the decision.

What to bring to an AE scope discussion

  • Property type, square footage, ownership entity and purchase and service dates.
  • Closing records, land allocation, construction or improvement records and the current depreciation schedule.
  • The proposed study, sample report, written fee and any excluded work.
  • Your expected sale timing and the return preparer's questions about deduction usability.

Review AE's published study pricing and proposal checklist. Ask the team to identify study work, tax-return implementation and any additional advisory work in the written scope before committing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the IRS require an engineering-based cost segregation study?

The IRS audit guide does not prescribe one preparation method or credential. It describes the evidence examiners assess, including preparer experience, methodology, classifications and cost support. The guide describes a site visit as an element of a quality study, but a visit or an engineering label alone does not guarantee that a report supports the return.

Is a low-cost or questionnaire-based study automatically inadequate?

No. Review the work behind the report rather than its price or intake format. Ask how components and costs were identified, who checked the inputs, how allocations reconcile to basis, and what support is included. Missing support is a reason to resolve the scope before filing, not evidence of a predetermined audit outcome.

When does a property need more detailed investigation?

Separate renovation phases, unusual building systems, mixed uses, uncertain land values and incomplete asset records can require more investigation. Ask the provider to identify which facts its standard method cannot verify and what additional work is proposed.

Can one study be applied to several properties?

A sampling or modeling approach may fit sufficiently similar properties, but similarity must be demonstrated. Building use, construction, location, costs and renovations can differ. The provider should explain the population, sampling method, error considerations and how exceptions are handled.

Does the largest reclassification produce the best return?

Not necessarily. Reclassified basis must be supported, and its depreciation depends on the applicable dates, methods, elections and conventions. The owner must also be able to use the deductions. Compare incremental after-tax cash flow with the full fee and expected sale consequences.
