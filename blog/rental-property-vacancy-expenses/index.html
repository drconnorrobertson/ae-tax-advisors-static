Vacant Rental During Renovation: What Can You Deduct?

Published September 22, 2026 | Real Estate Tax | AE Tax Advisors

If a previously rented property sits empty for six months while you renovate it for the next tenant, ordinary costs of holding it for rental use may still be rental expenses, and depreciation on the existing property may continue. The renovation itself is not automatically a current deduction. Classify each invoice, establish whether the property stayed in the rental activity, and test whether the resulting loss is currently usable before filing.

First, identify which vacancy you actually have

A tenant turnover, a six-month renovation of a formerly rented home, a newly purchased property that was never ready for tenants, and a property withdrawn from the rental market are not the same tax fact pattern. The prior lease and depreciation schedule show whether the building was already placed in service. Listings, a property-manager agreement, permits, insurance coverage, and the owner's plans help establish whether it continued to be held for rental use. A gap in rent alone does not make a former rental personal property; an empty house alone does not make a future rental placed in service.

IRS Publication 527 permits ordinary and necessary expenses, including depreciation, for managing, conserving, or maintaining property held for rental purposes while vacant. It does not permit a deduction for rent that could have been earned but was not. IRS Publication 946 says depreciation can continue while income-producing property is temporarily idle, but a newly acquired property does not begin depreciation until it is ready and available for its intended use. These are federal starting points, not a blanket authorization to expense every payment. Publication 527 distinguishes repairs from betterments, restorations, and adaptations. Even work performed during a vacancy can be capital. Conversely, an ordinary holding cost does not automatically become a building improvement just because contractors were on site. A large rehabilitation may also raise additional capitalization questions that require a project-level review.

How to show the rental activity continued

Write down why the unit was empty and what had to happen before the next tenant could move in. A signed property-manager agreement, contractor schedule, renewed landlord insurance, and a new listing after work ends can support continued rental intent. A later personal-use plan, a long-term withdrawal from the market, or a decision to sell without offering it for rent points in a different direction. The federal treatment still depends on actual use, ownership, accounting method, and the return year; state and local treatment should be checked separately.

The decision to make before filing

Build a month-by-month timeline: last tenant move-out, demolition and completion dates, when the home was ready and available again, listing, and next occupancy. Then reconcile each payment to the books and prior-year depreciation schedule. Group contractor invoices by work performed, not just by vendor, so a current repair does not disappear into a capital project or a new roof become an ordinary repair. If work spans tax years, track construction-in-progress, payments, and the later placed-in-service date rather than treating the opening balance as a fresh deduction.

Illustrative example: a six-month vacancy

A rental home had a tenant through March. The owner then spent April through September replacing the kitchen and roof and marketed the finished property for a new lease beginning in October. Assume the owner never used it personally and kept it in the rental activity. The original building's depreciation schedule generally continues through the vacancy; ordinary mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, and necessary utilities are separately tested as rental holding costs. The new kitchen and roof are reviewed as capital improvements, not simply netted against six months of lost rent. Each qualifying improvement gets its own basis and placed-in-service date when ready for its rental use. If instead the owner bought an unready house in April and never previously offered it for rent, the original building's first depreciation date needs a different analysis.

Suppose the vacancy creates a $24,000 Schedule E loss after correct classifications. That number is not necessarily a $24,000 current reduction of wages or other nonpassive income. Apply basis and at-risk limits where relevant, then the passive-activity rules, including any available special allowance or real-estate-professional treatment. Suspended losses need a carryforward schedule. See the rental-sale suspended-loss guide and IRS Publication 925.

Records that support the position

Keep the prior lease, dated vacancy calendar, listing screenshots, inquiries, agent agreement, permits, contractor proposals and itemized invoices, payment evidence, before-and-after photos, insurance declarations, and the new lease or marketing plan. Tie amounts on the return to bank activity and the agreement. Keep the prior depreciation schedule and a separate fixed-asset record for each capital improvement. When several assets, people, or uses are involved, write down the allocation method and apply it consistently. A short dated workpaper is easier to defend than a reconstructed explanation years later.

A common reporting error

A property held for future personal use is not automatically a rental simply because it is empty. The reverse error is stopping all depreciation the moment a tenant leaves, even when the previously rented property remains temporarily idle in a continuing rental activity. Another error is putting a complete roof or kitchen project into the repairs line because the work was done between tenants. Review both sides of the entry: a payment can affect basis, a liability, or an expense at different times. A correct cash total alone does not establish the correct tax character.

Before you file, decide what happens next

If the renovation is still open at year-end, do not assume every invoice is placed in service merely because it was paid. Separate completed and available improvements from unfinished work. If you decide to sell rather than re-rent, document when the rental plan changed: Publication 527 distinguishes a property still available for rent while listed for sale from one no longer held out for rent. A sale plan can also change how future holding costs are reported. If an earlier return started depreciation too late or deducted a capital project as repairs, compare an amended return with a potential accounting-method correction rather than silently changing this year's Schedule E. AE's amended-return versus Form 3115 guide explains that fork.

Where to verify the rule

Start with IRS Publication 527: Residential Rental Property for vacant rentals, repairs and improvements, and first rental use; Publication 946 for ready-and-available and temporarily idle depreciation; and Publication 925 for the loss limits. These publications are federal guidance; check the return year, later developments, ownership structure, and state rules.
