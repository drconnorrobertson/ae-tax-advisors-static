IRC 1041: Spousal Transfers, Carryover Basis and Exceptions

Published on July 29, 2026 | Divorce and Business | AE Tax Advisors

Section 1041 generally provides federal income-tax nonrecognition for property transferred between spouses, or between former spouses when the transfer is incident to divorce. That does not establish that every related transaction is tax-free, that the recipient receives a fair-market-value basis, or that the proposed settlement is legally equitable.

Reviewed November 22, 2026. Begin with the actual transferor, recipient, asset, dates, documents, basis and liabilities. A transfer of an owner's shares is different from a corporation transferring its own assets. Retirement arrangements and other special assets also require their applicable rules.

  • Identify the transfer
  • Check former-spouse timing
  • Separate value and basis
  • Compare direct and trust transfers
  • Review other exceptions
  • Prepare the records

Identify the transfer before applying nonrecognition

The general rule concerns property transfers between individuals in the specified spousal relationship. It does not require a contemplated divorce for a transfer between current spouses. Services are not property for this rule. Do not assume a corporation's transaction qualifies merely because spouses own the corporation.

The Treasury regulation, questions 2–5, distinguishes these situations. Establish the actual parties and transaction rather than treating every business or property movement connected with a divorce as the same transfer.

Former-spouse timing has two routes

A transfer within one year after the marriage ends meets the statutory incident-to-divorce timing route. For the related-to-divorce route, a transfer under the original or modified divorce or separation instrument within six years is treated as related to the marriage's end. These timing rules do not remove other exceptions.

Outside the instrument/six-year conditions, a presumption of unrelatedness can require a specific factual showing. It is not an automatic permanent bar, and a decree's wording alone does not guarantee the result. The regulation's question 7 discusses property division, impediments to an earlier transfer and prompt completion after an impediment is removed. Review the dates and evidence with the advisers.

A qualifying transfer generally carries basis, not market value

The recipient generally takes the transferor's adjusted basis immediately before a qualifying transfer. The rule applies to later gain and loss calculations; do not substitute the ordinary gift dual-basis shortcut or assume a buyout payment automatically creates a new fair-market-value basis. Questions 10–12 of the regulation explain the income-tax consequences, including qualifying direct transfers involving liabilities above basis.

Consider two properties, each worth $500,000. One has $400,000 of adjusted basis; the other has $100,000. Their built-in gains differ by $300,000 before other adjustments. At a hypothetical uniform 20% rate, that difference would represent $60,000 of future tax, but actual rates, depreciation-related gain, NIIT, state tax and the timing of sale can change the outcome. Equal appraised values need not mean equal after-tax values.

That comparison is a hypothetical sensitivity, not an agreed settlement value. A basis schedule, depreciation history, gain character, possible exclusions, tax rates and sale assumptions must be reviewed separately. Tax-adjusted figures can inform a discussion; they do not determine a legally equitable division.

A trust transfer can produce a different gain result

IRS Publication 504 describes an exception for property transferred in trust when the relevant liabilities exceed the transferor's adjusted basis. Recognized gain under that exception increases the trust's basis. A direct qualifying spousal transfer and a transfer in trust should therefore not be treated as interchangeable.

Original fictional comparison. Assume ordinary investment property with $400,000 fair market value and $150,000 adjusted basis immediately before each alternative transfer. Assume an otherwise qualifying transfer to a U.S.-citizen spouse, or to a trust for that spouse's benefit, with no installment obligation, separate debt cancellation, stock redemption or other special event. The liability column is one assumed aggregate input for the trust calculation, not a statement about actual loan documents.

Check exceptions and separate tax questions

  • Nonresident-alien recipient: the Section 1041 nonrecognition rule does not apply to a transfer to a nonresident-alien spouse or former spouse. The recipient's status matters.
  • Installment obligations in trust: Publication 504 describes separate gain-or-loss recognition for an installment obligation transferred in trust for a former spouse incident to divorce. The ordinary-property table above does not calculate that result.
  • Third-party transfers and stock redemptions: qualifying arrangements can involve a deemed first transfer to the spouse and a second transfer to the third party, with a separate taxable result. Certain stock redemptions have special rules. Do not extend the first transfer's nonrecognition to the entire transaction.
  • Retirement and other special assets: qualified-plan orders, IRA divorce transfers, account procedures and other asset-specific provisions need their own review.
  • Other taxes and obligations: federal income-tax nonrecognition does not itself decide gift-tax reporting, state taxes, transfer charges, lender consent, lien releases or settlement rights.

Prepare a transfer-by-transfer record

  • Identify the legal transferor, recipient, ownership interest and asset actually moving.
  • Record the relationship and relevant residency facts, marriage-ending date, transfer date and controlling instrument.
  • Reconcile adjusted basis, holding period, depreciation and recapture records; keep value estimates separate.
  • List assumed obligations, secured debts, trust terms and third-party steps rather than relying on the settlement's headline value.
  • Ask the responsible tax advisers and counsel to explain the applicable rule, any recognized amount, recipient basis, reporting and outstanding consents.

A property settlement, alimony, child support and interest can have different treatment. Labels alone do not resolve it. Review the agreement's execution date, modifications, payment terms and applicable rules; do not assume every post-2018 payment is simply the same kind of transfer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Section 1041 erase built-in gain?

No. A qualifying transfer generally uses carryover basis. The recipient may recognize the built-in gain on a later taxable sale.

When is a transfer incident to divorce?

A transfer within one year of the marriage’s end qualifies under the statutory timing rule; other transfers require the related-to-divorce analysis, including the applicable six-year presumption.

What if the recipient is a nonresident alien?

The Section 1041 nonrecognition rule does not apply to a transfer to a nonresident-alien spouse or former spouse.

Is a transfer to a third party automatically tax-free?

No. Qualifying arrangements can be treated as a transfer to the spouse followed by a second transfer, which may itself be taxable.

Can a transfer in trust trigger gain under Section 1041?

Yes. Certain trust transfers can trigger gain when relevant liabilities exceed adjusted basis, and special rules apply to installment obligations. The direct-transfer nonrecognition rule cannot be applied indiscriminately to a trust transfer.
