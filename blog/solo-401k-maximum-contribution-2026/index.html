Solo 401(k) Maximum Contributions for 2026

Published on June 23, 2026 | Retirement and Exit Planning | AE Tax Advisors

A one-participant 401(k) can receive employee deferrals and employer contributions when the owner, business and plan satisfy the applicable rules. Review compensation, contributions to other plans, employee eligibility and plan terms before treating a published ceiling as an available contribution or deduction.

Updated November 22, 2026. Compare four 2026 S-corporation contribution cases.

The Two Contributions

The employee deferral. You contribute as an employee of your own business, limited by the elective deferral limit of IRC Sec. 402(g). For 2026 the limit is $24,500, with an additional catch-up contribution of $8,000 available at age 50 or older. Participants aged 60 through 63 are eligible for a higher catch-up amount of $11,250 under the SECURE 2.0 provisions.

The employer contribution. Your business contributes as the employer, up to 25% of compensation. What counts as compensation depends on your entity type, which is the part that trips people up.

Annual additions, including regular employee deferrals, employer contributions and any voluntary after-tax employee contributions, are generally limited to the lesser of $72,000 or 100% of applicable compensation for 2026. Eligible catch-up contributions are excluded from that annual-additions ceiling. The compensation that can be taken into account is capped at $360,000 for 2026; the plan and other applicable limits can produce a lower amount.

These figures are indexed annually and should be confirmed against the current-year IRS notice before you fund, since the inflation adjustments change each year.

S-Corp Calculation

For an S corporation, compensation means your W-2 wages from the business. Distributions do not count. Neither does K-1 income.

The employer contribution can be up to 25% of eligible W-2 compensation as defined by the plan, subject to remaining annual-additions room and other applicable limits. Salary decisions also require reasonable-compensation, payroll-tax and broader return analysis.

Example. S-Corp owner, age 52, $180,000 W-2 salary, $220,000 in distributions.

  • Employee deferral: $24,500
  • Age 50+ catch-up: $8,000
  • Employer contribution: 25% of $180,000 = $45,000
  • Total: $77,500

The $69,500 of non-catch-up contributions sits under the $72,000 annual additions limit, and the catch-up is additional.

Now the same owner with a $90,000 salary and $310,000 in distributions:

  • Employee deferral: $24,500
  • Catch-up: $8,000
  • Employer contribution: 25% of $90,000 = $22,500
  • Total: $55,000

The examples compare contribution capacity, not total tax savings. Both assume sufficient eligible compensation, an appropriate plan and no competing deferrals. Required Roth catch-up treatment can mean the catch-up is not pre-tax. Salary changes also affect payroll tax and QBI, so model the full result while meeting reasonable-compensation requirements.

Sole Proprietor and Single-Member LLC Calculation

Without an S election, compensation is net earnings from self-employment, and the arithmetic changes.

The employer contribution is 25% of net earnings after reducing them by the deductible portion of self-employment tax and by the contribution itself. That circularity produces an effective rate of 20% of net earnings after the self-employment tax adjustment.

For a sole proprietor, calculate adjusted net earnings using the actual deductible portion of self-employment tax, then apply the plan’s self-employed contribution computation. A plan with a 25% employer rate generally produces an effective 20% rate on adjusted net earnings. Use the Publication 560 worksheet rather than applying 25% directly to Schedule C profit. Account for elective deferrals made to other plans.

Roth and After-Tax Options

Employee deferrals can be designated Roth contributions if the plan permits. For 2026, affected participants whose 2025 FICA wages from the sponsoring employer exceeded $150,000 generally must make catch-up contributions as Roth contributions. Review applicability with the administrator; this is not a test of current-year household income or distributions.

A plan may permit designated Roth employer matching or nonelective contributions when the requirements are met. These amounts are included in the participant’s income and require the appropriate reporting. Do not assume the employer loses its otherwise available deduction merely because the participant elects Roth treatment.

Plans with the right features may also permit after-tax contributions beyond the deferral limit, up to the annual additions limit, followed by an in-plan Roth conversion. That is the mega backdoor Roth, and it requires a plan document that specifically supports both features. See the mega backdoor Roth for business owners.

Deadlines

A plan may be adopted after year-end under applicable rules, but adoption, deferral elections and funding have different deadlines. A special rule can permit an eligible first-year sole proprietor with no employees to elect and fund first-year deferrals by the individual return deadline without extensions. An S-corporation owner cannot simply backdate payroll deferrals. Confirm the actual entity, first-year status and plan requirements.

Employee deferrals. For an S-Corp owner, deferrals come out of payroll and must be elected and withheld during the year. Plan for this before your final payroll run.

Employer contributions. Due by the due date of the return, including extensions.

See setting up a plan after year-end.

The Employee Rule

An owner-only plan requires review of all potentially eligible employees, including workers in related businesses. Track age, service and hours under the plan and current rules, including long-term part-time eligibility. Working fewer than 1,000 hours does not by itself establish exclusion. A participating spouse needs actual eligible compensation.

A hire triggers employee-eligibility review; the job label alone does not establish the participation date. When a common-law employee becomes eligible, the plan cannot continue excluding that employee merely because it began as an owner-only plan. Have the administrator review coverage, testing, filings and any necessary changes.

Controlled group and affiliated service group rules under IRC Sec. 414(b), (c), and (m) aggregate related businesses for this purpose. An owner with a solo 401(k) in one entity and employees in another commonly owned entity may have a coverage problem, and this is one of the more frequent errors in owner-only plans.

Solo 401(k) vs. SEP IRA

Compare a solo 401(k) and SEP using the actual owner compensation, employee coverage, permitted features, costs and administration. Different contribution components can change capacity, but they do not establish one universal best plan.

A SEP has no employee elective-deferral or catch-up component, so at $120,000 of eligible compensation a 25% employer contribution is $30,000. A qualifying solo 401(k) can add employee deferrals, subject to the combined limits. Roth SEP arrangements are now permitted where offered; compare actual plan features, administration, employee coverage and investment choices instead of assuming every SEP lacks a Roth option.

A SEP and a solo 401(k) have different contribution and administrative features. Compare their actual documents and funding requirements before choosing. See using a SEP and solo 401(k) together and how much you can contribute to a solo 401(k).

When to Go Beyond a Solo 401(k)

A cash balance plan may add contribution capacity for some businesses, but the amount depends on age, actuarial assumptions, employee coverage, compensation and funding requirements. Obtain a design and cost projection before committing to an annual contribution.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the maximum solo 401(k) contribution for 2026?

For 2026, regular elective deferrals are limited to $24,500. The annual-additions ceiling is generally the lesser of $72,000 or 100% of applicable compensation, excluding eligible catch-up contributions. If permitted, catch-up can be $8,000 at age 50 or older, or $11,250 instead for ages 60 through 63. Plan terms, other contributions and compensation rules can reduce the amount available; $360,000 is the 2026 compensation cap.

How is the employer contribution calculated for an S-Corp owner?

It can be up to 25% of eligible W-2 compensation under the plan, subject to remaining annual-additions room and other limits. S-corp distributions and K-1 income do not replace eligible compensation. Review reasonable compensation, payroll taxes and the full return before changing salary.

How does a sole proprietor calculate the employer contribution?

Use adjusted net earnings and the Publication 560 self-employed worksheets. A plan using a 25% employer rate generally yields a 20% self-employed rate after the applicable self-employment-tax adjustment, with further limits. This is a different base from eligible S-corp W-2 compensation, not proof that changing entity type increases a contribution.

Can I still set up a solo 401(k) after the year ends?

Adoption and funding deadlines differ. A qualifying first-year sole proprietor with no employees may have a special first-year deferral deadline at the individual filing due date without extensions. Other late adopters may be limited to employer contributions; confirm the entity and plan facts.

Can I have a solo 401(k) if I have employees?

An owner-only plan must be reevaluated when employees may become eligible. Review age, service, hours, long-term part-time requirements, related employers and plan terms with the administrator. A part-time or full-time label alone does not establish eligibility or exclusion; a spouse also needs actual eligible compensation.

Make Sure You Are Funding the Right Plan

A solo 401(k) is the right plan for many owners and the wrong one for others. We model it against a SEP, a defined benefit design, and your salary and QBI position so the plan matches the business.
