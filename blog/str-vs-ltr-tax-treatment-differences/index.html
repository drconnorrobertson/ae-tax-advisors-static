STR vs LTR Tax Treatment: Why the IRS Treats Your Rentals Differently

Published on July 14, 2026 | Updated on November 6, 2026 | Real Estate Investor Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Apply the tax tests separately: customer-use length determines whether a rental exception applies under Section 469; participation determines passive versus nonpassive treatment; building use determines depreciation classification. None of these alone settles self-employment tax or the amount deductible.

1. Calculate Average Customer Use

For the seven-day exception, calculate average customer use using the applicable rental-period rules and complete booking records, not the platform's marketing label. If 200 customer-use days represent 40 separate rental periods, the simple average is five days. Repeated or continuous use by the same customer can require a closer look at the actual period.

A seven-day-or-less average is one exception in Treasury Regulation 1.469-1T(e)(3). The regulation also contains other exceptions, including a 30-day-or-less average with significant personal services. Do not classify an eight-day stay automatically as a passive long-term rental without reviewing the other facts.

2. Determine Whether Rental or Non-Rental Participation Rules Apply

Rental real estate is generally passive even when an owner works in it. A qualifying real estate professional must also materially participate in the relevant rental to obtain nonpassive treatment. The special active-participation allowance is different: it may permit a limited passive loss deduction, with modified-AGI, filing-status and other restrictions.

When a customer-use exception removes the activity from rental classification, evaluate material participation directly. The regulation's tests include more than 500 hours, substantially all participation, and more than 100 hours with participation no less than that of any other individual. The facts-and-circumstances test has additional restrictions; merely describing work as regular is not sufficient.

3. Check the Allowable Loss, Not Just Its Label

Nonpassive treatment removes one limitation. Basis, at-risk, personal-use and excess-business-loss rules can still prevent or defer a deduction. Prior-year passive carryforwards have separate rules when an activity becomes nonpassive. A cost-segregation study changes depreciation timing, not the owner's participation or automatic eligibility for a full wage offset.

For the investment decision, use the separate STR versus LTR planning comparison. For the limitation sequence, see STR excess-business-loss guidance.

4. Assign the Building Recovery Period Separately

Under Section 168, residential rental property generally uses a 27.5-year recovery period and nonresidential real property generally uses 39 years under GDS. Residential rental classification includes an 80%-or-more gross-rental-income test for dwelling units, with a separate transient-establishment exclusion. The Section 469 seven-day test does not itself select either recovery period.

Use the STR building-life guide and IRS Publication 946 to evaluate actual use, method and recovery period. Land is not depreciable, and shorter-life components require support rather than a blanket percentage assumption.

5. Review Services, Return Reporting and Personal Use

An activity can be nonpassive under Section 469 without automatically belonging on Schedule C. Substantial services primarily for occupants' convenience can change rental reporting and self-employment-tax treatment. Classify the actual services; routine between-guest cleaning alone should not be treated as conclusive. Review IRS Topic 414 with the preparer.

Track personal-use days independently. Use as a home can limit expenses under Section 280A; a participation log does not erase vacation-home restrictions. The applicable rules depend on rental days, personal days and other facts.

A Classification File the Preparer Can Use

  1. Export customer names or booking references, arrival/departure dates and rental periods; reconcile to gross receipts.
  2. Separate personal use, owner repair work, rental availability and actual customer use.
  3. Document services offered during stays and who performs them.
  4. Identify the participation test and retain owner, spouse and relevant third-party work records.
  5. Keep purchase allocations, building-use evidence, improvements and depreciation elections.
  6. Reconcile current losses and carryforwards before projecting a tax saving.

Start with the STR documentation kit, then confirm the scope of STR tax preparation. For long-term rentals and REPS, review spousal qualification and optional aggregation. Publication 925 explains the passive-activity and at-risk framework.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a long-term rental loss offset W-2 income above $150,000?

Potentially. The special active-participation allowance generally phases out by $150,000 of modified AGI, but a qualifying real estate professional who materially participates can have nonpassive rental losses. Other deduction limits still apply.

Does a seven-day average stay automatically make losses deductible?

No. The seven-day exception removes automatic rental classification for Section 469. The owner must establish material participation and satisfy other rules, including basis, at-risk, personal-use and applicable excess-business-loss limits.

Does short-term rental status determine the building depreciation period?

No. Section 469 customer-use rules and Section 168 building classification are separate. Determine the building recovery period from its actual use and applicable dwelling-unit and transient-establishment rules.

Does an STR automatically require Schedule C and self-employment tax?

No. The passive-activity classification does not by itself decide Schedule C reporting or self-employment tax. Services provided to occupants and the applicable rental-income rules need a separate analysis.
