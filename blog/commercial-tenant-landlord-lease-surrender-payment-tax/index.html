Our landlord is paying our operating business to surrender its commercial lease. Is the payout capital gain?

Published September 25, 2026 | Business Tax | AE Tax Advisors

A genuine payment the tenant receives for canceling its lease is treated as an amount received in exchange for that lease—not automatically as ordinary rent or automatically as preferential capital gain. Internal Revenue Code Section 1241 supplies the exchange rule, and IRS Publication 544 distinguishes the tenant's receipt from a landlord's tenant-paid cancellation fee. For a business leasehold held longer than one year, Section 1231 may govern the lease-right gain; its netting and five-year lookback can change the final character. A settlement that also pays for buildout, equipment, moving, or lost operations needs a supportable component-by-component review.

This is a decision for an operating-business principal or CFO negotiating the exit, not a consumer cash-for-keys question. Giving up a strategic location can affect the entity's return, owners' K-1s, fixed assets, a replacement lease, cash flow, and a pending business sale. The settlement should identify what the landlord actually acquires or reimburses before the parties sign and before a single amount is booked as “other income.”

What Section 1241 does—and does not—decide

Section 1241 treats amounts received by a lessee for cancellation of a lease as received in exchange for the lease. That solves the sale-or-exchange question for the lease right; it does not create tax basis, fix the holding period, or automatically classify the entire settlement as long-term capital gain. Publication 544's Section 1231 rules include business leaseholds held longer than one year, then require current-year netting with other Section 1231 items and a review of nonrecaptured Section 1231 losses from the preceding five years. A lease held for one year or less, a right held as inventory, or a different payment purpose needs another character analysis.

Consider the taxpayer that owns the lease. If an S corporation or partnership signed it, the entity's receipt, adjusted basis, and gain character must be reconciled on its return and then to its owners; the shareholder cannot simply put the landlord's check on a personal capital-gain schedule. If the payment is for something other than surrendering the lease—such as tenant-owned improvements acquired by the landlord or a separate business-interruption claim—Section 1241 does not automatically turn those dollars into lease-sale proceeds. Contract labels are evidence, not a substitute for the underlying rights and asset records.

Worked settlement: separate the lease right from the buildout

Assume an operating company has used its commercial space for several years and negotiates a $900,000 exit package. A contemporaneous agreement and valuation support $600,000 for surrender of the lease right, $200,000 for specifically identified tenant-owned fixtures that the landlord will acquire, and $100,000 designated for documented relocation costs. The company's tax records show $80,000 of remaining, unrecovered cost to acquire the lease right. On those simplified facts, the lease-right calculation begins with $600,000 amount realized less $80,000 adjusted lease basis, or $520,000 potential gain, before selling expenses and the Section 1231 netting and lookback tests. It is not a claim that $520,000 will receive a particular tax rate.

The $200,000 fixture component requires each asset's ownership, adjusted tax basis, disposition and depreciation-recapture analysis; it cannot be pooled into the lease-right basis or assumed to be a tax-free reimbursement. The $100,000 relocation component likewise requires an income-and-cost review rather than an automatic exclusion: some costs may be currently deductible, while new-site improvements or acquired rights may be capitalized. If the agreement actually pays one indivisible amount and the parties cannot substantiate these allocations, the return should not invent them after year-end. IRS Publication 334 treats money received by a tenant for lease cancellation as a property disposition, while Publication 544 explains character and recapture.

Decision tree before the tenant signs the surrender

  1. Identify the legal payor, recipient, and surrendered rights. Read the old lease, options, amendments, settlement and new occupancy documents. Is the landlord paying the tenant for cancellation, the tenant paying the landlord to exit, or is a building buyer funding a separate asset or business transaction? Those are not interchangeable tax events.
  2. Map consideration to each item. Document lease-right value, any paid acquisition or assignment cost, tenant-owned improvements and fixtures, rent or deposit true-ups, relocation support, and any lost-income or noncompete term. Require an agreement allocation that matches the economics and bank flows; do not rename ordinary or asset-sale items as Section 1241 consideration.
  3. Determine basis, holding period, and character. Roll forward any capitalized lease acquisition costs, amortization and legal fees. Test whether the business leasehold was held more than one year; then apply the entity's Section 1231 netting, five-year lookback, and any separate asset recapture rules. Form 4797 instructions provide the business-property reporting framework, but the actual form lines depend on the final classifications.
  4. Reconcile the departure and replacement site. Decide which old improvements were sold, removed, abandoned, or retained. Separately classify new-site lease payments, improvements and move costs. Coordinate the entity return, owner schedules, state return, and any estimated payments before the landlord's settlement becomes final.

Records and return-position failures to catch

Collect the signed lease and amendments, buyout offer, surrender agreement and valuation support; original lease-acquisition costs and amortization schedules; fixed-asset detail and invoices for buildout and movable equipment; proof of who legally and economically owns each asset; deposit and rent reconciliation; relocation invoices; five years of Form 4797/Section 1231 workpapers; and draft entity and owner returns. A site visit or closing inventory can document what the company removed versus left behind. If the landlord acquires tenant-owned improvements, resolve their tax disposition separately from the lease right; AE's tenant-improvement guide explains that ownership and remaining basis matter.

Failure points include calling every dollar capital gain because Section 1241 says “exchange”; reporting the entire receipt as ordinary rent because a landlord wrote the check; deducting all buildout basis while also treating its sale proceeds as lease-right consideration; and calling relocation money tax-free without testing income inclusion and the related costs. The landlord-receipt guide addresses the opposite direction, where the tenant pays the landlord and the IRS generally treats the receipt as ordinary income. The tenant-paid exit guide addresses another separate transaction. None of those rules should be copied onto a landlord-to-tenant payout.

AE can review the settlement and tax-basis schedules, compare the lease-right and asset allocations, model Section 1231 and owner-return consequences, and coordinate questions about legal rights with lease counsel before signing. This is general federal tax information; state law, entity ownership, the settlement's true purpose, and the applicable return year may change the result. No tax rate or savings is promised by the payment's label.
