Section 179 Deduction 2026: Complete Guide for Business Owners

Published on August 4, 2026 | Business Owner Tax Strategy | By AE Tax Advisors Team

Section 179 is the provision that lets a business deduct the full cost of qualifying equipment in the year it is placed in service instead of recovering it over five, seven, or fifteen years. It is not obscure and it is not aggressive. It is a deliberate congressional incentive, and it is one of the few places in the Code where the deduction is available on the full purchase price even when you financed almost all of it.

This guide covers the 2026 numbers, what actually qualifies, the limitations that catch business owners off guard, and how Section 179 fits alongside bonus depreciation now that 100% bonus is permanent.

The 2026 Limits

For the 2026 tax year, the Section 179 deduction limit is $2,560,000, and the dollar-for-dollar investment phaseout starts when total qualifying property placed in service exceeds $4,090,000. These limits come from IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32, section 4.24. For comparison, the enacted 2025 limits under the OBBBA were $2,500,000 and $4,000,000; those are historical figures, not the 2026 limits.

The investment phaseout uses all qualifying Section 179 property placed in service, not just the assets you elect to expense. In a 2026 illustration with $3,400,000 of qualifying placements, there is no investment-based reduction: the ceiling remains $2,560,000 before the business-income and other limits. At $4,360,000 of placements, the $270,000 excess over $4,090,000 reduces that ceiling to $2,290,000. At $6,650,000 or more, the investment phaseout reduces the available Section 179 dollar limit to zero. These are calculations from the published limits, not automatic deductions.

The Taxable Income Limitation Nobody Warns You About

Section 179 is subject to a separate active-business taxable-income limit under IRC Sec. 179(b)(3)(A). Suppose an S-Corp has $280,000 of qualifying active-business taxable income before Section 179 and places $600,000 of otherwise eligible, entirely business-use equipment in service. Assuming no other relevant items, the entity can currently deduct $280,000, not $600,000. A shareholder must also apply the owner-level income, basis, and other applicable limits to the amount passed through.

In that illustration, $320,000 is disallowed by the entity-level income limit and carried forward, subject to the rules in later years. A carryforward is not a current cash tax benefit and future use is not guaranteed. Model the entity and owner limits before committing to the purchase.

Bonus depreciation is not subject to the Section 179 business-income ceiling and can create a tax loss. Whether that loss reduces this year's tax still depends on basis, at-risk, passive-activity, excess-business-loss, and net-operating-loss rules as applicable.

What Property Qualifies

Qualifying property under IRC Sec. 179(d)(1) is tangible personal property used in the active conduct of a trade or business, used more than 50% for business. In practice that includes machinery and production equipment, computers and servers, off-the-shelf software, office furniture and fixtures, tools and diagnostic equipment, agricultural machinery, and business vehicles subject to the weight rules.

Section 179(f) also reaches certain improvements to nonresidential real property placed in service after the building was first placed in service: roofs, HVAC, fire protection and alarm systems, and security systems. That is a meaningful carve-out, because those items would otherwise sit in 39-year property.

Used equipment qualifies, as long as it is new to you. Property acquired from a related party, inherited, or received as a gift does not.

The Vehicle Rules

Passenger automobiles are subject to the depreciation limits under IRC Sec. 280F, which also constrain Section 179 expensing. The weight test generally uses unloaded gross vehicle weight for cars and gross vehicle weight rating for trucks and vans. A qualifying truck or van rated above 6,000 pounds may fall outside the passenger-auto dollar limits, but that does not remove the separate SUV, business-use, and substantiation rules.

The 2026 Section 179 cap for a covered SUV is $32,000. The statutory category generally covers four-wheeled passenger vehicles rated above 6,000 and not above 14,000 pounds gross vehicle weight. Exceptions include vehicles designed for more than nine passengers behind the driver; a cargo area at least six feet long that is not readily accessible from the passenger compartment; or an integral enclosed driver/load compartment meeting the seating and front-body requirements. Verify the actual vehicle specifications against IRS Publication 946, not a dealer's blanket “heavy vehicle” label. Eligible remaining business basis may qualify for bonus depreciation, subject to its own rules.

Business use above 50% is mandatory and must be substantiated with a contemporaneous mileage log. If business use later drops to 50% or below, IRC Sec. 179(d)(10) forces recapture of the excess benefit as ordinary income.

How Section 179 Interacts With 100% Bonus Depreciation

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for eligible property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. Acquisition and binding-contract rules matter: placing an older acquisition in service during 2026 does not by itself establish eligibility for the restored 100% rate. See IRS Publication 946 before choosing between Section 179 and bonus depreciation.

The ordering is fixed. Section 179 is elected first on the specific assets you designate. Bonus depreciation under IRC Sec. 168(k) then applies to the remaining basis of eligible property. Regular MACRS covers whatever is left.

With 100% bonus available on essentially all the same equipment and no dollar cap, Section 179 is no longer the workhorse. It is now a precision tool. Use it when you want to shelter a specific amount of income rather than all of it, when the asset is not bonus eligible, when your state decouples from federal bonus rules but conforms to 179, or when you want asset-by-asset control that a class-level bonus election cannot give you. Our full side-by-side is in the equipment leasing tax deduction guide.

The Elections Are Not Automatic

Make the Section 179 election on Form 4562, identifying the property and elected amount. An original return or a qualifying amended return may be used under the applicable election rules. A revocation is generally irrevocable; later changes and bonus-depreciation elections have their own rules. Have the preparer confirm the available procedure and deadline before assuming an old return can simply be changed.

Bonus depreciation runs the other direction: it applies automatically unless you affirmatively elect out, and the election out is made by asset class, not by individual asset. Business owners who assume they can sort it out later frequently discover that the return already made the choice for them.

Financing Changes Cash Timing, Not Equipment Cost

Consider a financing illustration, not a client result: a $500,000 machine with a 10% down payment uses $50,000 of initial cash. Assumed closing costs of 3% of the purchase price add $15,000, for $65,000 upfront. If the full $500,000 equipment basis is currently deductible and every deducted dollar offsets income otherwise taxed at an assumed 35% rate, the rate-only tax reduction is $175,000. That is not a guaranteed refund or a profit: $450,000 of principal remains to be repaid, plus interest and operating costs. Closing costs have their own tax treatment, and payment timing, deduction limits, QBI, state conformity, and later recapture can change the result. Buy equipment for an operational need and compare its after-tax lifetime cost. The mechanics are discussed in financing equipment with 10% down.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Section 179 limit for 2026?

The 2026 dollar limit is $2,560,000. It is reduced dollar-for-dollar when total qualifying Section 179 property placed in service exceeds $4,090,000 and reaches zero at $6,650,000. A separate active-business income limit and other restrictions still apply. The enacted 2025 limits were $2,500,000 and $4,000,000.

Can Section 179 create a net operating loss?

Section 179 is limited by active trade or business taxable income, with unused income-limited amounts generally carried forward. Pass-through entities and their owners apply separate limits. Bonus depreciation can create or increase a loss, but basis, at-risk, passive-activity, excess-business-loss, and net-operating-loss rules can defer its use.

Does used equipment qualify for Section 179?

Yes. Used equipment qualifies as long as it is new to your business and was not acquired from a related party, by gift, or by inheritance. The same is true of bonus depreciation, which has covered used property since the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act.

Do I have to pay cash for the equipment to claim Section 179?

No. A financed purchase can qualify based on eligible cost rather than just the down payment if you are the tax owner and the property is placed in service for qualifying business use. A financing lease must be analyzed for tax ownership; an accounting “capital lease” label alone is not enough. A true rental lease generally produces rent deductions instead. See capital lease vs operating lease.

When does the equipment have to be placed in service?

By the last day of the tax year in which you claim the deduction. Placed in service means ready and available for its intended use, not merely ordered, paid for, or delivered in a crate. Timing detail is covered in how to time equipment purchases.
