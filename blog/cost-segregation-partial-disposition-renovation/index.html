Can I deduct the old roof's remaining basis when replacing it on a commercial rental?

Published September 22, 2026 | Updated September 24, 2026 | Cost Segregation | AE Tax Advisors

Short answer: Possibly. If a commercial rental owner physically removes and abandons an old roof, a supported partial-disposition election may recognize its remaining adjusted basis as a loss while the replacement roof is capitalized separately. The old roof's original cost and depreciation—not the new contractor invoice—determine the potential loss. A cost segregation study can help prove the historical allocation, but the election does not automatically require a new study. Decide before the disposition-year return is filed, and preserve evidence before demolition.

For an owner approving a substantial roof or multi-system capital project, the decision affects the current return, the remaining building depreciation schedule, and the new improvement's future deductions. It is a basis and filing review, not a promise of an immediate cash-tax saving; passive-loss and other limits can affect when a recognized loss is usable.

Decide before demolition and before filing

  1. Was an identifiable part actually retired? Document the old roof or other component removed, its physical scope, and the year of disposition. A new addition without a corresponding retirement is not the same event.
  2. Can the old cost be supported? Start with original construction or purchase records, any existing cost segregation study, plans, and the depreciation schedule. If direct cost records are impracticable to obtain, have the tax team evaluate a reasonable, documented allocation method; do not use the new roof's price as old basis.
  3. Does the election and return treatment fit? The IRS Form 4797 instructions say an elective MACRS partial disposition is reported on a timely filed return, including extensions, for the year of disposition. Some partial dispositions are mandatory; classify the actual event rather than assuming every removal is elective. Have the preparer determine the applicable Form 4797 reporting and election description.
  4. Is the economics case real? Compare the supportable remaining basis, expected use of the loss, study or reconstruction cost, and impact on future depreciation. A fully depreciated old component or weak records may change the answer.

Identify the retired component, not just the new invoice

A renovation invoice shows the cost of the new roof, HVAC system, or interior work. It does not by itself establish the basis of the old component. The owner needs to determine whether a portion of a MACRS asset was actually disposed of, the original component's placed-in-service date, its unadjusted basis, depreciation taken, and adjusted basis at retirement. A building addition without removal of an old component is not automatically a partial disposition.

For qualifying elective partial dispositions, IRS Form 4797 instructions say the election is made on a timely filed return, including extensions, for the year of disposition. Some partial dispositions must be reported. A late decision can therefore be consequential; the tax team should flag component removals during project planning, not only when the return is due.

Where the cost segregation study helps

An existing engineering-based study may have identified the cost and classification of the old component. That can make the retirement basis more supportable. If there is no study, the preparer may need an appropriate method to estimate the historical cost of the removed portion and reconcile it to original building basis. The new improvement is then analyzed separately under the capitalization and depreciation rules. Avoid counting the removed component in both old and new schedules.

Do you need a new study? Not merely to make the election. The real test is whether the owner can substantiate the retired component's historical cost and adjusted basis. A study or engineering reconstruction can be valuable when original records are poor, several components are removed, or the property's basis is large enough to justify the work. If an existing study already identifies the roof, request its allocation workpapers first. A post-demolition study cannot recreate missing photographs or prove that a specific asset was actually removed; coordinate the scope with the return preparer before commissioning it.

The IRS's partial-disposition practice unit asks whether the taxpayer can substantiate ownership, actual disposition, placed-in-service date, adjusted basis, and a reduction of the remaining asset's basis. These are concrete workpaper requirements, not just an election label.

Illustrative roof replacement

Assume a commercial-property owner replaces an entire old roof with a $650,000 roof. The old roof's supported original cost within the building is $240,000, and $150,000 of depreciation was allowed or allowable through the relevant calculation date. Its simplified remaining adjusted basis is $90,000. If the roof was actually abandoned and the election and other requirements are met, the owner and preparer can evaluate a $90,000 disposition loss; they must also remove the old $240,000 cost and $150,000 accumulated depreciation from the building records. The $650,000 replacement is separately analyzed and capitalized as appropriate. This is not a $90,000 tax saving, and final depreciation, loss character, timing, and use depend on the return facts.

The IRS Publication 551 roof example illustrates the same old-cost-minus-depreciation roll-forward and reports its qualified abandonment loss on Form 4797; it separately capitalizes the new roof. Do not double count the old roof by claiming a loss while continuing to depreciate its historical cost in the building account.

Build a before-and-after asset roll-forward

Keep demolition records, photographs before and after, contractor scope, invoices identifying removed components, original acquisition or construction documents, the existing depreciation schedule, and the method used to estimate old cost. Reconcile old basis removed, accumulated depreciation removed, new capitalized cost, and remaining basis. If a study is commissioned after renovation, provide pre-project photos and plans; an inspection of the finished building alone may not show what was removed.

For a property owned through a partnership or S corporation, also bring the entity's filed return, tax depreciation detail, ownership and activity information, and any prior cost segregation report. The asset-level answer must flow through the entity return and owner-level loss limits; a disposition entry in fixed-asset software is not the entire tax analysis. If a prior-year return already omitted the retirement, obtain the filed returns and schedules before assuming a late election, amendment, or Form 3115 is available.

IRS Publication 544 and the Form 4797 instructions discuss partial dispositions. See the cost segregation guide and repairs versus improvements for the surrounding decisions.

Coordinate three schedules

The workpaper should show the old building or component, the disposed portion, and the new improvement as separate schedules. First reconcile the original asset to tax basis and depreciation allowed or allowable. Next identify how much of that asset was physically retired and calculate its remaining basis. Finally classify and place the new work in service. If the same project includes repairs, capital improvements, and removals, allocate costs by scope rather than applying one tax label to the whole invoice.

A cost segregation firm and tax preparer should agree on the scope before the contractor disposes of old materials. Photos, measurements, and demolition records are hard to recreate after the work is complete. If the owner acquired the property recently, purchase-price allocation records can inform the historical component cost; if the building is decades old, cost-estimation assumptions need more explanation. A partial-disposition election may not be beneficial in every case, so compare the supported tax result with the preparation effort and other loss limits.

Failure points that change the return

  • New cost mistaken for old basis. The $650,000 replacement invoice does not prove the retired roof's $240,000 historic cost.
  • Loss taken with no basis reduction. The old component's cost and accumulated depreciation must be removed from the continuing asset records.
  • One invoice, one tax label. A project may contain removal, capital improvement, and separately analyzed repair scopes; the IRS tangible-property audit guide discusses how partial disposition and improvement rules interact.
  • Election noticed after filing. Do not assume a routine amended return or Form 3115 can repair a missed election; timing and method-change relief require a fact-specific review.
  • Gross loss confused with usable deduction. Entity and owner activity limits, basis, and other tax attributes may defer or limit current benefit.
