7 Common 1031 Exchange Mistakes That Trigger a Taxable Event

Published on April 8, 2026 | Real Estate Investor Tax | AE Tax Advisors

A properly executed Section 1031 exchange can defer gain on qualifying investment or business real estate. Some mistakes defeat the exchange, while others create partial taxable boot rather than making the entire transaction taxable. Before closing, verify the deadlines, identification, ownership, cash restrictions, liabilities, and related parties. These seven checks help distinguish a workable exchange from a tax result that differs from your plan.

1. Missing the 45-Day Identification Deadline

The ordinary identification deadline is 45 calendar days after the relinquished-property transfer under Section 1031(a)(3)(A). Identify the property in signed writing and deliver it to a permitted exchange participant, not yourself or a disqualified person. Weekends, holidays, and ordinary market or financing delays do not move the deadline. Applicable IRS relief can postpone it, including qualifying disaster relief under Revenue Procedure 2018-58. Check the IRS notice, taxpayer or transaction eligibility, and affected dates; a FEMA declaration alone is not enough.

Many investors treat this window casually, assuming they will find something before time runs out. When they do not, the exchange collapses and the full gain from the sale is taxable in that year. To protect yourself, begin your replacement property search before you even list the relinquished property. Use the three-property rule (identify up to three properties of any value) or the 200% rule (identify any number of properties whose combined fair market value does not exceed 200% of the relinquished property's value) to give yourself options. Deliver the written identification to your qualified intermediary or exchange accommodation titleholder well before the deadline.

2. Missing the 180-Day Closing Deadline

Receive identified replacement property by the earlier of 180 calendar days after transferring the relinquished property or the federal income-tax return due date for the transfer year, including extensions. Both periods run from the original transfer. For a November sale, an April return deadline can occur before day 180, so calculate the exact dates with your preparer rather than assuming every exchange gets six months.

If your ordinary return due date comes before day 180, arrange a timely filing extension when available. It can prevent that earlier date from shortening the exchange period, but it does not add time beyond day 180 or extend the time to pay tax. Check separately whether applicable IRS relief changes the dates.

3. Taking Constructive Receipt of Exchange Funds

If you receive or control all sale proceeds before receiving replacement property, Treasury Regulation 1.1031(k)-1(f) generally treats the transaction as a sale rather than a deferred exchange. Briefly depositing the proceeds in your own account does not preserve the exchange. A permitted distribution of boot in an otherwise qualifying exchange can produce partial recognition, so not every cash receipt invalidates the whole transaction.

Actual withdrawal is not the only concern: an unrestricted right to demand funds can be constructive receipt. Use a written agreement that meets the safe-harbor restrictions on receiving, pledging, or borrowing the funds. A qualified intermediary is the usual delayed-exchange route. Ask when funds may be released under the agreement and regulations, and verify account controls and payment instructions before closing.

4. Exchanging Into Non-Like-Kind Property

Section 1031(a)(1) requires qualifying real property held for business or investment and replacement property of like kind held for a qualifying purpose. An apartment building may be like-kind to raw land, a strip center, or a rental house. Ordinary securities and partnership interests are not qualifying real property, and Section 1031(a)(2) excludes property held primarily for sale. Personal-use homes do not meet the investment or business-use requirement. U.S. real estate is not like-kind to foreign real estate, though foreign-to-foreign exchanges may qualify.

Investors sometimes attempt to exchange into a property they intend to use personally, hoping to convert it to investment use later. The IRS has scrutinized these arrangements closely, particularly after Revenue Procedure 2008-16 established safe harbor rules for dwelling units. If you plan to acquire a property that has any personal-use component, consult with your tax advisor before closing to confirm the property qualifies. For the replacement-dwelling day counts and pre-closing records, use AE's rental-to-vacation-home exchange decision guide.

5. Failing to Reinvest the Full Sale Price (Boot Recognition)

For full deferral, model the entire exchange rather than only the purchase price: cash retained, non-like-kind property, liability relief and offsets, eligible exchange expenses, and separate recapture rules. Buying down, using exchange funds for personal debts, or retaining cash can create boot. Under the general boot rule, recognized gain is limited to realized gain; its tax character is determined separately.

Simplified illustration: selling a debt-free property for $1,000,000, acquiring an $850,000 replacement, and retaining $150,000 cash produces $150,000 cash boot before exchange expenses. Recognition is limited to realized gain, with recapture reviewed separately. If a $400,000 old loan is replaced by $300,000 debt, $100,000 of additional cash paid can offset that debt reduction. Matching the old borrowing is not mandatory. Conversely, a higher purchase price or additional borrowing does not by itself eliminate cash retained.

6. Not Using a Qualified Intermediary (or Using a Disqualified Person)

Treasury Regulation 1.1031(k)-1(g)(4) provides the QI safe harbor for a deferred exchange. The QI must not be a disqualified person. Recent employees, attorneys, accountants, investment bankers or brokers, and real estate agents or brokers generally count as agents during the two-year lookback; specified related-person relationships also matter. The agent test excludes prior qualifying-exchange services and specified routine financial, title-insurance, escrow, or trust services. An attorney or title company is not automatically disqualified solely because it handled an earlier exchange or routine covered service. Review the actual role and relationships before appointment.

A properly structured direct simultaneous exchange or another available safe harbor may qualify without a QI, so the absence of a QI is not automatically constructive receipt. For the usual delayed sale-and-replacement transaction, however, engage a suitable independent QI before closing. Check funds segregation, authorized release controls, insurance, financial condition, and applicable state regulation. For the distinction between exchange accommodation titleholders and qualified intermediaries, an EAT parks property for a QEAA reverse-exchange safe harbor, while a QI facilitates the exchange transfers.

7. Ignoring the Related Party Rules Under IRC Sec. 1031(f)

Section 1031(f) applies to related persons under Sections 267(b) and 707(b)(1), including specified family and ownership relationships. In a direct related-party exchange, disposition of exchanged property by either party within two years after the last exchange transfer generally triggers recognition in the year of the later disposition, not automatic amendment of the exchange-year return. Exceptions include death, a qualifying involuntary conversion, or showing that tax avoidance was not a principal purpose. Certain risk-reduction arrangements suspend the holding-period clock. File Form 8824 for the exchange and the following two years when required.

There is a separate anti-avoidance problem when a QI buys replacement property from a related party who receives cash or non-like-kind property. Revenue Ruling 2002-83 denies deferral on its stated facts; holding your replacement property for two years does not fix that related-party cash-out structure. Have the tax advisor review both sides, ownership attribution, basis, dispositions, and any claimed exception before signing. Hardship or a business-purpose memo is not an automatic exemption.

Get It Right the First Time

A failed or partially taxable exchange can create substantial current tax. Recognized gain may include ordinary-income recapture, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain subject to a maximum 25% federal rate for individuals, other capital or Section 1231 gain, NIIT where applicable, and state tax. Those categories are not all taxed at one rate, and recapture is not simply added again to a gain amount that already includes it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What property qualifies for a 1031 exchange?

Both properties must be qualifying real property held for investment or business use. A rental house may be like-kind to a warehouse or raw land. Personal-use residences and property held primarily for sale do not qualify. U.S. real property is not like-kind to foreign real property, but foreign-to-foreign exchanges may qualify if the other requirements are met.

What is boot in a 1031 exchange?

Boot includes cash, non-like-kind property, and net debt relief after permitted offsets. Additional cash paid can replace debt, so equal replacement borrowing is not mandatory. A more expensive replacement or extra borrowing does not by itself erase cash retained. Recognized gain is generally limited to the lesser of realized gain or boot after eligible exchange expenses, with recapture rules reviewed separately.

Can I 1031 exchange a short-term rental?

Yes, provided it is genuinely held for investment or business use rather than personal enjoyment. Significant personal use undermines the position, and the exchange must be structured before closing on the sale, not afterward.

What are the 1031 exchange deadlines?

Identify replacement property in signed writing within 45 calendar days after transferring the relinquished property. Receive it by the earlier of 180 calendar days or the due date of the federal return for the transfer year, including extensions. Both periods run from the transfer, not consecutively. Applicable IRS relief may postpone deadlines; ordinary weekends, holidays, and financing delays do not.
