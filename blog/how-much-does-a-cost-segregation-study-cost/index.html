How Much Does a Cost Segregation Study Cost?

Published on August 12, 2026 | Updated September 24, 2026 | Cost Segregation | AE Tax Advisors

Published market prices for a cost segregation study range from a few hundred dollars for a self-service or software-led product to $15,000 or more for a complex engineering engagement. A residential quote may fall in the low thousands, while a large, renovated, mixed-use, or special-purpose property can require substantially more work. Those prices are not automatically comparable: one may cover only a report, while another includes site review, tax oversight, Form 3115 support, return implementation, and audit assistance.

The decision is not “Is the fee lower than the projected deduction?” A deduction is not cash, and a large depreciation number can be suspended or produce less value than the proposal suggests. Compare the fee with the present value of tax actually deferred, after passive-loss, basis, at-risk, excess-business-loss, state, hold-period, and recapture effects.

What a Cost Segregation Quote Should Include

The IRS does not publish an approved fee schedule or certify providers. Its Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide instead focuses on the quality of the methodology, documentation, legal analysis, cost allocations, and reconciliation to the taxpayer's records. Price is therefore a purchasing fact, not proof that a study is adequate.

Factors That Affect Pricing

The primary driver of cost is the complexity and size of the property. A single-family rental home purchased for $400,000 requires less engineering analysis than a 50-unit apartment complex or a commercial office building. Other factors include the availability of construction documentation (blueprints, invoices, and contractor records), whether a physical site inspection is required, and the geographic location of the property.

Studies can use actual-cost records, detailed estimates, modeling, or sampling. A site visit can be valuable when records are incomplete or components are difficult to verify, but a physical visit is not a stand-alone IRS safe harbor. The defensibility question is whether the method fits the facts, the report explains its assumptions, the classifications are supported, and the result reconciles to the taxpayer's basis.

Typical Price Ranges by Property Type

Current published provider pricing spans a much wider range than legacy engineering quotes: self-service or software-led products may start below $1,000; reviewed residential and small-property work often lands in the low thousands; and tailored engineering engagements for large or complex commercial property can run into five figures. These are market observations, not AE quotes or IRS standards. Obtain a written scope because two proposals at the same price may include materially different work.

Some firms charge a flat fee while others use a percentage of the tax savings identified. Be cautious with percentage-based pricing, it can create incentives to be overly aggressive in reclassifications, which could increase audit risk. A reputable firm will charge a fee based on the scope of work rather than the outcome.

Two Cost Segregation Quotes That Are Not Actually Comparable

Worked quote comparison: older short-term rental

Owner A receives a $1,200 proposal for a study on a short-term rental placed in service three years ago. The proposal includes a PDF allocation but excludes depreciation-history review, Form 3115, the Section 481(a) computation, state adjustments, and return implementation. A second proposal is $4,000 and includes those items plus preparer support. The first proposal is not necessarily bad, but the owner's total project cost is unknown until the omitted work is priced. The lower study fee should not be presented as the lower all-in tax-compliance cost.

Worked quote comparison: renovated commercial building

Owner B bought a building, completed two renovation phases, received tenant reimbursements, and placed different portions in service on different dates. A $3,500 modeling quote assumes one building and one placed-in-service date. A $9,000 engagement reconciles the acquisition, construction draws, retirements, reimbursements, and phased service dates. The higher fee may reflect a different scope rather than a premium price for the same study. Before choosing, the owner should require both providers to state which basis, phases, and return schedules their fee covers.

Calculating Your Return on Investment

A first-pass calculation may divide estimated current tax reduction by the all-in project fee, but the decision requires more than that ratio. For a hypothetical property with $500,000 of depreciable basis, assume a study moves $150,000 into shorter recovery periods. The current deduction is not automatically $150,000: calculate depreciation using the applicable placed-in-service date, class life, convention, election, and bonus-depreciation rules described in IRS Publication 946. Then determine how much of that deduction is allowed on the return and at what marginal tax rate.

For a smaller illustration, assume a rental has $240,000 of depreciable basis and the study identifies $72,000 of shorter-lived components. Do not label $23,040—$72,000 multiplied by a 32% rate—as current savings until the return model confirms the deduction timing, loss usability, and state treatment. If the loss is suspended, the present value may be far lower even though the classification itself is correct.

A Five-Gate Break-Even Test Before You Pay

  1. Eligible basis: Remove land, nondepreciable amounts, and any basis not owned by the taxpayer commissioning the study.
  2. Incremental timing: Compare depreciation with and without the study by year; do not count total reclassified basis as an immediate deduction.
  3. Loss usability: Apply basis, at-risk, passive-activity, excess-business-loss, and other return-level limits.
  4. Exit effects: Model the expected hold period, Section 1245 recapture, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, and disposition costs.
  5. All-in fee: Add the study, Form 3115 preparation when applicable, return implementation, state work, and any continuing support.

For a lookback engagement, the provider should identify who determines method-change eligibility and prepares the filing package under the current Instructions for Form 3115. “Form 3115 included” should mean more than inserting a catch-up number into tax software.

When It May Not Be Worth the Cost

There is no universal minimum basis that makes a study worthwhile. Smaller-basis or land-heavy properties have less depreciable cost to analyze, but fee level, asset mix, tax rate, placed-in-service year, loss usability, and hold period can change the answer. Use a property-specific projection instead of treating $150,000, $200,000, or $500,000 as an automatic cutoff.

A near-term sale can also compress the deferral period and trigger different recapture rules for Section 1245 components and depreciable real property. A study can still be useful in some sale-year or suspended-loss situations, but the exit return must be modeled. The strongest candidate generally has meaningful eligible basis, an identifiable way to use the deductions, and a hold period long enough for the timing benefit to exceed the all-in fee and exit cost.

Documents to Gather Before Requesting Quotes

  • Purchase agreement, closing statement, appraisal, and support for the land allocation.
  • Construction draws, contractor invoices, change orders, blueprints, and renovation dates.
  • Placed-in-service dates for each building and improvement phase.
  • Current fixed-asset ledger, prior Forms 4562, and depreciation schedules.
  • Prior Form 3115 filings and Forms 8582 showing suspended passive losses.
  • Ownership entities, federal and state returns involved, and the anticipated hold period.
  • Any pending refinance, sale, exchange, casualty, demolition, or renovation plan.

Common Pricing and Scope Failures

  • Comparing only the headline fee. One bid may be an estimate; another may be a complete tax implementation engagement.
  • Paying based on projected savings. The provider has an incentive to enlarge the projection unless assumptions are independently reviewed.
  • Leaving the tax preparer out. A technically sound report can still be implemented incorrectly on the return.
  • Assuming every short-life asset receives the same first-year treatment. Dates, conventions, elections, and property eligibility matter.
  • Ignoring state depreciation. Federal bonus treatment may not carry into the state return.
  • Failing to reconcile basis. The study total, tax ledger, purchase allocation, and later sale schedules must tell the same story.
  • Buying a study without a loss-use plan. A suspended deduction can be valid but may not justify an immediate fee.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a cost segregation study cost?

Published market prices range from a few hundred dollars for self-service or software-led products to $15,000 or more for complex engineering engagements. Compare the property scope, methodology, site-review assumptions, asset-level schedules, Form 3115 work, return implementation, and audit support included in each quote.

Is a cost segregation study worth the cost?

It depends on depreciable basis, the timing benefit after tax, study and implementation fees, loss usability, hold period, and exit-year recapture. A property-value threshold or projected deduction alone does not establish that the engagement is worthwhile.

What is a cost segregation study?

A cost segregation study analyzes eligible building costs and assigns components to the appropriate tax classifications and recovery periods. The resulting depreciation timing depends on placed-in-service dates, asset eligibility, conventions, elections, accounting-method history, and the law applicable to the property.

How much of a property typically gets reclassified?

It depends on the asset class. Office and warehouse properties commonly reclassify 15% to 25% of depreciable basis, multifamily 20% to 35%, restaurants and self-storage 30% to 40%, and hotels 30% to 45%. Furnished short-term rentals usually land between 25% and 35%.

Can I do a study on a property I bought years ago?

Often, but the correction procedure depends on the depreciation history and current accounting-method rules. A qualifying automatic method change may use Form 3115 and a Section 481(a) adjustment, while other fact patterns require a different treatment. Confirm eligibility before assuming an amended return is unnecessary.
