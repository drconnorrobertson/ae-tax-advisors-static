Real Estate Agent Tax Planning: Retirement, Records and Rentals

Published August 10, 2026 | Updated November 6, 2026 | Tax Strategy | AE Tax Advisors

A real estate agent with $180,000 of net Schedule C commission profit should compare self-employment tax with a properly modeled S-corp return. The Social Security wage base, Medicare tax, deductions, reasonable salary, and state costs all affect the result; gross commissions alone do not establish savings.

Brokerage is a qualifying real property trade or business under IRS Publication 925. That gives a working agent a plausible path to real-estate-professional status, but each year's hours and any rental's material participation still must be proved. This page addresses agent planning broadly; the S-corp and rental-ownership review addresses whether rental property belongs in the commission company.

When the S corporation election is worth modeling

For example, an agent with $180,000 of net profit could compare remaining a sole proprietor with an S corporation paying a $95,000 W-2 wage and passing through the balance. That is not a $12,000 guaranteed saving: the wage must be reasonable, and employer payroll tax, the self-employment-tax wage base, QBI effects, state tax, payroll processing, and return fees belong in the same comparison.

The salary has to be reasonable. For an agent, the analysis looks at what a comparable licensed producer would earn for the same production and how much revenue the agent personally generates. There is no universally defensible 50/50 or 60/40 split. A team with support staff and independent lead generation can have a different wage analysis from a solo producer; document duties, time, and comparable pay under the IRS reasonable-compensation guidance.

An S corporation is not required to establish a retirement plan. A sole proprietor can also adopt an eligible plan, with contributions based on adjusted net earnings from self-employment. For an S corporation, employer contributions generally depend on eligible W-2 compensation, not distributions. Entity selection and plan selection should be modeled together; see IRS Publication 560.

There is a real estate specific complication. Some brokerages will only pay commissions to a licensed individual, not to an entity. Many states allow payment to a licensed professional entity, but the rules vary and the brokerage's policy matters as much as the statute. This should be confirmed before the election, not after.

Real estate professional status and rental participation are separate tests

An agent who materially participates in real property trades or businesses for more than 750 hours annually, and for more than half of all personal service time, qualifies as a real estate professional under IRC Sec. 469(c)(7).

Full-time brokerage work can count toward both tests, but a license or job title does not prove the hours, the more-than-half comparison, or the employee ownership rule. Real-estate-professional status does not by itself make rental losses deductible. Each rental generally must pass material participation individually; a valid election may combine rental interests for that test, but the election alone does not prove participation. See IRS Publication 925 for the current framework.

If the individual meets the real-estate-professional tests and materially participates in the rental (or validly aggregated rentals), its otherwise allowable loss may offset nonpassive commission income, subject to basis, at-risk, excess-business-loss, and other limits. A cost-segregation study affects depreciation timing, not those tests. An agent netting $220,000 who buys a $600,000 rental should model the property-specific basis, study allocation, rental participation, and eventual recapture before assuming any amount of first-year commission income can be sheltered. The separate ownership question is analyzed in our agent S-corp and rental-property decision guide.

Keep credible records identifying the work performed, dates and time spent, distinguishing brokerage activity from rental participation and other personal-service work. Contemporaneous logs can help, but Treasury Regulation 1.469-5T(f)(4) permits proof of participation by reasonable means, including appointment books, calendars and narrative summaries. It does not require contemporaneous daily logs in every case. A later reconstruction is not automatically rejected or accepted: it must credibly establish the actual services and hours, without unsupported estimates or double counting.

Vehicle method choices and business-use records

Compare standard mileage with actual expenses using the vehicle's cost, expected business use and permitted depreciation. For an owned car, choosing the standard mileage method in its first year of business availability generally preserves the option to use actual expenses later. A later switch requires the applicable straight-line depreciation and basis adjustments. Prior MACRS, Section 179 or special depreciation can prevent use of standard mileage. A leased vehicle using standard mileage generally must continue that method throughout the lease, including renewals. Review the complete history before changing methods; see IRS Publication 463.

Keep mileage, business purpose, dates and total-use records. Commuting is generally personal. A vehicle's weight alone does not establish a full deduction. Passenger-auto classification, business use, the separate Section 179 heavy-SUV limit, bonus eligibility and possible recapture all matter. For listed property, accelerated treatment generally requires more than 50% qualified business use. Choose a vehicle for the business need, then calculate eligible deductions rather than treating a heavy SUV as a tax-free purchase.

Home-office eligibility before reimbursement

An office used for personal activities as well as work generally fails the exclusive-use test. For an agent's ordinary office, regular and exclusive use and a qualifying business purpose, such as a principal place of business, must be established. Administrative or management use can qualify under its conditions, including the absence of another fixed location where substantial administrative or management activities are conducted. A license, desk or occasional work from home is not enough. Review IRS Publication 587.

For an S-corporation shareholder-employee, evaluate reimbursement of eligible, substantiated expenses under an accountable arrangement. Business connection, timely substantiation and return of excess payments matter. A written policy alone does not make personal housing costs deductible or guarantee tax-free reimbursement. Keep the corporation's records and the owner's supporting expense allocation consistent; Publication 463 explains accountable-plan requirements.

Retirement planning for variable commissions in 2026

For 2026, the regular employee 401(k) deferral limit is $24,500 and the defined-contribution annual-additions ceiling is $72,000, subject to compensation, plan terms and other limits. Eligible catch-up contributions are separate. These figures are ceilings, not automatic deductions. Verify the year with IRS retirement limits.

A one-participant 401(k) can be considered when owner-only eligibility is met, including the rules for a working spouse. Review eligible staff and related employers before assuming an owner-only plan is available. Employee deferrals across plans and related-employer rules can constrain contributions. Sole proprietors use the self-employed contribution calculation; S-corporation distributions are not plan compensation.

Compare a 401(k), SEP or other eligible design using administrative cost, employee coverage, cash flow and plan terms. Some employer profit-sharing contributions are discretionary, but required contributions under a particular design and amounts already elected cannot simply be ignored in a weak year. A cash balance plan needs actuarial calculations, employee-coverage review and an ongoing funding analysis, not an age-based promised deduction.

Plan adoption, employee elections, deposits and employer funding have separate deadlines. Some employer funding can occur by a return deadline including extensions, while employee elections and deposit rules differ. Confirm the applicable entity, plan and year with the administrator before relying on a later deadline. Traditional and Roth contributions have different current tax treatment. See Publication 560.

Worked Example: $310,000 Producer

A 46-year-old agent nets $310,000 on a schedule C, owns two rentals, and has no entity or retirement plan.

This is an illustration, not a client result. Assume the agent evaluates an S corporation with $150,000 of reasonable eligible W-2 compensation, qualifies for an owner-only 401(k), has no competing contributions and satisfies the plan and timing rules. A 25% employer contribution would be $37,500. Adding a $24,500 employee deferral gives $62,000 for 2026, below the $72,000 annual-additions ceiling. At age 46, the example has no age-based catch-up. Pre-tax treatment is assumed; Roth amounts do not produce the same current deduction. Actual compensation, plan terms, contributions elsewhere and deadlines must be checked.

Assume the agent documents enough qualifying brokerage hours to pass both real-estate-professional tests, properly elects to combine the two rentals, and materially participates in that combined rental activity under one of the IRS tests. Assume defensible studies produce $186,000 of otherwise allowable depreciation. Only after confirming basis, at-risk, passive-loss, and other individual limits could those deductions reduce nonpassive income instead of being suspended.

The illustrative $62,000 of retirement contributions and $186,000 of accelerated depreciation are separate potential tax items, not a guaranteed $248,000 reduction in taxable income or a fixed tax saving. The applicable contribution limit, reasonable salary, property facts, loss limitations, state conformity, and future depreciation recapture must be modeled on complete returns before quoting a net benefit.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should a real estate agent elect S corporation status?

There is no universal profit threshold. Compare a reasonable W-2 wage, self-employment and payroll taxes, QBI, state tax, and the cost of payroll and a separate return. Confirm first that your brokerage and state rules permit commissions to be paid to the entity.

Do real estate agents automatically qualify as real estate professionals?

No. Qualifying brokerage work may count toward the more-than-half and over-750-hour tests, but hours, ownership, and material participation must be proved for the individual year. Each rental generally must also meet material participation separately, or a valid election may combine rental interests for that test; making the election alone does not establish participation.

What documentation do I need for real estate professional status?

Keep credible records of actual services and hours. Contemporaneous logs can help, but the participation regulation permits other reasonable means, including appointment books, calendars and narrative summaries. Unsupported estimates and double counting do not establish eligibility.

Is a vehicle over 6,000 pounds automatically deductible?

No. Confirm vehicle classification, business use, acquisition and service dates, Section 179 and bonus rules, and possible recapture. Weight alone does not establish a full deduction.

Do I need an S corporation for a retirement plan?

No. Eligible sole proprietors can also establish plans. Self-employed earnings calculations differ from S-corporation W-2 compensation, and eligible employees, related employers and plan terms must be reviewed.

Does $150,000 of W-2 compensation automatically support $72,000 of contributions?

No. In the stated 2026 illustration, a 25% employer contribution is $37,500 and a $24,500 employee deferral produces $62,000, assuming eligibility, no other contributions and all plan and timing conditions. The annual ceiling is not an entitlement.
