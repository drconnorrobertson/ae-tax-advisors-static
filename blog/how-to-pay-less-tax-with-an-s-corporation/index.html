How to Pay Less Tax With an S Corporation

Coordinate owner pay, QBI and retirement contributions before deciding how much to distribute.

By AE Tax Advisors · November 22, 2026 · 5 minute read · 3:16 video

Watch: S corporation tax planning with AE Tax Advisors

How can you pay less tax with an S corporation? Start by reviewing the interaction between a defensible owner salary, business income, the qualified business income deduction and retirement contributions. Looking at payroll taxes alone can miss the larger planning decision.

Watch this video on YouTube · Subscribe to AE Tax Advisors

The example is a planning illustration.

The recording compares two approaches for a business with $500,000 of profit. Its chart assumes 2026 rules, married filing jointly, a non-SSTB business and federal taxes only. The illustrated tax difference also includes retirement contributions. It is not a promise of savings, a universal salary recommendation or a complete tax projection for your household.

1. Start with reasonable compensation

An S corporation owner who performs services needs to address reasonable compensation before treating payments as non-wage distributions. The IRS considers the work performed and the business facts; a fixed salary-to-distribution percentage does not establish the right amount.

Document your duties, time, experience, responsibilities and comparable compensation. The video's $250,000 and $150,000 salaries are scenario inputs, not an instruction to cut every owner's pay. See our guide to reasonable S corporation owner compensation for the records behind that decision.

2. Model payroll tax and income tax together

Wages and pass-through business income have different tax consequences. Reducing wages can change employment taxes, but the outcome depends on applicable wage bases, Medicare rules and the owner's other earnings. Do not apply one flat payroll-tax percentage to every dollar.

Also distinguish the corporation's taxable income reported on Schedule K-1 from the cash it distributes. Moving cash does not, by itself, determine taxable income. A useful projection reconciles wages, employer payroll costs, business deductions, pass-through income and household taxable income.

3. Include the QBI deduction

Eligible owners may qualify for a deduction of up to 20% of qualified business income, subject to the applicable limits. Reasonable compensation paid to an S corporation shareholder is excluded from QBI. That makes compensation one part of the calculation, but lowering salary does not automatically maximize the deduction.

Business type, taxable income, wages and other limitations can affect the result. Review our S corporation QBI deduction guide alongside the video.

4. Compare retirement contributions and spendable cash

The alternative scenario in the recording includes a traditional 401(k) deferral and an employer profit-sharing contribution. That changes the tax comparison and moves money into retirement rather than leaving all of it available to spend.

S corporation distributions do not create earned income for retirement-plan contribution purposes. Contributions depend on eligible compensation and the plan's rules. Traditional contributions generally defer income tax; later taxable withdrawals still need to be considered. Employee deferrals generally do not remove wages from Social Security and Medicare taxes.

Ask your advisor to compare total tax, retirement funding, employer costs and remaining cash in the same projection. If the business has employees, include plan eligibility and contribution obligations before assuming an owner-only result.

A practical checklist before changing owner pay

  1. Reconcile year-to-date business profit, payroll and distributions.
  2. Update the owner's reasonable-compensation analysis.
  3. Project household income and the QBI deduction under each scenario.
  4. Confirm retirement-plan eligibility, contribution capacity and deadlines.
  5. Compare taxes and cash after retirement funding, then implement payroll changes correctly.
