Why is my taxable gain higher than the cash I received when I sold my rental property?

Published September 22, 2026 | Real Estate Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Because mortgage principal payoff reduces your closing check but generally does not reduce taxable gain. Gain is based on sale consideration less eligible selling expenses and adjusted tax basis—not on the net bank wire. Broker commissions and other costs directly tied to selling the rental may reduce amount realized; purchase costs and capital improvements may already be in basis, while depreciation lowers basis. Property-tax and rent prorations, loan interest, tenant deposits, and repair charges need separate classification instead of being called “selling costs” because they appear on the same statement.

If you have a contract or closing disclosure, AE can reconcile each line against the asset ledger before the gain and depreciation-recapture figures are filed. Bring the purchase and sale statements, improvement records, and all depreciation schedules, especially if you had a cost-segregation study.

Use three separate tax buckets, not the net wire amount

IRS Publication 544 calculates gain as amount realized minus adjusted basis. Its sale example subtracts selling expenses from consideration and separately reduces basis for depreciation. The Form 4797 instructions likewise distinguish gross sales price from cost or other basis plus expense of sale when reporting qualifying business property. That means the closing agent's single “cash to seller” number is only a cash reconciliation, not the gain computation.

  • Amount realized: start with the price and other consideration, including relevant debt assumed by the buyer, then reduce it by costs directly facilitating the sale, such as the seller's broker commission and qualifying conveyance charges. Review legal, title, escrow, advertising, and transfer charges by the service actually provided and who bore it.
  • Adjusted basis: reconstruct acquisition basis, eligible original buying costs, and capital improvements, then account for depreciation allowed or allowable and other basis changes. IRS Publication 551 explains purchase-side basis and adjustments; these amounts are not new selling expenses just because they reduce the final gain.
  • Other closing cash flows: mortgage principal payoff changes cash and debt, not the underlying gain by itself. Accrued interest or a payoff charge may require a separate tax analysis. Property-tax, rent, utility, security-deposit, and repair allocations likewise do not all belong in one sale-expense bucket.

Do not deduct the same charge twice—once through basis or Schedule E and again as a sale expense. The rental purchase-closing-cost guide addresses acquisition-side basis; this page addresses the seller's closing worksheet.

Worked example: taxable gain can exceed cash received

Assume an owner sells a wholly rental property for $800,000. The seller pays a $40,000 broker commission and $6,000 of qualifying transfer and closing charges. The adjusted tax basis—including the original allowable purchase costs and improvements, but after accumulated depreciation—is $500,000. Amount realized is $754,000 ($800,000 less $46,000 of selling expenses), and the preliminary gain is $254,000. If the closing agent also pays off a $300,000 mortgage, the seller receives about $454,000 before other prorations. Neither the $300,000 payoff nor that $454,000 wire changes the $254,000 preliminary gain calculation.

This is not a $254,000 “capital gain taxed at one rate.” The property may contain land, a Section 1250 building, and Section 1245 components from cost segregation. Sale price, selling costs, and adjusted basis may need allocation among those assets; the result can include ordinary recapture, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, and Section 1231 gain or loss. Publication 544 and the Form 4797 instructions govern that reporting. If you accelerated depreciation, see AE's cost-segregation sale guide before treating every dollar of gain as a capital-gain-rate item.

How to classify the hard lines on a settlement statement

  1. Read the contract and final closing statement together. Match the stated sale price, buyer credits, debt assumption or payoff, and each seller charge. A credit or concession needs its actual contractual purpose tested rather than automatic “selling expense” treatment.
  2. Identify direct disposition costs. Separate the broker's commission and seller-paid conveyance work from property operations, financing, and post-closing obligations. Keep invoices when a title or legal bill includes more than one service.
  3. Reconstruct basis from the beginning. Use the purchase settlement, improvements ledger, casualty or partial-disposition history, and depreciation schedules. A missing improvement can understate basis; missed depreciation cannot simply be ignored because it was never claimed.
  4. Reconcile prorations and tenant items separately. Property taxes, prepaid rent, refundable deposits, utilities, and repairs may affect rental income, deductions, or liabilities under their own rules. Do not force them into the sale-expense total to make the bank deposit match.
  5. Run the return and recapture calculation. Confirm the ownership and asset-level reporting, any prior Form 3115 adjustments, passive-loss carryforwards, and state basis differences. A 1031 exchange, installment sale, mixed personal use, or related-party sale changes the worksheet and should be reviewed before closing.

Documents to bring before filing or amending

Bring the signed purchase and sale contracts, both closing statements, Form 1099-S if issued, broker and legal invoices, payoff statement showing principal separately from interest and fees, tenant deposit and rent-proration ledger, property-tax bill, capital-improvement invoices, depreciation schedules and cost-segregation report, and prior Forms 4797, 4562, and 8582. If the sale was already reported, include the filed return and workpapers so the review can distinguish a simple transcription error from a basis or accounting-method issue.

Common failure points: treating the mortgage payoff as a selling cost; counting purchase closing costs twice; treating a repair paid at closing as a capital improvement without examining the work; omitting depreciation allowed or allowable; and applying one tax rate to land, building, and cost-segregated components. AE's rental-sale planning guide covers broader deferral and loss choices; this page is the line-by-line closing-cost review.

This is a federal framework for an ordinary taxable rental sale, not an individualized filing position. Debt relief, mixed-use history, ownership through an entity, sale of multiple assets, installment terms, and state rules can change the result. Resolve the line-item classification while the closing documents and service invoices are still available.
