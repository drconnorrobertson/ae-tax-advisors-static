Buying Out a Practice Partner: Tax Review Before Agreement

Buying out a dental or medical practice partner? Compare direct purchase, redemption and a final-owner LLC buyout, including basis and final-return records.

By AE Tax Advisors Team · Updated November 6, 2026

Start with the decision

A practice partner buyout needs a tax review of who is buying, what is being acquired and how the payment will be funded. A partner selling an interest directly to another owner is different from the practice redeeming that interest. A business taxed as a partnership also differs from a corporation, even when both owners call themselves partners.

For established dental and medical practices, the buyout often interacts with equipment debt, a jointly owned building, retirement plans and the departing owner’s remaining work. Resolve those connections while the agreement is being negotiated.

Identify the payer and tax classification

Collect the governing agreement, ownership ledger and recent returns. Determine whether the buyer is the continuing owner, the operating entity or another acquisition entity. Then identify whether the transaction is an interest purchase, redemption or asset acquisition.

For a partnership, a direct interest sale and payments liquidating a retiring partner’s interest follow different rules. Receivables, goodwill, liabilities and the terms of the agreement can affect character and basis. Corporate redemptions need a separate analysis; partnership rules do not apply simply because the owners use the word “partner.”

Separate the purchase price from other payments

Payment or obligationReview question
Ownership purchase priceWho pays it and what basis results?
Unpaid compensation or ongoing servicesHow is work before and after departure documented?
Seller note and interestWhat are payment dates, guarantees and tax timing?
Building interest or leaseDoes the property remain jointly owned or change hands?
Existing loansWhich liabilities and guarantees change after the buyout?

A total price without this breakdown can hide a funding gap or inconsistent reporting. Ask each party’s advisors to reconcile the intended treatment before the closing schedules are finalized.

Do not assume the buyer gets a new equipment deduction

Count the owners who remain for federal tax purposes before treating every buyout as a Section 754 question. These paths require different work:

  • Direct purchase with a continuing partnership: when at least two tax owners remain, review the buyer’s outside basis, the partnership’s asset basis and any Section 743(b) adjustment, including an effective Section 754 election or a mandatory-adjustment rule. The Section 743(b) transfer guide covers that continuing-partnership analysis.
  • Redemption paid by the partnership: review the retiring-owner payment and distribution rules separately. A continuing partnership’s possible Section 734(b) adjustment is not the buyer-specific Section 743(b) adjustment. Use the cash-redemption and Section 734(b) guide; if the redemption also leaves only one tax owner, add a termination review rather than assuming a continuing partnership.
  • Direct purchase leaving one owner: an existing member buying the other member’s entire interest in a two-member, partnership-taxed LLC needs the final-owner analysis below. A corporation or an LLC taxed as a corporation does not follow this partnership-to-disregarded-entity path.

Retain prior depreciation and basis schedules. The buyer needs a reliable opening position, while the seller needs records supporting the disposition. The same balance-sheet capital number should not be assumed to answer every tax-basis question.

What if buying out your partner leaves you as the only LLC owner?

For a domestic LLC taxed as a partnership, an existing member’s direct purchase of the other member’s entire interest ends the partnership for federal income tax purposes. Unless corporate tax treatment applies, the remaining single-member LLC is disregarded for income tax purposes. The state-law LLC can continue operating; ending the federal tax partnership does not itself dissolve the legal entity.

Revenue Ruling 99-6, Situation 1 (printed pages 6–7), gives the seller and continuing owner different tax views of that same transaction. The seller reports a sale of a partnership interest. For the continuing owner, the partnership is deemed to distribute its assets in liquidation to both members, followed by the continuing owner’s purchase of the assets deemed distributed to the seller.

Keep the old ownership portion and the purchased portion separate

  • Previously owned portion: determine gain or loss, if any, under Section 731 and the distributed assets’ basis under Section 732(b), using the continuing owner’s adjusted outside basis and applicable money, liability and allocation rules. This is distribution basis, not an automatic fair-market-value reset. The partnership’s holding period generally carries over for this portion.
  • Purchased portion: determine cost basis under Section 1012 and allocate that cost to the acquired assets under the applicable allocation rules. This portion has a new holding period. Separate land, equipment, receivables, inventory, goodwill and other intangibles before deciding how basis is recovered.

For a simplified arithmetic illustration, assume the old portion’s properly computed distribution basis is $40,000 and the purchased portion’s allocated cost is $100,000, with no debt, cash distribution, transaction costs or special-asset complications. Total opening asset basis is $140,000 ($40,000 + $100,000), not $200,000 merely because the other half cost $100,000. This is a hypothetical basis reconciliation, not a valuation, deduction or client savings result.

The ruling’s examples assume no debt and no unrealized receivables or substantially appreciated inventory for Section 751(b). A real practice may have cash-method patient receivables, equipment recapture, inventory, loan balances and guarantees. Review Section 751 ordinary-income treatment for the seller and the liability effects for both owners; do not assume the seller’s entire gain is capital gain or that cash paid is the only relevant amount.

This final-owner purchase is not a routine Section 743(b) adjustment inside a continuing partnership. A Section 754 election is not what creates the purchased portion’s cost basis under this ruling. Nor does a new cost basis establish an immediate equipment or bonus-depreciation deduction: asset class, acquisition facts, related-party rules, placed-in-service timing and other applicable limits still matter.

Build an opening-basis and final-return checklist

  • Fix the closing facts: retain the signed agreement, payer, ownership before and after, closing date, price allocation, payment schedule and evidence of the LLC’s tax classification. Do not substitute an entity redemption for a direct purchase in the workpapers.
  • Reconcile the two basis portions: retain the continuing owner’s outside-basis rollforward through closing, debt allocations, cash distributions, historical asset tax basis and depreciation. Build an asset-by-asset schedule separating Section 732 distribution basis from Section 1012 purchased cost, holding periods and future depreciation or amortization. A capital-account balance alone is not outside basis.
  • Close the partnership tax period: assign the final Form 1065 and final Schedules K-1, allocate income and deductions through termination and reconcile the deemed distributions with the seller’s interest sale. If termination is midyear, address the short-period return and its filing or extension deadline rather than waiting automatically for the usual annual deadline. Review Section 751 and Form 8308 reporting where applicable.
  • Open the post-buyout reporting: identify where the disregarded LLC’s income and expenses go on its owner’s return. An individual operating a practice generally uses Schedule C; an entity owner has different reporting. Coordinate state returns and the transition of accounting records.
  • Keep payroll and tax IDs separate: a disregarded LLC remains separate for employment tax and certain excise taxes and uses its own name and EIN for those obligations. Review income-tax information reporting, payroll registrations and whether an EIN change is actually required; do not assume the partnership’s end means cancelling every tax ID or stopping payroll.

Bring the prior returns, K-1s, outside-basis workpapers, debt and fixed-asset schedules, and proposed agreement to the review. Ask who will prepare and reconcile the final partnership return and the first post-buyout asset schedule before the closing date is fixed. See IRS Publication 541, the Form 1065 instructions and IRS single-member LLC guidance.

Model the practice after the partner leaves

A hypothetical two-owner practice borrows to buy out one owner. The continuing owner expects the same profit with one fewer owner distribution. The forecast should also account for reduced clinical production, replacement staffing, debt service and transition costs.

Compare available cash after the buyout with owner compensation, retirement funding and operating reserves. The transaction can be affordable on an earnings statement and still strain monthly cash. This example illustrates planning questions rather than a client outcome.

Create a closing and first-year handoff

  • Agree on transaction structure and required independent legal or valuation work.
  • Reconcile ownership, basis, liabilities and historical fixed assets.
  • Assign final or continuing K-1, corporate and owner reporting.
  • Review payroll, retirement plans and property arrangements affected by the departure.
  • Schedule a post-closing review against the final agreement and loan documents.

Arrange secure delivery of agreements and returns. A first conversation can begin with the entity type, ownership percentages, proposed payer, approximate price and target date.

Questions owners ask

Is a practice buyout payment always deductible?

No. Treatment depends on the transaction and payment component. A purchase of ownership, redemption payment, service payment and interest can have different consequences.

Does buying my partner’s share create a new basis in all practice assets?

Not automatically. The entity’s tax classification and transaction structure determine whether an asset-basis adjustment is available and how it is recorded.

What happens if I buy my partner’s entire share and become the only LLC owner?

For a direct purchase by the remaining member of a two-member, partnership-taxed LLC, Revenue Ruling 99-6 Situation 1 treats the seller as selling an interest and the buyer as receiving an asset distribution for the old portion and purchasing the seller’s asset portion. Absent corporate tax treatment, the partnership ends and the LLC becomes disregarded for income tax. The old portion has distribution basis; the purchased portion has cost basis. The whole practice does not automatically reset to fair market value.

Do I need a Section 754 election when I buy out the final other member?

A Section 754 election is not what creates the purchased asset portion’s cost basis in the direct final-owner purchase described in Revenue Ruling 99-6 Situation 1. Distinguish that termination from a direct interest purchase with a continuing partnership, where Section 743(b) may apply, and a partnership redemption, where distribution rules and possibly Section 734(b) need review.

Do we still file a partnership return if the LLC keeps operating with one owner?

Yes, the partnership-taxed period needs a final Form 1065 and final Schedules K-1. A midyear termination generally creates a short tax year with its own deadline. Post-termination activity of a disregarded LLC is reported on its owner’s return, while employment tax, certain excise taxes, state filings and EIN requirements need separate attention.
