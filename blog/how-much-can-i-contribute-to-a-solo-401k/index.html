How Much Can I Contribute to a Solo 401(k) in 2026?

Published on August 11, 2026 | Retirement Plans | AE Tax Advisors

A solo 401(k) contribution is limited by compensation, plan terms, other contributions and employee eligibility. The dollar ceiling is not an automatic deduction. Use the 2026 limits below and have the administrator reconcile both employee and employer amounts before funding.

2026 limits and catch-up contributions

For 2026, employee elective deferrals are limited to $24,500. A plan that permits catch-up contributions can allow another $8,000 for an eligible participant age 50 or older, or $11,250 instead for someone attaining age 60, 61, 62 or 63 during 2026. The annual-additions ceiling is the lesser of $72,000 or 100% of applicable compensation, excluding eligible catch-up contributions. Employee deferrals, employer contributions and voluntary after-tax employee contributions share that ceiling. The corresponding dollar ceilings including catch-up are $80,000 or $83,250, not amounts everyone can contribute.

Who qualifies for an owner-only plan?

An owner-only plan is for an owner, or owner and spouse, without other eligible employees. Do not equate this with having no full-time staff: review age, service, eligible long-term part-time employees, and employees of controlled-group or affiliated-service-group businesses. A spouse needs actual services and compensation. A plan administrator must determine coverage before an owner-only design is used.

Sole-proprietor calculation: use the worksheet

For a plan using a 25% employer contribution rate, the corresponding self-employed rate is generally 20% of net profit reduced by the deductible part of self-employment tax, before the self-employed employer contribution. Do not simply subtract the employee elective deferral from that amount and multiply by 20%. The Publication 560 worksheet applies additional earned-income, deferral and annual-additions limits.

Illustration: a sole proprietor under age 50 with $200,000 Schedule C profit, no other wages or plan contributions, and the regular Schedule SE method has $184,700 net self-employment earnings. At the 2026 $184,500 Social Security cap, regular SE tax is $28,234.30: $22,878 Social Security plus $5,356.30 Medicare. Its deductible half is $14,117.15. The 20% employer calculation is $37,176.57, and adding a $24,500 elective deferral gives $61,676.57 before return-level rounding and final worksheet checks. No Additional Medicare Tax arises on these facts for a single filer with no other covered wages. This is a contribution illustration, not a refund estimate.

S-corp salary and other employer plans

For an S-corp owner, an employer contribution is generally limited to 25% of eligible W-2 compensation, not distributions. At $150,000 W-2 compensation, $37,500 employer plus $24,500 employee equals $62,000, assuming plan permission, funding and no conflicting contributions. At $200,000 W-2 compensation, a full $24,500 deferral leaves $47,500 of the $72,000 annual-additions ceiling for employer and voluntary after-tax contributions.

The employee deferral limit generally applies across your 401(k) and 403(b) plans personally; opening a second business plan does not reset it. Related employer plans can require aggregation. A spouse has a separate personal limit only with actual eligible compensation and participation; household contributions are not automatically doubled.

Roth treatment and 2026 catch-up payroll

For 2026, an affected participant whose 2025 FICA wages from the sponsoring employer exceeded $150,000 generally must make catch-up contributions as Roth contributions. This is a prior-year employer-wage test, not a test of household income or S-corp distributions. Have payroll and the administrator confirm applicability and plan support. Roth contributions do not produce a current income-tax deduction; qualified Roth distributions can be tax-free.

Adoption, election and deposit dates are different

Separate plan adoption, deferral election, payroll deposit and employer funding deadlines. Employer contributions can generally be funded by the employer return due date including extensions. Employee salary deferrals generally require a timely election before compensation is available; payroll deposits have their own rules. A special first-year rule allows a sole proprietor with no employees to establish a new 401(k) after year-end and make first-year elective deferrals by the individual return deadline without extensions, if the requirements are met. It does not permit an S-corp owner to retroactively defer already-paid wages. Ask the administrator for a dated written calendar.

Records needed before funding

Provide the administrator your employee census including part-time workers, related-company ownership, W-2 or Schedule C information, other-job deferrals, plan adoption documents and prior contributions. Ask for a calculation by contribution type and tax year, the required filing calendar and the person responsible for implementing each step. Tax planning, plan administration and tax return preparation need an explicit division of work.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the 2026 solo 401(k) maximum?

The annual-additions ceiling is the lesser of $72,000 or applicable compensation, excluding eligible catch-up. With catch-up, the dollar ceiling can be $80,000 or $83,250 for ages 60 through 63, subject to plan and compensation rules.

Do I subtract elective deferrals before the 20% self-employed employer calculation?

Do not use that shortcut. Start with net profit less the deductible part of self-employment tax and apply the Publication 560 worksheet, including its earned-income and overall limits.

Can I contribute the full employee limit to two 401(k) plans?

No. The personal elective-deferral limit generally aggregates deferrals to your 401(k) and 403(b) plans. Related employers may also require aggregation of other plan limits.

Can a solo 401(k) be established after year-end?

Some employer contributions can follow timely post-year-end adoption. A special first-year sole-proprietor rule can allow deferrals by the unextended individual return deadline if its conditions are met. It does not allow retroactive deferral of an S-corp owner’s already-paid wages.
