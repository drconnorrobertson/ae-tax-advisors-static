The buyer assumed my business debt in an asset sale. What goes on Form 8594 and my tax return?

Published September 25, 2026 | Exit Planning | AE Tax Advisors

Generally, debt the buyer actually assumes is part of the seller's amount realized, even though that amount never arrives as cash. In an applicable business-asset acquisition, seller consideration for Form 8594 is the amount realized, not the wire to the owner. The IRS sale-of-assets guidance expressly includes liabilities assumed by the buyer. First reconcile what the contract means by price; then determine which obligations were legally and economically assumed, allocate total consideration among the assets sold, and trace each asset's gain or loss to the entity and owner returns. The buyer's assumption is not a separate check to the shareholder and should not be counted twice.

If you are signing an asset purchase agreement or assembling the sale-year return, this is a pre-filing decision. Start with the contract, not the cash wire

A headline “$4.6 million purchase price” can mean different things. It may be a gross price with $600,000 of assumed debt and $4 million wired, or the contract may state $4.6 million cash plus assumed debt. In the first case, adding $600,000 again would overstate consideration; in the second, ignoring it would understate consideration. Use the defined purchase-price provision, liability schedule, funds-flow statement, payoff letters, and actual creditor release or buyer undertaking to decide which description is true. Distinguish debt the buyer assumed from seller debt merely paid with the seller's cash proceeds at closing: both can affect the economics, but they need different lines in the reconciliation.

Also separate retained obligations from transferred ones. The buyer may decline pre-closing income taxes, litigation, owner loans, or particular payables. A promise to reimburse the seller only if a future claim occurs is not the same as an identified fixed liability assumed at closing. The Form 8594 instructions distinguish purchaser cost from seller amount realized and provide supplemental reporting when consideration later changes; the timing and value of contingent or disputed obligations require transaction-specific analysis.

A $4.6 million example: the cash check is not the sale price

Assume a calendar-year S corporation sells its operating assets for a stated gross $4.6 million. The buyer wires $4 million to the corporation and takes over an identified $600,000 bank loan that the seller would otherwise have had to satisfy. Assume no other debt, selling costs, escrow, or contingent adjustment. The starting seller consideration is $4.6 million: $4 million cash plus $600,000 liability relief. Do not report just the $4 million wire; do not add the debt a second time if the $4.6 million contract price already includes it. The actual cash distributed to shareholders, corporate asset basis, selling costs, and shareholder stock basis are different amounts.

Suppose the defensible allocation assigns $1 million to inventory and receivables, $1.4 million to equipment and other tangible assets, and the remaining $2.2 million to acquired intangibles and goodwill, subject to the IRS residual-method and fair-market-value rules. Those numbers are an illustration of a reconciliation, not a tax-rate promise or a direction to place the assumed loan into goodwill. The seller calculates gain or loss separately for the assets, including ordinary-income and depreciation-recapture consequences where applicable. The S corporation reports the asset dispositions on its return, and the resulting items affect shareholder K-1s and stock basis; the owner does not simply put $4.6 million of capital gain on a personal return. How to reconcile Form 8594 and the sale return

  1. Fix the transaction form. Confirm whether the contract is an actual asset purchase, a stock sale, or a deemed asset sale under an election. Do not attach Form 8594 automatically to every share transfer; read the form's applicability rules.
  2. Build a gross-to-net schedule. Start with cash, notes and other property received, then separately list each seller obligation actually assumed or attached to transferred property. Reconcile the result to the contract's gross or net price definition, the settlement statement, lender confirmations and general ledger.
  3. Classify hard cases separately. Analyze accounts payable, deferred revenue, accrued compensation, taxes, earnouts and indemnities by their legal terms and the seller's accounting method. A balance-sheet label does not prove that the buyer assumed a fixed tax liability or when consideration is taken into account.
  4. Allocate the consideration. Reconcile buyer and seller asset schedules and use the residual method and asset classes in the Form 8594 instructions. Inventory, equipment, customer-based intangibles, and goodwill do not necessarily produce the same seller tax character.
  5. Carry the result through the filings. Match the allocation to the entity's disposition forms, ordinary income and recapture workpapers, Form 8594, final or sale-year Form 1120-S or 1065, K-1s, and owner-basis schedules. If consideration changes after closing, review the supplemental Form 8594 and affected return year rather than silently changing the original schedule.

Documents and errors to catch before filing

Gather the signed asset purchase agreement and amendments, exhibits identifying assumed and excluded liabilities, lender payoff and release documents, funds-flow and closing statement, seller note and escrow agreements, working-capital true-up, invoices and aging for transferred payables, tax-basis balance sheet, depreciation schedules, inventory valuation, purchase-price allocation, Form 8594 drafts, and the entity and owner return workpapers. A reviewer should be able to trace each assumed dollar to both the legal obligation and the asset-sale schedule.

  • Counting debt twice. Adding assumed debt to a contract price that is already stated gross creates an inflated Form 8594 total.
  • Using the shareholder's net check as proceeds. Debt, escrow, selling costs and corporate distributions may all explain why cash received differs from amount realized.
  • Forcing every liability into one rule. Deferred revenue, unpaid expenses, contingent claims and taxes can have different timing and deduction consequences; the contract and accounting method matter.
  • Allocating all extra consideration to goodwill. The IRS residual method allocates in order and limits non-goodwill assets by fair market value.
  • Filing inconsistent buyer and seller schedules. Resolve the allocation and later true-ups while both sides still have the underlying documents. AE's working-capital true-up guide covers one common post-closing adjustment.

This is a federal framework for an operating-business asset sale, not an opinion on a particular purchase agreement. Entity type, accounting method, assumed-liability terms, nonrecourse debt, state taxes and buyer/seller reporting can change the answer. If the return was already filed, quantify the difference before choosing an amended entity return, partnership AAR or owner correction path.
