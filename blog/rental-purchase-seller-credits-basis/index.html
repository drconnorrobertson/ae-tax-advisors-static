Do Seller Credits on My Rental Purchase Reduce Depreciable Basis?

Published September 22, 2026 | Real Estate Tax Planning | AE Tax Advisors

Sometimes, but not automatically. A genuine reduction of the purchase price generally lowers the buyer's acquisition basis before land is separated from depreciable property. A seller payment toward the buyer's loan costs, a property-tax proration, and money held for later repairs are different transactions. A settlement statement may label all four “seller credit,” so the signed agreement and the actual disbursements—not the column heading—control the return workpaper.

If the first rental return or cost-segregation study is being prepared from a closing statement with credits, AE can reconcile the contract, lender charges, tax prorations, and asset basis before the wrong amount starts depreciating.

Start with the settlement statement, then read the contract

A closing disclosure may put several economically different items in one column called “seller credit.” One might be a negotiated reduction in the price of a property with known defects. Another might reimburse buyer loan charges. A third might settle property taxes attributable to the seller's ownership period. They should not automatically receive the same tax treatment. Match each line to the purchase agreement, invoices, tax bill, and lender documents before posting a journal entry.

The buyer's starting basis generally includes the amount paid for the property plus eligible acquisition costs. A genuine price concession generally lowers what the buyer paid. By contrast, a cost that is separately deductible or a cost of borrowing does not become building basis merely because it appeared at closing. The land/building allocation must then be applied to the resulting acquisition basis; land is not depreciated. IRS Publication 551 identifies which settlement charges can enter basis and which loan, insurance, and prepaid items must be kept out of property basis.

Distinguish three common credit patterns

Price reduction for condition. If the seller agrees to a lower effective purchase price because the roof needs work, the credit may reduce the property's cost basis. The buyer must still classify the subsequent roof work on its own facts; a substantial replacement is not converted into a current repair simply because the seller funded it indirectly.

Buyer closing costs. If a credit pays title or transfer charges that otherwise enter acquisition basis, reconcile the gross charge, who was obligated to pay it, and the offsetting credit rather than adding both as separate investments. If it pays loan origination charges, those costs are generally analyzed as financing costs over the loan's life, not as building basis. If it pays a prepaid insurance premium, the coverage period matters. Do not automatically import the special seller-paid-points rule for an owner-occupied main home into a rental purchase.

Seller-period obligations. A property tax proration is tied to periods of ownership. The buyer and seller generally allocate real property taxes under the applicable federal rule regardless of which party writes the check. A rent or security-deposit transfer also needs its own treatment; a refundable tenant deposit is a liability, not purchase-price income to be blended into basis.

Worked example: same closing sheet, different basis result

Scenario A—actual price reduction: A duplex contract starts at $500,000. An inspection addendum explicitly cuts the price by $12,000 for a roof defect, making the agreed property price $488,000. The buyer separately pays $4,000 of qualifying title and recording charges. Before allocating to land and improvements, the acquisition-basis workpaper starts at $492,000. If the buyer later replaces the roof, the new work is classified separately; the $12,000 concession does not make that replacement an immediate repair deduction.

Scenario B—seller funds a buyer loan charge: Instead of changing the $500,000 price, the agreement says the seller will contribute up to $3,000 to the buyer's lender fees and the final settlement statement shows $3,000 actually applied. Do not simply repeat Scenario A's $488,000 figure or add $3,000 to the building. Keep the $500,000 contract price, the seller contribution, and the lender fee as separate lines for review. The tax treatment of the payment and borrowing cost depends on the agreement and fee; the loan charge is not property-acquisition basis merely because it appears at closing. If the credit cap was $3,000 but only $2,100 was used, the unused $900 does not become an actual payment by assumption.

In either scenario, the buyer must support the land allocation before depreciating the improvement portion. A later cost-segregation review of an acquired property must start from this buyer-side basis, not the seller's old depreciation schedule. These figures are illustrations, not a concluded tax position for a particular settlement.

A five-line reconciliation before the first return

  1. Contract price: record the final signed price and each amendment, not the first offer or a preliminary closing estimate.
  2. Credit purpose: trace every seller credit to a price change, named buyer cost, tax proration, refundable tenant liability, or conditional repair escrow.
  3. Actual disbursement: compare the permitted maximum credit with what escrow actually paid. Do not assume an unused allowance changed the economics.
  4. Separate schedules: assign qualifying acquisition costs to property basis, borrowing costs to a loan schedule, owner-period taxes and operating items to their proper year, and later improvements to their own asset ledger.
  5. Land and return tie-out: allocate supported property basis between nondepreciable land and depreciable assets, then compare that schedule with Form 4562, Schedule E, and any cost-segregation deliverable.

Records and a clean basis schedule

Keep the executed purchase agreement and addenda, final settlement statement, invoices paid through escrow, lender fee detail, tax prorations, and an allocation support file such as an appraisal. Record the full transaction once, then reconcile cash paid, debt assumed, and every credit. Maintain separate schedules for land, building, later improvements, and loan costs. This makes future depreciation and sale calculations auditable.

For the broader distinction between purchase basis and operating deductions, see the rental property tax questions hub and rental closing-cost guide. The IRS's Publication 551 explains basis and settlement costs; Publication 527 covers rental expenses and depreciation.

Questions to resolve before filing

Was the concession fixed in the signed purchase agreement, negotiated after an inspection, or merely a lender-approved cap on buyer costs? Did the closing agent actually use the whole credit? A $10,000 permitted credit with only $7,000 of eligible charges is not the same transaction as a $10,000 price reduction. Review the final, not preliminary, settlement statement and trace unused amounts. If the seller paid a buyer obligation directly outside escrow, retain proof of payment and describe why that payment was made.

Also compare the appraisal's land/building percentages to local assessment data and the contract's allocation. An unsupported allocation can inflate depreciation even when the total property basis is right. If a seller funds a post-closing escrow for repairs, document who owns the escrow, the release conditions, and when funds are actually paid. That arrangement may differ from a concession delivered at closing, and later invoices still need independent classification. Publication 551 also distinguishes seller-period real estate taxes that a buyer assumes without reimbursement from taxes for the buyer's own ownership period; do not treat both as the same Schedule E expense.

Failure points to catch before filing

  • Using the lender's credit label as the tax conclusion. The contract, obligation, and actual settlement flow determine whether price or a separate cost changed.
  • Double-counting a seller-funded fee. A fee paid from a credit is not both an unreduced buyer cash outlay and a new property-basis addition.
  • Deducting repair escrow at closing. Retained or conditional funds are not themselves proof of a paid, currently deductible repair; classify the later work and funding on its facts.
  • Applying the credit only to building value. A property-level price change must be reconciled before the supported land/building and asset-level allocations.
