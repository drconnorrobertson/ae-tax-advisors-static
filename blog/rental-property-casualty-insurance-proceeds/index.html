My rental burned and insurance paid more than its basis. Do I owe tax if I rebuild?

Published September 22, 2026 | Real Estate Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Possibly, but rebuilding can postpone qualifying casualty gain. First compare the physical-damage insurance proceeds with the adjusted basis of the damaged rental asset, not the property's market value or the amount of the repair invoice. If proceeds exceed basis, there may be gain; under the involuntary-conversion rules, timely qualifying restoration or replacement may postpone some or all of it. A plan to rebuild by itself is not an automatic tax exemption.

If your insurer has settled a rental fire claim and you are choosing whether to rebuild, bring the settlement breakdown, pre-fire depreciation schedule, and contractor estimates. AE can review the gain, replacement deadline, and return position together.

Separate the claim before you calculate gain

Identify what the insurer actually paid for: the damaged building, a separately depreciated improvement or personal-property asset, debris removal, or lost rent. Do not apply the entire property's land-and-building basis against a payment for one damaged asset. Reconstruct adjusted basis using acquisition allocation, capital improvements, and depreciation allowed or allowable. For a wholly destroyed rental asset, the IRS generally measures loss using adjusted basis less salvage and expected reimbursement, without a fair-market-value decline test; partial damage has a different loss computation. IRS Publication 547 explains both cases, and Form 4684 instructions require separate reporting for business or income-producing property.

Insurance money earmarked for replacement of lost rent is not necessarily part of the physical-property conversion. Obtain the insurer's line-item settlement and have the rental-income and property-damage components characterized separately. A lender's receipt of part of the proceeds does not by itself remove that payment from the amount realized; Publication 547 includes payments used to satisfy a mortgage or lien in the reimbursement amount.

When rebuilding can postpone the gain

Publication 547's postponement-of-gain rules allow a taxpayer with gain on damaged property to postpone reporting it when the reimbursement is spent to restore the property, or when qualifying similar-or-related-in-service-or-use replacement property is acquired within the applicable replacement period. To postpone all the gain, qualifying replacement cost generally must at least equal the reimbursement; if less is spent, gain is recognized up to the unspent reimbursement, limited by total realized gain. The replacement property's tax basis is reduced by postponed gain. This is a deferral with a basis consequence, not a permanent exclusion.

The replacement period generally begins on the casualty date and ends two years after the close of the first tax year in which gain is realized. Special disaster rules and extensions can change that deadline; confirm the applicable event and year before relying on a calendar date. Publication 547 also describes the statements to attach when choosing postponement, subsequent reporting when replacement occurs, and an amended return if the promised replacement is not completed or costs less than expected.

Worked example: full rebuild versus a shortfall

Assume a fire completely destroys a rental building (land survives). The building's adjusted basis, after depreciation, is $180,000; there is no salvage. The insurer pays $240,000 solely for that building. The potential casualty gain is $60,000 ($240,000 less $180,000). If the owner constructs a qualifying replacement rental building for $300,000 within the applicable period and properly chooses postponement, all $60,000 may be postponed; the new building's starting basis would generally be $240,000 ($300,000 less $60,000 postponed gain). This simplified example assumes no other affected asset, claim component, or limitation.

If qualifying replacement cost is only $220,000, the $20,000 of reimbursement not reinvested can make $20,000 of the $60,000 gain currently reportable. The remaining $40,000 may be postponed, leaving a $180,000 basis in the replacement building ($220,000 less $40,000). The filed return and depreciation schedule must reconcile to the final invoices, not an early contractor bid.

A filing sequence that prevents a second correction

  1. Build the pre-casualty asset ledger. Separate land, building, prior improvements, and shorter-life assets; reconcile depreciation allowed or allowable to filed returns.
  2. Reconcile claim components. Match the policy, adjuster statement, deductible, salvage, lien payment, lost-rent coverage, and actual receipts. Document any remaining reasonable prospect of reimbursement.
  3. Calculate the casualty result. Test each affected asset, determine whether it was partially damaged or wholly destroyed, and prepare the applicable Form 4684 Section B workpapers. Do not double-count proceeds as ordinary income and casualty gain.
  4. Test the replacement choice. Confirm similar-use eligibility, owner and entity identity, the replacement period, qualified spending, and whether the return needs a postponement statement. Track the outcome if the settlement and construction cross tax years.
  5. Update basis and depreciation. Reduce basis for the casualty consequences and account for restoration or replacement costs and any postponed gain. Coordinate asset-disposition and renovation records before assigning new lives.

Documents to bring to a return review

  • Insurance policy, adjuster allocation, settlement letters, checks, mortgage-payoff detail, deductible, and business-interruption or lost-rent schedules.
  • Closing statement, land/building allocation, fixed-asset ledger, prior Form 4562 schedules, and records of later improvements.
  • Fire report, photographs, demolition and salvage records, appraisals or estimates where relevant, and dates the damaged asset stopped being used.
  • Rebuild contracts, invoices, permits, payment records, placed-in-service date, and any replacement-property purchase agreement.
  • Filed federal and state returns for the casualty and gain years, Form 4684 drafts, and any previously attached postponement statement.

Failure points to catch before filing

  • Using fair market value as tax basis. A property may be worth far more than its depreciation-adjusted basis.
  • Netting every claim line against one asset. Physical damage, lost rent, and separate improvements need their own classification.
  • Assuming all repair costs are immediately deductible. Casualty basis adjustments and restoration rules may require capitalization; a separate routine repair should be analyzed on its own facts.
  • Assuming an intent to rebuild completes Section 1033. The qualifying replacement, spending amount, deadline, statements, and final basis must be tracked.
  • Ignoring the other return years. A later settlement or lower-than-planned replacement cost may change the gain-year filing and subsequent depreciation.

This is a federal framework, not a filing position for every casualty. Entity ownership, mixed personal use, prior cost segregation, disaster designations, depreciation recapture, and state rules can change the result. Have the claim and return reconciled before filing or amending.

The tax treatment

Insurance recovery and the property's adjusted basis must be compared; a casualty loss, gain, repair deduction, or basis adjustment can result depending on facts. This is a federal income tax starting point; the contract, ownership, accounting method, and actual use can change the result. State and local treatment should be checked separately.

The decision to make before filing

Model the damaged component, proceeds, deductible, and restoration costs before booking the settlement. The useful planning step is to resolve the classification while the underlying documents are still available, then reconcile it to the books and the prior-year return. If the transaction spans more than one year, track the opening balance and what happened to it afterward.

Illustrative example

A pipe bursts, and an insurer pays for floor restoration after applying a deductible. The owner records the damaged asset's basis, insurance proceeds, and restoration invoices rather than claiming the contractor's full bill in isolation. New upgraded flooring may also have a different treatment from repair work.

Records that support the position

Keep the insurer's adjustment, repair estimates, checks, photographs, and pre-loss basis records. Tie amounts on the return to bank activity and the agreement. When several assets, people, or uses are involved, write down the allocation method and apply it consistently. A short dated workpaper is easier to defend than a reconstructed explanation years later.

A common reporting error

Deducting the full repair bill while ignoring the insurance recovery can double-count a loss. Review both sides of the entry: a payment can affect income, basis, liability, or an expense at different times. A correct cash total alone does not establish the correct tax character.

Where to verify the rule

Start with IRS Publication 527: Residential Rental Property, Publication 547: Casualties, Disasters, and Thefts, and the Form 4684 instructions. Their examples and cross-references explain the underlying federal rule; check the current version and any later IRS guidance for the year at issue.
