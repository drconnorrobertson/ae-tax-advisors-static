I guaranteed my S corporation's bank loan. Can I deduct this year's loss?

Published September 22, 2026 | Updated September 24, 2026 | Business Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Short answer: Signing a personal guarantee on a bank loan made to your S corporation does not by itself give you shareholder debt basis to deduct a K-1 loss. The bank is the lender to the corporation, even if you are personally at risk. Under the IRS Form 7203 instructions, a guarantor may obtain debt basis to the extent they actually pay under the guarantee. A separately documented, bona fide loan owed directly by the corporation to you must be tested on its own facts. Reconcile these paths before claiming the loss on your individual return.

For an owner financing a substantial operating business, the distinction can change the current deduction, suspended losses, and future shareholder-loan repayment treatment. A K-1 showing a loss is not proof that all of it is deductible this year.

Three financing facts that produce different basis answers

  • The corporation borrows; you guarantee. The corporation owes the bank. Your signature, pledged collateral, or contingent obligation alone does not create debt basis in a loan owed to you.
  • You pay under the guarantee. A documented payment on bona fide corporate debt may increase your debt basis to the extent paid. Check the payment date, whose funds were used, the amount actually applied, and the resulting rights between you and the corporation. A year-end promise to pay is not the payment.
  • You lend to the corporation directly. A separate loan that runs from the corporation to you may create debt basis if it is bona fide under the facts and federal tax principles. A bank loan to you followed by a real advance to the corporation is not the same as signing its bank note; the cash trail, obligation, books, and repayment terms matter. See owner-to-business loan documentation.

The final Section 1366 debt-basis regulations distinguish bona fide indebtedness running directly to the shareholder from a mere guarantee and permit basis from an actual payment under a qualifying guarantee. They do not turn every journal entry labeled “shareholder loan” into a real debt.

Worked return example: the guarantee versus an actual payment

Assume a sole owner guaranteed a $900,000 corporate bank loan. The company reports a $320,000 K-1 loss, and the owner's correctly computed stock basis before applying the loss is $40,000. There is no separate shareholder loan and no payment under the guarantee by year-end. The guarantee contributes $0 debt basis; only $40,000 of the loss passes the basis limitation, and $280,000 is suspended for lack of basis, before testing other limits. The $900,000 bank principal is not the owner's deductible basis.

Now assume instead that the owner paid $100,000 of the qualifying corporate debt under the guarantee before year-end and can substantiate the payment and resulting debt relationship. On these simplified facts, the owner may have $100,000 of additional debt basis. The basis-limited loss could rise to $140,000, leaving $180,000 suspended before at-risk, passive, and excess-business-loss rules. That is a change in potentially allowable loss, not a $100,000 tax saving. If payment occurred in a later tax year, do not backdate the basis to the earlier return; test when suspended losses can be released under the later year's rules.

Return-signing sequence for the owner and preparer

  1. Rebuild stock basis. Start with the prior Form 7203 and stock records, then apply income, distributions, nondeductible expenses, and losses in the required order. Debt basis does not make a distribution tax-free; that calculation uses stock basis.
  2. Map every creditor. For each note, identify the legal borrower, lender, guarantee, bank disbursement, shareholder advance, corporate ledger entry, and repayment. Do not infer a shareholder loan from an S corporation balance-sheet liability to the bank.
  3. Document payment and timing. If the bank called the guarantee, match the personal bank transfer to the lender statement and determine whether the corporation then became directly indebted to the owner. If the owner advanced funds separately, support that loan's bona fide terms.
  4. Prepare the owner-level limitations. Complete Form 7203 for stock and debt basis, then evaluate at-risk, passive-activity, and excess-business-loss limits in that order. The IRS stock-and-debt-basis guide explains why a K-1 loss alone does not establish deductibility.
  5. Carry forward what is not allowed. Reconcile suspended basis-limited losses to the prior return and future basis increases. If a reduced-basis shareholder loan is later repaid, review the potential taxable repayment rather than assuming all principal is tax-free; see the repayment decision guide.

Records and failure points before claiming the loss

Bring the current and prior Forms 1120-S and K-1, prior Forms 7203 or basis worksheets, bank note and guarantee, lender statements, personal and corporate bank transfers, general-ledger liability accounts, shareholder notes, repayment history, ownership changes, and suspended-loss schedules. For a large year-end transaction, keep dated copies that establish whether the payment or advance occurred in the year claimed.

  • Counting the bank's loan as your loan. Even a fully secured personal guarantee does not convert a corporate bank note into debt owed directly to the shareholder.
  • Using a spreadsheet entry without a real transaction. A reclassification may not prove bona fide debt, payment, or a change in creditor. Review legal documents and cash flows.
  • Skipping stock-basis ordering. Distributions and nondeductible items can consume basis before K-1 losses, and a shareholder's records—not just the K-1—establish the calculation.
  • Stopping at Form 7203. Basis is the first limit, not the last; at-risk, passive, and excess-business-loss rules can still restrict the owner.
  • Ignoring future repayment. Losses that reduced debt basis can make later principal repayments taxable. Preserve note-by-note basis records.

This is a federal filing framework, not a conclusion about any owner's guarantee. The lender agreement, actual payment, corporate obligation, ownership, other basis adjustments, and return year control the answer; state treatment may differ.
