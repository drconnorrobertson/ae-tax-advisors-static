Sold a Rental Without Claiming Depreciation: What to Fix Before Filing

Published September 22, 2026 | Real Estate Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Yes, missed rental depreciation can still affect the gain you report after a sale. The IRS generally requires basis to be reduced by depreciation allowed or allowable, whichever is greater, even if no deduction appeared on your Schedule E. Do not use the unreduced purchase cost as sale basis or add years of missed depreciation to the sale-year return without first identifying the correct correction procedure. IRS Publication 946 states the basis rule directly.

If your closing has occurred and the sale return is being prepared—or was already filed—bring the purchase and sale files, prior returns, and depreciation schedule to a coordinated review. AE can trace the missing deductions, sale basis, gain character, and possible amendment or accounting-method route before the next filing compounds the error.

First establish when depreciation was actually allowable

Start with the placed-in-service date, not merely the closing date. Determine when the property was ready and available for rental use, identify land (which is not depreciable), and reconstruct the building, improvements, and any separately depreciable assets. A former personal residence, mixed-use property, inherited rental, or property transferred between entities may have a different starting basis or use history. Compare the allowable schedule with every filed Schedule E and Form 4562. Publication 946 covers placed-in-service timing and allowable depreciation; AE's rental-basis correction guide addresses an incorrect starting amount.

The workpaper should show, by asset and tax year, opening basis, depreciation actually deducted, depreciation allowable under the correct method, improvements and dispositions, and the closing-date adjusted basis. If a prior cost-segregation study identified shorter-lived property, do not blend its gain with the building's gain; asset classification can change the sale reporting and gain character.

Then choose the correction path for the missing deduction

  • One return or a fact error: An amended return may be the appropriate route for certain errors that have not become an accounting method, subject to applicable filing and refund limits. Do not infer the procedure from the size of the missed amount alone.
  • The same impermissible depreciation treatment on two or more consecutively filed returns: That generally establishes an accounting method. A Form 3115 method change and Section 481(a) adjustment may be required or available under the governing procedure, rather than simply amending every year. Publication 946 explains the two-return rule and Form 3115 instructions explain the application.
  • The sale is in the current, unfiled tax year: Ask whether the specific disposed-property automatic-change rules permit a Form 3115 in that year and how the catch-up adjustment interacts with the sale. AE's Form 3115 sale-year guide examines that procedure in detail; this is not an automatic fix for every sale, election, or one-year error.
  • The sale return was already filed: Recompute both the historic depreciation and the filed sale basis and gain before selecting an amendment or other permitted correction. A Form 3115 filed for a later year should not be assumed to repair a property that was disposed of in an earlier year. Check the controlling procedure and open return years with a preparer.

A sale worksheet shows why the two issues must be coordinated

Illustrative numbers, not a tax estimate: Assume $500,000 of original tax basis is properly allocated $100,000 to land and $400,000 to a residential rental building. By closing, $60,000 of building depreciation was allowable, but the filed rental returns claimed none. Assume the net amount realized after properly classified sale costs is $650,000. The building-and-land adjusted basis is $440,000, and the simplified realized gain is $210,000. Using the unreduced $500,000 basis would incorrectly show $150,000 of gain—a $60,000 difference. The owner must separately determine whether and how the missed $60,000 deduction can be recovered; it cannot simply be netted against sale proceeds without a valid filing path.

These numbers omit financing, personal-use changes, other asset classes, suspended passive losses, and state tax. On the federal return, ordinary Section 1245 recapture may apply to shorter-lived assets; qualifying gain attributable to straight-line building depreciation may instead be unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, subject to its own rules and rate ceiling. Not every dollar called “depreciation recapture” is taxed the same way. Publication 544 and the Form 4797 instructions distinguish these categories.

Documents to bring before filing or amending

  • Purchase and sale closing statements, Form 1099-S, purchase allocation, and records of selling costs.
  • Rental listing and lease history proving the placed-in-service date, plus personal-use and conversion records where relevant.
  • Capital-improvement invoices, prior cost-segregation reports, fixed-asset ledgers, and records of any partial disposition.
  • All affected Forms 1040, Schedules E, Forms 4562 and 8582, entity returns and K-1s if owned through a pass-through, and the filed or draft sale-year Form 4797.
  • A year-by-year schedule of depreciation claimed versus allowable, with a written explanation of the original error.

Failure points that change the result

  • Counting land or pre-rental personal-use years as depreciable. That overstates allowable deductions and can distort both the correction and gain.
  • Using original cost as adjusted basis because no deduction was claimed. The allowed-or-allowable rule can still reduce basis. Publication 946 is explicit.
  • Amending every year by default. A repeated impermissible treatment may be an accounting method; the proper route depends on the years, error, asset, and disposition timing.
  • Treating all gain as ordinary “recapture” or assuming a flat 25% tax. The asset-by-asset character calculation and the owner's other income matter.
  • Ignoring suspended passive losses or state filings. A full taxable disposition may affect passive-loss release, but related-party, installment, partial, or exchange transactions require separate analysis. See the passive-loss carryforward guide.

This is a federal framework, not a filing position for every landlord. Tax years, ownership, basis, use, depreciation elections, return deadlines, and state law can change the correction.
