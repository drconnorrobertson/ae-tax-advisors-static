Our warehouse inventory was damaged before year-end and insurance settled later. What goes on the business return?

Published September 22, 2026 | Updated September 24, 2026 | Business Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Direct answer: Do not deduct destroyed inventory twice or treat the insurer's later payment as unrelated cash. For a genuine casualty or theft of inventory, the IRS identifies two reporting paths: recognize the loss through properly computed cost of goods sold and report insurance recovery in gross income, or claim a separate casualty loss while removing the affected items from cost of goods sold and reducing that loss by reimbursement. The claim's reasonable prospect of recovery and the business's accounting method determine the year and amount to report. A settlement next year does not by itself put the entire loss or recovery on next year's return.

This matters most to a distributor, manufacturer, or retailer facing a material warehouse event close to filing. The owner's tax team needs one schedule connecting the physical count, tax inventory method, claim, settlement, and entity return. A book write-down, insurer reserve, and tax deduction are not interchangeable.

First decide what was lost and what was still saleable

A sudden flood, fire, or theft can be a casualty; ordinary spoilage, gradual deterioration, or obsolescence may require a different inventory-valuation analysis. Separate totally destroyed units from goods still saleable at a discount, customer-owned or consigned goods, fixed assets, and business-interruption proceeds. Document title to the goods. The tax cost of merchandise—not its expected sales price or insurance policy limit—is the starting point for the inventory-cost reconciliation. IRS Publication 334 explains how opening inventory, purchases and other inventoriable costs, and closing inventory produce cost of goods sold; small-business inventory-method exceptions can change the mechanics.

Choose one inventory-loss path, then trace insurance

  • Cost-of-goods-sold path: Properly remove destroyed goods from ending inventory so their supported cost passes through cost of goods sold. Do not also claim that cost as a separate casualty expense. Under Publication 547, insurance or other reimbursement for this inventory loss is included in gross income; determine its recognition year under the entity's facts and accounting method.
  • Separate casualty-loss path: Remove the affected goods from cost of goods sold by adjusting opening inventory or purchases as appropriate, then compute the eligible separate loss after salvage and reimbursement received or reasonably expected. Do not include the same reimbursement again in gross income under this path. The separate-loss amount cannot be claimed for a covered portion while a reasonable prospect of insurance recovery remains unresolved.

A change in inventory method, LIFO layer, or valuation convention is not made casually to obtain a better number. For an S corporation, reconcile the chosen path to Form 1125-A and Form 1120-S; partnerships and C corporations have corresponding entity-return schedules. The IRS Form 1125-A page identifies the entity filers, and the Form 1120-S instructions address inventory methods and cost of goods sold.

Work through a year-end claim without double counting

Suppose a distributor's December flood destroys units with $500,000 of supported tax cost. The remaining salvage is worth $20,000. Based on the policy and adjuster's file, the company reasonably expects $350,000 of inventory coverage, but the claim is settled and paid the next year. The economic unrecovered amount is approximately $130,000. That is a reconciliation check, not an automatic tax-return line or a representation that the entire $500,000 is immediately deductible.

  1. Reconcile the $500,000 to SKU counts, invoices, cost layers, and the adopted tax inventory method. Remove or revalue only the units actually affected; account separately for the $20,000 salvage.
  2. Choose and document the cost-of-goods-sold or separate-loss path. On the first path, trace the inventory-cost effect and the insurer's reimbursement as gross income in their correct tax years. On the second, eliminate the affected cost from COGS and test the $350,000 expected recovery before measuring a separate loss.
  3. When the final settlement differs from the expected amount, reconcile the difference under the applicable recovery-year rules. If a return was already filed using an inconsistent amount or both paths, review whether an entity amendment and corrected owner K-1s are needed rather than silently netting the difference into an unrelated later period.

What to gather before filing or amending

  • Dated inventory counts by SKU or lot, purchase and production cost support, prior-year closing inventory, photos, disposal certificates, and salvage-sale documents.
  • Insurance policy, claim submission, adjuster estimate, correspondence about disputed coverage, settlement statement, and payment dates—separating inventory from equipment, building damage, and lost profits.
  • Book entries, tax inventory method, Form 1125-A workpapers, filed or draft entity return, owner K-1s, and any state returns affected by the same event.

Failure points and the next decision

The most expensive errors are deducting the same cost through COGS and a casualty line, valuing a loss at retail price rather than supported tax cost, ignoring salvage, treating an insurer's unresolved claim as zero recovery, mixing business-interruption proceeds with inventory coverage, or filing an amendment without tracing the effect to owners. A federally declared disaster may create an optional prior-year loss election under additional requirements, but a warehouse flood alone does not establish eligibility; Publication 547 gives the conditions and inventory carryover adjustment. State conformity and accounting-method issues need separate review.

For a material claim, have the preparer reconcile both methods and the year-end recovery evidence before Form 1120-S, Form 1065, or Form 1120 is filed. If the return is already filed, identify the exact inventory, insurance, and pass-through lines that would change before deciding whether to amend.
