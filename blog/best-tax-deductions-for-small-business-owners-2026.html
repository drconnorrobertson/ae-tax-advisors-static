Best Tax Deductions for Small Business Owners in 2026

Every year, small business owners hand over thousands of dollars in taxes they did not legally owe, simply because nobody told them about a deduction that applied directly to their situation. The tax code is not a mystery designed to punish business owners -- it is full of intentional incentives for exactly the kind of risk-taking and job creation small businesses represent. Below are the deductions that make the biggest difference heading into the 2026 filing season.

Retirement Plan Contributions

Contributions to a SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k), or SIMPLE IRA reduce your taxable income dollar for dollar while building your own retirement nest egg. A Solo 401(k) allows both an employee deferral (up to $23,500 for 2026, plus catch-up if 50 or older) and an employer profit-sharing contribution of up to 25% of compensation, with a combined cap around $70,000. For owners with higher income and a desire to save more aggressively, a cash balance plan can push deductible contributions well beyond that. This is one of the most powerful and underused tools available -- see our deeper dive on tax strategies for business owners making over $1 million for how these plans scale.

The Home Office Deduction

If you use part of your home regularly and exclusively for business, you can deduct a proportional share of your mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, and depreciation, or use the simplified method of $5 per square foot up to 300 square feet. Many owners avoid this deduction out of audit fear, but a properly measured and documented home office is one of the most defensible deductions in the code.

The Augusta Rule (Section 280A(g))

Renting your personal residence to your own business for up to 14 days a year for legitimate business meetings, planning retreats, or client events generates a deduction for the business and tax-free income for you personally. The key is documentation: a written rental agreement, a fair market rate comparable to local venues, and minutes or an agenda from each meeting. Full details are in the Augusta Rule explained.

Vehicle and Mileage Deductions

Business use of a vehicle can be deducted using the standard mileage rate or actual expenses, whichever produces the larger benefit. For owners who purchase a heavier vehicle (over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight) used primarily for business, Section 179 and bonus depreciation can allow a substantial first-year write-off. Keep a contemporaneous mileage log -- the IRS disallows vehicle deductions more often for lack of documentation than for lack of legitimacy.

Health Insurance and HSA Contributions

Self-employed individuals and S-Corp owners can generally deduct 100% of health insurance premiums for themselves and their dependents. Pairing a high-deductible health plan with a Health Savings Account adds another fully deductible contribution, currently up to $4,300 for individual coverage and $8,550 for family coverage in 2026, with funds that grow tax-free and can be used tax-free for qualified medical expenses.

Section 179 and Bonus Depreciation on Equipment

Equipment, machinery, computers, and certain software purchased and placed in service during the year can often be fully expensed in the year of purchase rather than depreciated over many years. With 100% bonus depreciation now permanent, this applies broadly to both new and used qualifying property, making year-end equipment purchases a legitimate way to reduce taxable income when cash flow allows.

Cost Segregation for Business-Owned Real Estate

If your business owns the building it operates from, or you hold investment real estate, a cost segregation study reclassifies components like flooring, fixtures, parking lots, and specialty electrical into shorter depreciation categories, unlocking large first-year deductions instead of spreading them over 27.5 or 39 years. This strategy alone can create six-figure deductions for owners who have never had a study performed.

Qualified Business Income (QBI) Deduction

Most owners of pass-through entities -- sole proprietorships, partnerships, and S-Corps -- can deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income under Section 199A, subject to income thresholds and, for certain service businesses, phase-outs. Structuring your reasonable compensation and business income correctly can materially change the size of this deduction. See maximizing your QBI deduction under Section 199A for the mechanics.

Startup and Organizational Costs

New businesses can deduct up to $5,000 in startup costs and $5,000 in organizational costs in the year the business begins, with the remainder amortized over 15 years. This applies to costs like market research, legal fees for formation, and initial marketing, even if incurred before the business technically opened.

Professional Fees and Continuing Education

Fees paid to attorneys, accountants, and consultants for business purposes are fully deductible, as is the cost of continuing education, professional certifications, and industry conferences that maintain or improve skills required in your current business.

Why a List of Deductions Is Not a Tax Strategy

Deductions matter, but they are only one lever. The bigger opportunity for most owners is in entity structure, retirement plan design, and income timing -- the kind of planning covered in how proactive tax planning saves thousands. If your current advisor's involvement stops at filing a return with the deductions you happened to remember, you are managing your taxes reactively instead of strategically.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the single most overlooked deduction for small business owners?

The home office deduction and the Augusta Rule are the two most commonly skipped, usually because owners assume they are not worth the paperwork or fear an audit. Both are legitimate, well-documented strategies when applied correctly.

Can I deduct my health insurance premiums as a small business owner?

Yes. Self-employed individuals and more-than-2% S-Corp shareholders can generally deduct 100% of health insurance premiums paid for themselves and their family, either as a self-employed health insurance deduction or through the S-Corp's wages with an offsetting adjustment.

Do I need receipts for every deduction I claim?

Yes, and the standard is more than just a bank statement. The IRS wants documentation showing the business purpose, amount, date, and, for meals and travel, who was present and what was discussed. Sloppy recordkeeping is the number one reason legitimate deductions get disallowed on audit.
