Cost Segregation for Multifamily and Apartment Buildings

Published on May 14, 2026 | Real Estate Investor Tax | AE Tax Advisors

A multifamily property may contain repeated appliances, removable floor coverings, and site assets that merit a cost-segregation review. Repetition makes measurement important, but a kitchen or bathroom is not automatically short-life property, and no fixed reclassification percentage follows from the unit count alone. A quality study reconciles the acquired basis, actual assets, and classification authority; see the IRS cost-segregation audit guide for the examination framework.

The Baseline

Residential rental property is depreciated over 27.5 years under IRC Sec. 168(c), recovering about 3.64% of basis annually. A property qualifies as residential rental when 80% or more of gross rental income comes from dwelling units.

That 27.5-year life generally applies to the residential building and its structural components. Separately identifiable appliances, some floor coverings, and qualifying site improvements can have different lives. Cabinetry, lighting, and installed systems require fact-specific analysis rather than an automatic shorter-life label.

What Reclassifies in an Apartment Building

Potential per-unit personal property. Appliances, removable carpet, blinds, and other property may qualify for shorter recovery periods when their actual attachment, function, and classification support it. Do not automatically classify vinyl flooring, cabinets, counters, fixtures, or dedicated building systems as five-year property. Multiply only defensible unit-level allocations by the units in which those assets actually exist.

Potential common-area personal property. Furniture and movable fitness or laundry equipment can differ from structural finishes, building electrical, security systems, or pool infrastructure. Document the function and asset boundary before assigning a recovery period.

Potential land improvements. Parking, fencing, landscaping, and site lighting may be separately depreciable from the building; treatment of carports, retaining walls, drainage, pools, and other mixed systems depends on their facts. Land itself is not depreciable. Measure and price actual improvements rather than using a generic percentage of the purchase price.

The reclassified share varies with the property, purchase allocation, assets, and support. A study estimate is not a guaranteed deduction or tax saving.

The Numbers on a Real Deal

A 48-unit garden apartment complex purchased for $7,200,000, with $1,100,000 allocated to land, leaves a $6,100,000 depreciable basis.

For illustration only, suppose a study supports $1,647,000 of shorter-life property, or 27% of that depreciable basis. The actual asset mix could be materially different. Do not deduct that entire amount automatically: each asset must meet bonus-depreciation qualification, acquisition, placed-in-service, and election rules. The IRS's 2026 bonus guidance generally provides 100% treatment for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025.

If every illustrative shorter-life asset qualified for 100% bonus and the full-year simplifying assumptions held, the remaining $4,453,000 of structure would produce about $162,000 of straight-line depreciation, for a rough $1,809,000 first-year total. Actual first-year depreciation depends on conventions, dates, elections, and classifications; the simplified $222,000 without-study comparison is not an individualized return computation.

That simplified scenario shows an approximate $1,587,000 timing difference, not tax saved and not a result every multifamily property can claim. See bonus depreciation under OBBBA and the complete cost segregation guide.

Who Can Actually Use the Loss

A deduction that large runs straight into IRC Sec. 469, and the answer depends on how the property is held.

Individual owner-operators. A long-term multifamily rental is generally passive under Sec. 469(c)(2). A real estate professional must satisfy the statutory personal-service tests and materially participate in the particular rental activity (or validly grouped rental activity) for nonpassive treatment; a full-time title alone does not establish either test. Other limited exceptions and owner-specific rules may apply. See IRS Publication 925.

Syndication limited partners. Many investors receive passive rental losses that may offset passive income or carry forward. Release on disposition depends on the sale and activity facts; a partial transfer or related-party sale can change the result. See how cost segregation affects your syndication K-1.

Closely held C corporations. Under IRC Sec. 469(e)(2), passive losses may offset net active income. See the closely held C-Corp rule.

Real estate professionals. A qualifying taxpayer may elect under Treas. Reg. 1.469-9(g) to treat rental-real-estate interests as one rental activity for material-participation purposes, subject to the election's requirements and consequences. This is distinct from general activity-grouping rules. See grouping elections.

Partnership Allocation Issues

Multifamily is usually held in a partnership or multi-member LLC, which adds a layer.

Depreciation allocations depend on the partnership agreement and applicable tax-allocation rules, including substantial economic effect and other Section 704 limitations. Do not assume the sponsor can redirect deductions to particular investors merely because the study creates them; the tax capital accounts, debt allocations, and agreement must reconcile.

Partner-level limits apply in sequence: outside basis, at-risk, passive activity, and potentially excess-business-loss rules. An allocated partnership liability may increase outside basis but does not always increase the partner's at-risk amount. Qualified nonrecourse financing has conditions; model each investor rather than treating a mortgage as automatic current-year usability. The IRS Schedule K-1 instructions describe this order.

Buying Into an Existing Property

Multifamily changes hands frequently, and each purchase resets the analysis. A new buyer takes a new cost basis and can commission a new study regardless of whether the prior owner did one. Depreciation history does not carry over; it stays with the seller and is recaptured on their return.

Renovation spending after acquisition also matters. Value-add operators should distinguish repairs from capital improvements, identify assets actually removed, and classify each new asset by its facts and placed-in-service date; flooring and cabinetry are not automatically five-year property. An acquisition study can help document soon-to-be-retired components, while a coordinated later study may be sufficient when records support it. The IRS depreciation guidance and partial-disposition examination unit explain the separate issues. See the value-add renovation study-timing decision guide before demolition and filing.

An eligible roof replacement may also warrant a partial-disposition analysis: establish what was actually removed, the original asset and adjusted basis, and the year of disposal before considering a timely election. See partial asset disposition.

Properties Already Owned

A prior-year study can sometimes be implemented through Form 3115 and a Section 481(a) adjustment rather than a series of amended returns, but the procedure depends on the method adopted, years affected, eligibility for automatic consent, and the applicable year's bonus rules. Some corrections are not accounting-method changes. See lookback cost segregation and the IRS Form 3115 instructions.

Recapture at Exit

A later sale can trigger ordinary-income recapture on Section 1245 property and unrecaptured Section 1250 gain rules on qualifying real-property gain; the 25% figure is a maximum rate category, not an automatic tax rate. A Section 1031 exchange now generally covers real property, so personal-property allocations and recapture require separate transaction review rather than an assurance that the exchange defers both. Model sale timing, allocation, and tax before promising a net study benefit. See IRS Publication 544.

Getting the Study Right

Insist on a physical inspection with unit-level sampling across floor plans and renovation vintages, since a property with three unit types and two renovation phases has meaningfully different component values. Verify the land allocation against the appraisal. Confirm the report reconciles to total basis and cites classification authority. And require that site improvements be quantified from actual measurements rather than estimated as a percentage.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much of an apartment building can be reclassified?

There is no universal percentage. It depends on the purchase allocation, actual components, engineering support, and correct classification. A unit count or amenity list alone does not establish the tax result.

Can a passive multifamily investor use the depreciation loss?

Generally not against wages or operating-business income. A long-term rental loss may offset passive income or carry forward. A real estate professional must also materially participate for nonpassive treatment. Release of suspended losses on a sale depends on the transaction and activity facts.

How does partnership debt affect my ability to deduct the loss?

Allocated liabilities may increase outside basis, but not every liability increases the partner's at-risk amount. Apply basis, at-risk, passive activity, and other applicable limits in order, including conditions for qualified nonrecourse real-property financing.

Should I do the study before or after a value-add renovation?

It depends on planned demolition, records, placed-in-service dates, and filing deadlines. An early study can document assets removed during renovation, but it is not a legal prerequisite for a partial-disposition election. Later capitalized improvements need their own asset analysis; see our detailed sponsor decision guide.

Can I still do a study on a building I bought several years ago?

Often yes, but the procedure depends on prior treatment and accounting-method rules. Form 3115 with a Section 481(a) adjustment may be available for an eligible depreciation-method change; some errors need amended returns instead. Test the original asset acquisition and placed-in-service dates for bonus eligibility.
