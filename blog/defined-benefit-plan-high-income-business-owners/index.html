Defined Benefit Plans for High Income Business Owners: When $250,000 of Deduction Is Available

Reviewed November 6, 2026. Tax-year 2026 limits are identified separately from examples.

Published on August 11, 2026 | Business Owner Tax | AE Tax Advisors

For 2026, a defined contribution plan has a $72,000 annual additions ceiling before permitted catch-up contributions, subject to eligible compensation. A defined benefit pension uses a different benefit-based limit and can support larger contributions for some high-income owners.

A larger contribution is not automatically deductible or affordable. An enrolled actuary must determine the required funding and maximum deductible contribution, while the tax preparer checks compensation, other plans, QBI and return timing.

The decision is whether the owner benefit and tax result justify the required staff benefits, funding risk, administrative costs and long-term commitment.

How the Contribution Is Determined

The 2026 Section 415(b) annual-benefit dollar limit is $290,000, generally also limited to 100% of average compensation for the three highest consecutive years, with adjustments for age, participation, service and benefit form. It is not a fixed $290,000 contribution allowance.

An enrolled actuary determines minimum funding and maximum deductible amounts from the promised benefits, current assets, compensation history, remaining time and applicable assumptions. Older age or higher compensation may support more funding but does not establish a quoted deduction by itself.

For planning only, a 58-year-old with sufficient eligible compensation may need more annual funding than a 38-year-old promising the same retirement benefit. A $250,000 contribution must be supported by the actual calculation; age and current business profit alone cannot establish it.

A cash balance plan is a defined benefit plan expressed as a hypothetical account with pay and interest credits. The employer carries funding and investment risk even though the statement resembles an account balance.

Stacking With a 401(k)

A defined benefit plan can be paired with a 401(k) rather than replacing it. The 6% figure is not a universal contribution or deduction cap. Under Section 404(a)(7), employer defined contribution amounts up to 6% of covered compensation are excluded from the combined deduction calculation; when employer contributions exceed 6%, the excess enters the applicable combined test. Employee elective deferrals are excluded. A PBGC-covered defined benefit plan is excluded from this combined limit, but separate plan limits still apply. Have the actuary and tax preparer calculate both the minimum funding and maximum deductible contribution.

For 2026, the elective deferral limit is $24,500 and the defined contribution annual additions limit is $72,000, excluding permitted catch-up contributions. The ordinary age-50-or-older catch-up is $8,000; participants attaining ages 60 through 63 may use $11,250 instead, if permitted. Catch-up contributions for participants whose prior-year wages from the sponsoring employer exceeded $150,000 generally must be Roth in 2026. That wage test is not household income or net self-employment earnings, and Roth contributions are not current deductions.

An owner must compare total contributions, deductible employer amounts and pre-tax employee deferrals separately. Required Roth catch-up is retirement savings but not a current deduction. Eligible W-2 pay supports S-corporation employer contributions; distributions do not. Self-employed compensation requires the Publication 560 adjustments.

The Staff Cost Is the Deciding Factor

Defined benefit plans must satisfy minimum participation, coverage and nondiscrimination requirements. Eligibility and permissible combined-plan testing can affect benefits, but employees cannot be ignored merely because the owner wants a larger deduction.

Check controlled groups and affiliated service groups under Section 414 before deciding which businesses and employees belong in the census. Include ages, service, compensation, ownership and existing plan benefits. An owner-and-spouse business is not necessarily staff-free if related employers have eligible workers.

An older owner with younger staff may produce a more favorable owner-to-staff allocation than a workforce close to the owner in age and pay, but an 8-to-1 ratio is not a standard entitlement. The actuary must quantify and test actual benefits before adoption.

If there truly are no eligible non-owner employees after the related-employer review, there may be no non-owner staff contribution. Plan administration, actuarial work, funding rules and owner benefits still need analysis.

The Commitment Is Real

Minimum required funding under Section 430 must be met; underfunding can cause Section 4971 excise taxes and reporting obligations. Defined benefit minimum funding is generally due 8.5 months after the plan year ends, normally September 15 for a calendar-year plan. Quarterly installments may apply. An October 15 extended individual tax-return deadline does not postpone that funding deadline. Contributions paid by the applicable extended employer return deadline may be deductible for the prior tax year when properly designated and within Section 404 limits; the funding and deduction calculations must both be satisfied.

Most qualified employer plans may be adopted by the employer tax-return due date, including extensions, and treated as adopted for the prior tax year. Adoption, employee deferral elections, deposit deadlines, and employer contribution deductibility are separate tests. Salary deferrals generally require a timely election before compensation becomes available; adopting a plan later does not authorize retroactive S-corporation payroll deferrals. A narrow first-year exception permits an individual who owns the entire unincorporated business and is its only employee to adopt a new 401(k) and make first-year elective deferrals by the unextended individual return deadline.

A qualified pension is intended to be permanent as an ongoing retirement program. Three to five years is sometimes used as a planning horizon, but it is not a statutory safe harbor. A rapid termination needs a legitimate business-reason and facts-and-circumstances review.

Prospective amendments and a freeze may change future accruals with required notices, but cannot erase already earned benefits or an existing funding shortfall. A frozen plan can still require funding, actuarial valuations, reporting and PBGC premiums where covered. Termination can require additional cash before benefits are distributed.

Investment risk rests with the employer. Asset losses can increase required funding, while stronger returns can reduce it. Size the commitment for a weak year and review a contemplated business sale before adoption.

Costs and Administration

Actuarial and administration fees typically run $2,500 to $6,000 annually for a small plan, plus setup costs. These are planning ranges, not a fee quote. Annual actuarial work and applicable Form 5500-series filing must be addressed. Eligible one-participant plans generally use Form 5500-EZ, with the $250,000 combined-asset filing exception and a final-return requirement regardless of assets. Required Schedule SB is generally retained, rather than attached, for Form 5500-EZ filers.

PBGC coverage is determined by law, not elected solely to obtain a tax result. A qualifying professional-service employer plan is exempt only if it has never had more than 25 active participants; current total participants alone do not answer the question. The employer must meet the professional ownership and service requirements. A separate exemption applies to plans covering only substantial owners under PBGC rules. Obtain a coverage determination when uncertain.

For a PBGC-covered defined benefit plan, premiums and termination procedures add costs, and that plan is excluded from the Section 404(a)(7) combined deduction limitation. Other funding and deduction rules still apply. Compare recurring fees, staff benefits and exit liabilities with the modeled after-tax benefit rather than treating them as trivial.

Who Should Not Do This

A business whose cash flow cannot reliably meet minimum funding, including in a weak year.

A business whose required staff benefits and administration outweigh the owner benefit after complete census testing.

An owner who has not compared a simpler 401(k) design and its usable contribution capacity. Automatically maximizing profit sharing first may not fit a combined plan deduction calculation.

An owner approaching a sale who has not reviewed plan permanence, transaction obligations and termination funding with the actuary.

A younger owner with modest eligible compensation whose projected pension benefit does not justify the cost and commitment.

Worked Example: Two Owner Firms

The following firms are hypothetical arithmetic illustrations, not client outcomes or validated actuarial designs. They retain assumed contribution and cost inputs to show how to compare proposals. Age, profit and salaries alone do not establish that these allocations pass coverage, benefit, funding or deduction tests.

Firm A assumes a 56-year-old consultant with $680,000 of business net income and two employees aged 29 and 34 earning $62,000 and $71,000. The owner's eligible plan compensation must be calculated separately. Assume a proposal allocates $70,000 of 401(k) contributions to the owner with $9,800 of staff cost, and $237,000 of cash balance contributions to the owner with $16,400 more staff cost. The $70,000 is an assumed amount below the 2026 $72,000 non-catch-up ceiling, not the current ceiling.

The assumed owner total is $307,000 and staff contributions total $26,200, a ratio of about 11.72 to 1. If all $307,000 is permitted and pre-tax and a flat 42% marginal benefit applies, simple multiplication gives $128,940, approximately $129,000, before QBI and other tax effects. Administration is assumed at $5,000. This is not proof of a deductible design: the employer portion above 6% of covered compensation may enter the combined deduction test, and Roth amounts would not produce current deductions.

Firm B assumes three architecture-firm partners aged 54, 56 and 58, with four senior staff aged 47 to 55 earning $110,000 to $145,000. Assume a separate proposal allocates $580,000 to partners and $198,000 to staff. The arithmetic ratio is about 2.93 to 1; those inputs are not a finding that the same plan formula produces those amounts.

Firm B might still value the staff benefits and retirement funding. Neither illustration should be implemented without a signed actuarial proposal, related-employer review, PBGC coverage analysis and tax projection. Compare total business cash cost, not only owner-to-staff ratios.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can a defined benefit plan deduct?

The allowable deduction is actuarially determined under Section 404 and applicable combined-plan rules. The 2026 $290,000 annual-benefit dollar limit is not a contribution allowance. Age, eligible compensation, existing assets, benefits and employee census must be modeled before quoting a deduction.

Can I have a defined benefit plan and a 401(k)?

The 6% figure is not a universal contribution or deduction cap. Under Section 404(a)(7), employer defined contribution amounts up to 6% of covered compensation are excluded from the combined deduction calculation; when employer contributions exceed 6%, the excess enters the applicable combined test. Employee elective deferrals are excluded. A PBGC-covered defined benefit plan is excluded from this combined limit, but separate plan limits still apply. Have the actuary and tax preparer calculate both the minimum funding and maximum deductible contribution.

What does a defined benefit plan cost me in staff contributions?

The cost depends on actual employees, compensation, ages, service, plan formulas and related employers. Owner-to-staff ratios in illustrations are not guaranteed allocations. Have the actuary test the complete census and calculate staff funding before adoption.

Am I locked in once I start?

Minimum funding and earned benefits remain obligations. Prospective amendments or a freeze can affect future accruals but do not erase funding shortfalls or reporting. Three to five years is not a legal permanence safe harbor, and termination can require additional cash and applicable PBGC procedures.

Who is a poor candidate?

An owner with volatile cash flow, unfavorable employee costs, limited eligible compensation or an impending sale without a funding and termination review may be a poor fit. Compare simpler plan designs and obtain actual actuarial and tax projections rather than deciding from profit or age alone.
