How Should I Structure My Real Estate Portfolio for Tax Efficiency?

Published on August 21, 2026 | Real Estate Tax Strategy | AE Tax Advisors

Tax-efficient portfolio structuring is one of the most impactful decisions a real estate investor can make. The right structure can save hundreds of thousands of dollars over the life of your portfolio, while the wrong structure can create unnecessary tax liability, compliance burdens, and missed deductions. A tax-efficient real estate portfolio addresses entity selection, depreciation strategy, passive activity planning, and exit strategy from the beginning.

Entity Structure: Matching the Right Entity to Each Property

The default starting point for most rental property investors is the single-member LLC (SMLLC), which is treated as a disregarded entity for federal tax purposes under Treas. Reg. Section 301.7701-3. Income and expenses flow directly to your personal Schedule E, and the LLC provides liability protection without adding tax complexity. For most investors with one to five properties, individual SMLLCs for each property, or a Series LLC in states that permit them, provide the right balance of protection and simplicity.

Multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships (Form 1065) are appropriate when you co-invest with partners. The partnership structure provides flexibility in allocating income, losses, depreciation, and credits among partners through the operating agreement, as long as allocations have "substantial economic effect" under IRC Section 704(b). This is useful when one partner contributes capital while another contributes management expertise.

S corporations are rarely the best choice for holding rental property because rental income is not subject to self-employment tax, and transferring appreciated property into or out of an S-corp can trigger taxable events. C corporations are even less suitable, rental income trapped in a C-corp faces double taxation.

Depreciation Strategy: Maximizing Front-Loaded Deductions

A tax-efficient portfolio maximizes depreciation deductions, particularly in years when the investor has the highest marginal tax rate. This means commissioning cost segregation studies on every property with a depreciable basis exceeding $300,000 (a rough threshold where the study's cost is justified by the tax savings). Cost segregation reclassifies 20% to 35% of the building's value into shorter recovery periods eligible for bonus depreciation under IRC Section 168(k).

Timing acquisitions strategically can amplify the benefit. Purchasing properties late in the year still generates a full year of bonus depreciation on reclassified components (bonus depreciation is not subject to the mid-month convention). An investor who closes on a $1 million property in December can claim $250,000 or more in cost segregation deductions for that tax year.

Passive Activity Planning

Portfolio structuring must account for the passive activity loss rules under IRC Section 469. The ideal structure ensures that passive losses generated by depreciation-heavy properties can be used currently, not suspended indefinitely. There are three tiers of planning depending on income level.

For investors with MAGI under $100,000, the $25,000 special allowance under IRC Section 469(i) allows rental losses to offset active income. Portfolio structuring should ensure the investor actively participates in each rental activity and that total rental losses do not significantly exceed $25,000 (unless excess losses can be absorbed by passive income from other sources).

For higher-income investors, REPS qualification becomes the primary objective. Structuring the portfolio so one spouse can dedicate full-time effort to real estate management, and documenting 750+ hours with a contemporaneous log, converts all rental losses from passive to non-passive. The grouping election under Treas. Reg. Section 1.469-9(g) treats all properties as a single activity, simplifying material participation compliance.

Exit Strategy: Planning Before You Buy

Tax-efficient structuring begins with the end in mind. Every property should have a planned exit strategy that accounts for capital gains, depreciation recapture, and available deferral mechanisms. The most common exit strategies include a 1031 exchange into larger or more productive properties, an installment sale to spread gain recognition over multiple years, a conversion to primary residence to capture the IRC Section 121 exclusion (subject to the non-qualified use limitations), or a hold-until-death strategy that leverages the stepped-up basis under IRC Section 1014.

For investors building generational wealth, the hold-and-refinance strategy is particularly tax-efficient. Instead of selling and triggering gain recognition, you refinance the property to access equity tax-free (loan proceeds are not taxable income). You continue holding the property, claiming depreciation, and using the cash-out refinance proceeds for new acquisitions. This creates a cycle of tax-free cash access, ongoing depreciation deductions, and portfolio growth, all without triggering capital gains.

Putting It All Together

The most tax-efficient real estate portfolios share common characteristics: properties are held in appropriate entities, cost segregation is performed on every qualifying acquisition, one spouse qualifies as a Real Estate Professional (for households above the $150,000 MAGI threshold), passive losses are matched against passive income where REPS is not available, and exit strategies are planned at acquisition rather than improvised at sale. A real estate CPA should be involved in structuring decisions before you close on your next property.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does estate planning interact with real estate depreciation?

Accelerated depreciation creates recapture exposure on sale. Holding the property until death converts that exposure into a permanent benefit because the basis step-up eliminates the deferred gain, which is why exit planning and depreciation strategy are decided together.

Should business interests be transferred during life or at death?

It depends on expected appreciation and basis. Transferring an interest expected to appreciate substantially removes future growth from the estate, while holding an appreciated low-basis asset until death captures the step-up. The right answer usually blends both.

What is the difference between a revocable and irrevocable trust?

A revocable trust remains under the grantor's control, is included in the estate, and provides probate avoidance rather than tax reduction. An irrevocable trust generally removes assets from the estate for transfer tax purposes but gives up control and may have its own income tax consequences.

What is the step-up in basis at death?

Under IRC Section 1014, most assets included in a decedent's estate receive a basis equal to fair market value at the date of death. This eliminates unrealized appreciation and, for real estate, wipes out accumulated depreciation recapture, which is why holding appreciated property until death is a core planning strategy.
