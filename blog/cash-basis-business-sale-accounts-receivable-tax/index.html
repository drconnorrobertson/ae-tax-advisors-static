I am selling my cash-basis business. Are unpaid receivables ordinary income if the buyer takes them?

Published September 25, 2026 | Exit Planning | AE Tax Advisors

Generally, yes: the amount properly allocated to trade receivables the buyer acquires is ordinary income to a cash-method seller, not capital gain from goodwill. Unpaid customer invoices for services or inventory ordinarily have not yet entered a cash-method seller's taxable receipts, and their tax basis is often zero. IRS Publication 544 identifies ordinary-course trade receivables as noncapital assets; the Form 8594 instructions place accounts receivable in Class III for an applicable asset acquisition. The actual answer depends on whether the buyer acquires the invoices, what consideration is allocated to them, the seller's tax method and basis, and who later collects or bears a shortfall.

This matters to a service-business owner or CFO negotiating the purchase agreement and final return, not just to the bookkeeper cleaning up an aging report. AE can reconcile the invoice register, tax-method workpapers, asset purchase agreement, funds flow and Form 8594 before ordinary income is hidden in goodwill or counted again when customers pay.

First establish who owns the invoices after closing

An asset purchase agreement may include all closing-date receivables, exclude them for the seller to collect, or let the seller collect as the buyer's servicing agent. A working-capital schedule may set a target and later true-up the price for collections or writeoffs. None of those arrangements can be inferred from the balance-sheet label alone. Read the purchased-assets exhibit, excluded-assets exhibit, collection covenant, post-closing remittance procedure, and buyer credit-risk terms together. Also distinguish an existing invoice from unbilled work in progress, a deposit for future services, or a disputed customer claim; they are not automatically interchangeable receivables.

If the selling entity keeps its invoices, their later collection generally follows its cash-method income rules rather than becoming buyer-paid sale proceeds. If the buyer acquires them for value, the seller must analyze the transferred receivables as an asset sold. A seller that merely receives customer payments as the buyer's agent should not automatically record those deposits as retained seller revenue; the contract and remittance ledger must support the agency relationship. The IRS small-business tax guide explains the cash method's receipt rule, while the asset-sale rules govern what the buyer actually bought.

A $720,000 allocation is not $800,000 of goodwill

Suppose an S corporation uses the cash method for tax, has $800,000 face amount of valid, unpaid service invoices at closing, and sells its operations in an asset deal. After an invoice-level collectibility review, the buyer and seller support a $720,000 value and allocate that amount of the total purchase consideration to the receivables. Assume the seller has not previously recognized the $720,000 and has no tax basis in those invoices. The starting seller result is $720,000 of ordinary income from the transferred receivables, not $800,000 because that is the face amount, and not long-term capital gain merely because the sale agreement also includes goodwill. The entity's return and shareholder K-1s must reflect the asset-level result. The buyer's later collections are a separate buyer-side question.

Change the facts before copying the example. If the seller retained $80,000 of other invoices, those are not part of the $720,000 sold pool; track their later collections separately. If an invoice was previously included in taxable income under an accrual method or another rule, it may have tax basis and require a different gain or loss calculation. If buyer payments for the receivables depend on later collections, determine when and how the contingent consideration is taken into account instead of assuming all face value is taxable at closing. Do not use the $720,000 example as a fixed discount, rate, or valuation method.

Build the sale-year return from an invoice-level schedule

  1. Verify the deal form and seller tax method. An actual asset sale, plain stock sale, and deemed asset sale have different filing paths. Reconcile the seller's tax accounting method to its book-basis receivable ledger; a GAAP balance does not establish tax basis. AE's stock-versus-asset guide covers the first fork.
  2. Identify the transferred invoices. Use invoice date, customer, service period, face amount, credits, disputed amounts, and collections through closing. Mark each invoice as purchased, retained, or collected for the buyer after closing.
  3. Support the allocation. Compare the buyer's valuation and aging reserve with actual customer history, then reconcile Class III receivables to the other asset classes under the Form 8594 residual method. A contractual goodwill label cannot turn a transferred invoice into goodwill. See AE's allocation decision guide for the neighboring asset classes.
  4. Carry the result through the filings. Trace the receivable sale to ordinary-income workpapers, Form 8594, Form 1120-S or 1065, K-1s, and owner basis schedules as applicable. Reconcile cash received, any note or holdback, and any assumed liabilities separately so one item is not counted twice.
  5. Track later changes. A working-capital true-up, customer credit or contingent collection payment may change consideration or a retained collection in a later year. Review the supplemental Form 8594 rules and AE's post-closing true-up guide before changing a filed return.

Documents and failure points before the buyer receives the ledger

Bring the signed asset purchase agreement and asset schedules; tax-method history; receivables aging and invoice register; contracts and customer credits; payment history, collection and servicing agreement; working-capital formula; buyer and seller valuations; closing statement; prior return and fixed-asset schedules; Form 8594 draft; and a dated reconciliation of each invoice to buyer or seller ownership. If a filing was already made, include the entity return, K-1s and owner basis workpapers before choosing any correction procedure.

  • Putting all value in goodwill. Receivables transferred in an applicable asset acquisition have their own Form 8594 class and generally ordinary seller character.
  • Taxing the face balance by default. The sale allocation is based on the consideration and supported value, not automatically the entire invoice total.
  • Taxing the same amount twice. Reconcile sale-year income from transferred invoices with later customer collections and buyer-agent remittances.
  • Assuming book method equals tax method. An accrual financial statement can coexist with a cash-method tax return; identify whether income has already been recognized and any resulting basis.
  • Ignoring later price changes. A disputed balance or working-capital adjustment can affect the amount and year reported; document the settlement rather than silently changing goodwill.

This is a federal return framework, not an individualized conclusion about your invoices. Related-party sales, state income tax, factoring, doubtful accounts, contingent consideration, installment obligations and partnership-interest sales can change the treatment. In particular, a partnership interest sale may invoke Section 751 hot-asset rules rather than this direct receivable-asset analysis; see AE's partnership buyout guide. Get the agreement and invoice schedule reviewed before the parties sign inconsistent allocations.
