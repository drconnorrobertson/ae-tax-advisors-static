Can I Do a Cost Segregation Study on a Property I Already Own?

Can You Do a Cost Segregation Study on a Condo?

Yes, if the condo is used in a trade or business or held to produce income. A rental condo can qualify even though the owner does not own an entire freestanding building. The study must follow the property rights and basis the taxpayer actually acquired: the deeded unit, the owner's percentage interest in common elements, qualifying limited common elements, and separately purchased furnishings or improvements. Land, personal-use portions, and property owned solely by the association cannot be treated as the unit owner's depreciable building components without support.

This is not a “no land because it is a condo” analysis. IRS Publication 527 explains that a condominium owner generally owns the unit plus a share of common elements such as land, lobbies, elevators, and service areas. That ownership share may carry both depreciable improvement basis and nondepreciable land basis. The declaration, deed, closing records, and a supportable allocation—not a generic percentage copied from another project—determine the study boundary.

A six-gate condo review before ordering the study

  1. Confirm income-producing use. Identify the rental or business portion, personal-use days, and the date the unit was ready and available for rent.
  2. Reconcile tax basis. Start with closing costs, credits, improvements, casualty adjustments, and any conversion-date limitation—not merely the price shown on a listing.
  3. Read the ownership documents. The declaration and deed establish the unit boundaries, percentage interest, and rights in parking, storage, balconies, and other limited common elements.
  4. Separate land and common improvements. Allocate the owner's acquisition basis between nondepreciable land and depreciable improvements using property-specific evidence.
  5. Classify only supported assets. Connect each unit or common-element component to the taxpayer's basis and the applicable tax authority; an association asset is not automatically a separate owner asset.
  6. Model the return result. Test passive-loss, at-risk, basis, business-use, hold-period, recapture, and state-conformity effects before treating accelerated depreciation as usable tax savings.

Worked example: rental condo with common-element and furnishing basis

An investor buys a furnished rental condo for $800,000 and pays $12,000 of capitalizable acquisition costs. The declaration gives the unit a 2.4% undivided interest in the association's common elements and an exclusive-use parking space. The closing records do not separately price land, building, furnishings, or parking. The investor also spends $28,000 replacing appliances and interior finishes before the unit is ready to rent.

A defensible study does not apply an online “accelerated percentage” to $840,000. The team first reconciles acquisition and improvement basis, identifies the furnishings actually conveyed, analyzes the parking right, and develops a supportable allocation for the unit's share of land and common improvements. The engineer then classifies eligible components only within the resulting depreciable basis. The return model separately tests when each cost was placed in service, the available depreciation rules, and whether the investor can currently use the deduction.

HOA dues and special assessments require different treatment

Publication 527 states that payments to an association for maintenance of common elements can generally be deducted as rental expenses, but special assessments for improvements are not currently deductible as ordinary maintenance. The owner's supportable share of a capital improvement may instead be recovered through depreciation. That does not mean every reserve contribution creates a separately classifiable asset: determine what was built, when it was placed in service, whether the owner has basis in it, and which recovery period applies.

Condo documents to gather

  • Closing statement, deed, purchase agreement, appraisal, and property-tax or other land-allocation evidence.
  • Declaration, condominium plat, percentage-interest schedule, bylaws, and amendments describing unit and limited-common-element boundaries.
  • HOA reserve study, capital-assessment notices, board minutes, project invoices, and proof of the owner's payments.
  • Floor plans, photographs, inspection reports, renovation invoices, and an inventory of appliances and furnishings.
  • Rental listing, lease or management agreement, personal-use calendar, and evidence of the ready-and-available date.
  • Prior Forms 4562, depreciation schedules, fixed-asset ledgers, and returns for every year the condo was depreciated.

Condo-specific failure points

  • Claiming there is no land allocation. A condo interest can include an undivided share of the underlying land, which remains nondepreciable.
  • Studying 100% of shared facilities. The taxpayer generally has only the percentage interest stated in the governing documents.
  • Depreciating association spending with no owner-basis trail. Dues, reserves, repairs, and capital assessments must be traced separately.
  • Ignoring personal use. Vacation use can affect expense allocation and deduction limits even when the condo is advertised for rent.
  • Treating a co-op as a condo. Stock-basis and tenant-stockholder rules require a different analysis.
  • Ordering a study before testing the return. A technically sound reclassification may not produce a current cash benefit if deductions are suspended or the unit will be sold soon.

Study quality should also be measured against the current IRS Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide, while Publication 946 supplies the general depreciation and method-change framework.

Can You Do Cost Segregation on a Primary Residence?

Not on the portion used only as your personal home. Cost segregation accelerates depreciation; it does not make personal-use property depreciable. A study may become relevant when you convert the home to a bona fide rental, rent a separately supportable part of it, or use a qualifying portion in a trade or business. Even then, only the depreciable business or income-producing portion—and not the land or personal-use portion—can enter the analysis.

The phrase “primary residence” is not enough to answer the question. The tax result follows the property's actual use, the date that use changed, the portion involved, the adjusted basis and fair market value on the conversion date, and the records supporting each allocation. The IRS Publication 527 states that personal-use property cannot be depreciated and that a former home converted to rental use begins depreciation when its use changes and it is ready and available for rent.

The Conversion-Date Basis Rule Can Shrink the Study

When a personal residence becomes a rental, its depreciation basis is generally the lower of its adjusted basis or fair market value on the conversion date. That rule can prevent a decline in personal-use value from becoming a depreciation deduction. After identifying the lower starting amount, allocate between land and depreciable improvements; only then can a study classify eligible building and site components.

Worked example: former home converted after a value decline

An owner bought a home for $700,000, allocated $140,000 to land, and made $40,000 of capital improvements. At conversion, the adjusted property basis is $740,000, but fair market value is $620,000. If a supportable conversion-date allocation assigns $124,000 of fair market value to land and $496,000 to the building, the preliminary depreciable basis is $496,000—not the original $560,000 building allocation plus all improvements. A cost segregation study would classify eligible components within the conversion-date depreciable basis; it would not study the personal-use decline as though it were a tax cost.

Worked example: renting one floor while living in the rest

A homeowner converts a legally separate lower level representing 28% of supportable building area into a long-term rental. A simple 28% allocation may be a starting point for whole-building costs, but costs serving only one portion should be traced directly and common mechanical or site components need a reasoned allocation. The study should exclude the owner-occupied portion and land. The return must also allocate recurring expenses between personal and rental use.

When a Home Is Actually Placed in Service

Moving out is not necessarily the depreciation start date. A converted home is generally placed in service when it is ready and available for rent. Repairs made before the property is available, advertising dates, lease dates, and the condition of the home can affect this conclusion. Publication 527 gives an example in which a former home is treated as placed in service when listed and available for rent, before the tenant's move-in date.

This date controls the depreciation system and convention and can affect bonus depreciation eligibility for shorter-lived property. It also separates nondeductible personal-use periods from rental periods. Do not backdate the placed-in-service date to the original home purchase simply because the later study examines original construction components.

Decision Checklist Before Ordering a Study

  1. Define the use. Document whether the property is entirely personal, fully converted, partly rented, used for a qualifying office, or subject to vacation-home rules.
  2. Fix the placed-in-service date. Gather listings, management agreements, readiness records, leases, and dated photos.
  3. Compute conversion basis. Reconcile original cost, land, capital improvements, casualty adjustments, credits, and conversion-date fair market value.
  4. Allocate personal, rental, and land portions. Use direct tracing when possible and a supportable method for shared costs.
  5. Test study economics. Compare the fee and after-tax timing benefit with hold period, current depreciation, loss usability, and future recapture.
  6. Choose the filing route. A current-year conversion and a lookback correction are not the same filing task. Prior consistent depreciation may require Form 3115; a one-year error can follow different correction rules.

The current IRS Publication 946 explains when a depreciation change is a method change, how a Section 481(a) adjustment works, and when automatic-change procedures may be available. The IRS's 2025 Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide describes the records and methodology examiners evaluate; it is an examination guide, not binding authority.

Documents to Gather for a Converted or Mixed-Use Home

  • Original closing statement, purchase allocation, and records of capital improvements made before conversion.
  • A conversion-date appraisal or other contemporaneous fair-market-value support with a separate land allocation.
  • Rental listing, property-management agreement, lease, occupancy history, and evidence of the ready-and-available date.
  • Floor plans, square footage, photographs, construction invoices, and records identifying components used only by the rental or business portion.
  • Prior depreciation schedules, Forms 4562, Schedules E, and any prior cost segregation or fixed-asset report.
  • Personal-use and rental-day calendars for a mixed-use vacation property.
  • Participation and passive-loss records if the projected deduction is being evaluated against wages or business income.

Common Failure Points

  • Studying the full home while the owner still occupies part of it. Personal-use basis does not become depreciable because an engineer lists components.
  • Using original cost after a lower-value conversion. The lower-of-adjusted-basis-or-fair-market-value rule must be applied first.
  • Ignoring land. Land is not depreciable and must be excluded before asset classification.
  • Backdating depreciation to the home purchase. Personal-use years are not rental placed-in-service years.
  • Assuming a deduction equals tax savings. Passive activity, basis, at-risk, and other return-level rules can suspend the accelerated deduction.
  • Using Form 3115 automatically. The correction route depends on the existing method, number of returns, ownership, year of change, and the current automatic-change scope rules.
  • Ordering a study without an exit model. A short hold period, sale plan, or personal reconversion can change the present value and recapture analysis.

The IRS Mechanism: Form 3115

When you perform a cost segregation study on a property you already own, you may be changing your method of accounting for depreciation on certain components. Revenue Procedure 2015-13 supplies the general method-change procedures, and the current automatic-change list and modifications must be checked for the requested year. An impermissible-to-permissible depreciation change for eligible property is commonly filed on Form 3115 under DCN 7, but the facts and current scope rules—not the label “cost segregation”—determine the correct DCN and filing route.

The critical advantage is the IRC Section 481(a) adjustment. When you change your depreciation method, you calculate what your depreciation would have been if you had used the correct (reclassified) method from the start, compare it to what you actually claimed, and the difference, called the Section 481(a) adjustment, is taken as a deduction in the current year. There is no need to amend prior-year returns.

A Practical Example

Suppose you purchased a rental property five years ago for $600,000, with a depreciable basis of $480,000. You have been depreciating the entire building on a straight-line basis over 27.5 years, claiming approximately $17,455 per year in depreciation. A cost segregation study now identifies $144,000 (30%) as 5-year, 7-year, or 15-year property.

Under the correct depreciation method, those components would have been fully depreciated years ago (assuming bonus depreciation was available at the time of purchase). The cumulative depreciation you should have claimed on those components is $144,000 in Year 1 (at 100% bonus depreciation), but you only claimed $26,182 over five years on those same components using the 27.5-year schedule. The Section 481(a) adjustment, the catch-up deduction, would be approximately $117,818, all taken in the current tax year.

Key Requirements and Timing

For an eligible automatic change, Form 3115 is generally attached to the timely filed federal income tax return, including extensions, for the year of change, and the filer must follow the current duplicate-copy or electronic procedures in effect for that filing. The form and attachments require the property facts, old and new methods, legal support, and Section 481(a) computation. Filing mechanics can change, so use the current Form 3115 instructions and applicable revenue procedures.

A qualifying method change uses a current-year Section 481(a) adjustment rather than reopening every affected return. That does not mean every old property or every error qualifies automatically. Property ownership, prior method, number of returns, year-of-change status, scope limitations, disposed assets, elections, and procedural compliance must be checked before relying on Form 3115.

Properties That Benefit Most

Properties placed in service during tax years when bonus depreciation was available at high percentages (80% or above under IRC Section 168(k)) produce the largest catch-up deductions. Properties purchased before 2017, when bonus depreciation did not apply to used property, may generate smaller but still significant adjustments through MACRS accelerated methods (200% declining balance for personal property) versus straight-line depreciation.

The lookback approach may work for many types of depreciable real property—single-family rentals, apartment buildings, office buildings, retail spaces, restaurants, and medical facilities—but not every property produces a useful current deduction. Depreciable basis, asset mix, prior depreciation, study cost, remaining hold period, passive-loss position, and exit consequences should be modeled first.

Work with a tax advisor who understands both the engineering and the Form 3115 filing requirements to ensure the study and the method change are properly documented and defensible.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you do a cost segregation study on a condo?

Yes, when the condo is used in a trade or business or held to produce income. The study must start with the owner's actual depreciable basis and ownership rights under the deed and declaration, including any supportable share of common elements, while excluding land and personal-use portions. A cooperative apartment follows different stock-basis rules and should not be analyzed as a condominium.

Are HOA special assessments deductible or depreciable for a rental condo?

An assessment for ordinary maintenance may be a current rental expense, but a special assessment for a capital improvement generally is not currently deductible. The owner's supportable share may create or increase depreciable basis, depending on what the assessment funded and the owner's legal interest, and then must be recovered under the applicable depreciation rules.

Can you do cost segregation on a primary residence?

Not on the portion used only as your personal home, because personal-use property is not depreciable. A cost segregation analysis may apply after a documented conversion to rental use or to a separately supportable business or rental portion, but only the depreciable portion and its properly determined basis can be reclassified.

Is a cost segregation study worth the cost?

It depends on depreciable basis, asset mix, study fee, hold period, tax rate, depreciation already claimed, and whether the resulting deduction can be used. A dollar threshold alone cannot establish value; compare the after-tax timing benefit with the fee and future recapture before proceeding.

What is a cost segregation study?

A cost segregation study is an engineering-based analysis that separates eligible building costs into components with the appropriate MACRS classifications, often including 5-year, 7-year, and 15-year property instead of only 27.5-year or 39-year building property. The deduction timing depends on placed-in-service dates, asset eligibility, conventions, elections, and the bonus-depreciation law that applies.

Can I do a study on a property I bought years ago?

Often. If prior consistent depreciation is an impermissible method and the taxpayer is eligible under current procedures, Form 3115 may capture the cumulative difference through a Section 481(a) adjustment. A one-year error, disposed property, or ineligible change may require a different procedure, so Form 3115 is not automatic.

Do I need an engineering-based study or is an estimate enough?

The IRS Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide identifies the detailed engineering approach as the most reliable method. Rule-of-thumb allocations without site work or construction document review are the first thing challenged on examination, particularly for specialty systems and site improvements.
