Cash Balance Plans: How Business Owners Are Sheltering $300K+ Per Year in 2026

Reviewed November 6, 2026. Tax-year 2026 limits are identified separately from examples.

Published on June 29, 2026 | Business Tax Strategy | AE Tax Advisors

Most high-earning business owners know about the 401(k). Some have added a SEP-IRA or a profit sharing component. But there is a retirement vehicle hiding in plain sight that can dwarf every other option on the table: the cash balance plan.

A properly designed cash balance plan paired with a 401(k) can support substantial retirement contributions for some high-income owners, sometimes exceeding $300,000. That is not an automatic age-based allowance or a dollar-for-dollar reduction in final taxable income. An actuary must calculate funding and deductible limits, and the tax projection must include employee costs, Roth contributions, QBI effects and the owner's actual brackets.

Cash balance plans are qualified defined benefit plans governed by the Internal Revenue Code and, where applicable, ERISA. They require ongoing funding and administration. The decision is whether the projected retirement benefit and after-tax result justify the cash commitment, staff benefits and plan costs for this business.

What Is a Cash Balance Plan?

A cash balance plan is a type of defined benefit pension plan. Unlike a 401(k), where the employee chooses how to invest and bears the investment risk, a defined benefit plan promises a specific retirement benefit and requires the employer to fund it. The employer carries the investment risk.

What makes a cash balance plan different from a traditional pension is its structure. Rather than calculating benefits based on years of service and final salary, a cash balance plan maintains a hypothetical account balance for each participant. Each year, the plan credits two things to that account: a contribution credit (typically a percentage of pay) and an interest credit (a fixed or variable rate set in the plan document). The participant sees a growing balance that feels similar to a 401(k), even though the legal structure underneath is a defined benefit plan.

The hypothetical account presentation can be easier for participants to understand than a traditional pension formula. It does not turn the plan into an individual investment account or eliminate the employer's obligation to fund promised benefits.

Why the Contribution Limits Are Dramatically Higher

For 2026, the elective deferral limit is $24,500 and the defined contribution annual additions limit is $72,000, excluding permitted catch-up contributions. The ordinary age-50-or-older catch-up is $8,000; participants attaining ages 60 through 63 may use $11,250 instead, if permitted. Catch-up contributions for participants whose prior-year wages from the sponsoring employer exceeded $150,000 generally must be Roth in 2026. That wage test is not household income or net self-employment earnings, and Roth contributions are not current deductions.

A cash balance plan funds a promised benefit rather than using the defined contribution dollar cap. For 2026, the Section 415(b) annual-benefit dollar limit is $290,000, subject to compensation, age, service, participation and benefit-form adjustments. It is not a $290,000 contribution allowance. An enrolled actuary calculates minimum required funding and maximum deductible contributions based on the benefit formula, compensation history, existing assets and applicable assumptions.

Older owners may have fewer years to fund a promised benefit, which can support larger contributions. The following rough planning ranges are illustrations only, not statutory limits, actuarial quotes or client results. They require sufficient eligible compensation and a validated plan design; actual amounts may fall outside these ranges:

  • A 45-year-old business owner may be able to contribute approximately $100,000 to $150,000 per year
  • A 55-year-old may be able to contribute $200,000 to $250,000 per year
  • A 60-year-old may be able to contribute $280,000 to $350,000 per year

Do not simply add the largest illustrated pension amount to a maximum 401(k). The 6% figure is not a universal contribution or deduction cap. Under Section 404(a)(7), employer defined contribution amounts up to 6% of covered compensation are excluded from the combined deduction calculation; when employer contributions exceed 6%, the excess enters the applicable combined test. Employee elective deferrals are excluded. A PBGC-covered defined benefit plan is excluded from this combined limit, but separate plan limits still apply. Have the actuary and tax preparer calculate both the minimum funding and maximum deductible contribution.

The Stacking Strategy: 401(k) Plus Cash Balance Plan

The most powerful implementation for a business owner is the combination plan: a 401(k) profit sharing plan paired with a cash balance plan. These two plans can coexist in the same business and are separately governed by their respective IRC sections.

Illustrative 2026 arithmetic, not an actuarial quote or client outcome: assume a 55-year-old S-corporation owner has $300,000 of eligible W-2 compensation, no other elective deferrals, no eligible employees after related-business review, and prior-year wages from this sponsor exceeding $150,000. Assume the actuary validates a $250,000 cash balance contribution and the full deduction for this particular design. The owner's $8,000 catch-up is therefore Roth, not pre-tax:

Traditional 401(k) elective deferral: $24,500
Age-55 Roth catch-up: $8,000
Employer profit sharing (6% of $300,000 W-2 pay): $18,000
Cash balance contribution (assumed validated actuarial result): $250,000

The illustration totals $300,500 of contributions, but only $292,500 is pre-tax: $268,000 of assumed deductible employer contributions plus $24,500 of traditional employee deferrals. The $8,000 Roth catch-up does not reduce current income. Multiplying $292,500 by 37% gives $108,225 before QBI effects, bracket changes, state rules and other tax interactions; that arithmetic is not a promised tax saving.

Who Is the Ideal Candidate?

Cash balance plans are not right for every business. They require annual actuarial valuations, ERISA compliance, and ongoing contributions regardless of income fluctuations. Before setting one up, a business owner should evaluate several factors.

Age and eligible compensation matter. Older owners may support higher annual funding for a given promised benefit, but age alone cannot establish a contribution. Review compensation history, retirement age, prior service, assets and the plan formula. An owner under 40 may still benefit, with a different funding path.

Cash flow must support required funding. Income above $400,000 or $600,000 can make a review worthwhile, but neither figure is a legal eligibility threshold. A weak year does not cancel the actuarially determined minimum contribution. Model a downside year as well as the expected deduction.

Employee demographics matter. Model owners and all eligible staff, including employees of controlled groups and affiliated service groups under Section 414. Coverage, minimum participation and nondiscrimination rules can require meaningful employee benefits. Older, highly paid staff can change the economics, and adding a related business can change who must be considered.

Self-employed professionals and partners. Physicians, attorneys, dentists, consultants, and partners in professional practices are among the most common adopters of cash balance plans. Many of these individuals are high earners, older, and working in entities with few or no non-owner employees.

How the Tax Deduction Works

Employer cash balance contributions may be deductible under Section 404 within the actuarially determined and applicable combined-plan deduction limits. A contribution paid after year-end but by the employer's extended return deadline can be treated as made for the prior tax year when properly designated. That deduction deadline is separate from the minimum-funding deadline. Defined benefit minimum funding is generally due 8.5 months after the plan year ends, normally September 15 for a calendar-year plan. Quarterly installments may apply. An October 15 extended individual tax-return deadline does not postpone that funding deadline. Contributions paid by the applicable extended employer return deadline may be deductible for the prior tax year when properly designated and within Section 404 limits; the funding and deduction calculations must both be satisfied.

Plan assets generally grow tax-deferred, and distributions of pre-tax benefits generally produce ordinary income unless rolled over in an eligible transaction. Plan-level tax, prohibited-transaction and qualification issues still require review; the trust is not a blanket exemption from every tax rule.

An owner planning a sale should model the pension alongside the transaction. Plan assets generally remain held for participants, but funding shortfalls, promised benefits, controlled-group liabilities and termination costs can affect the deal and cash available at closing. Adoption or termination does not guarantee a sale-year deduction.

The OBBBA and Retirement Planning in 2026

The 2025 legislation made the Section 199A qualified business income deduction permanent, but the general deduction remains up to 20%, not 23%. Eligibility and the final amount depend on taxable income, the taxable-income-minus-net-capital-gain ceiling, specified service business rules, and applicable wage and qualified-property limits. Retirement planning must use the enacted law rather than an earlier proposal.

Qualified-plan contributions attributable to a business can reduce its QBI, which may reduce the Section 199A deduction. The result depends on the owner's full return and thresholds. A $1 retirement deduction therefore need not reduce final taxable income by $1 after the QBI interaction. Model after-tax wealth, liquidity and later distributions, not only the gross contribution.

Plan Design, Setup, and Ongoing Costs

A third-party administrator and enrolled actuary develop the written benefit formula, contribution and interest credits, participant coverage, and funding projection. Most qualified employer plans can be adopted by the employer tax-return due date, including extensions, for the prior tax year. Adoption after year-end does not remove minimum-funding deadlines or create retroactive employee salary deferrals. Obtain a deadline schedule covering adoption, participant notices, elections, deposits and returns.

Annual costs typically include actuary fees ($2,000 to $5,000 per year), third-party administrator fees ($1,500 to $3,000 per year), and plan investment management fees. These planning ranges are not a binding fee quote. On an assumed fully deductible $200,000 contribution, 37% multiplication gives $74,000 before QBI and other tax effects; compare the actual net benefit with recurring fees, staff funding and any PBGC premiums.

The annual return depends on plan type and eligibility. A one-participant plan generally files Form 5500-EZ when combined year-end assets of the employer's one-participant plans exceed $250,000, with a final return generally required on termination regardless of assets. The $250,000 asset figure is not an independent-audit threshold. ERISA audit requirements generally depend on participant-count rules and applicable small-plan waivers, including their asset, bonding and disclosure conditions. The enrolled actuary's valuation and required Schedule SB work are separate; eligible Form 5500-EZ filers generally retain Schedule SB rather than attach it.

Terminating a Cash Balance Plan

A plan can be frozen or terminated, but neither erases already earned benefits or an existing funding shortfall. A freeze generally stops future accruals prospectively and can require advance notice, while actuarial work, funding and reporting continue. Termination requires benefit calculations, required notices and distributions, and may require additional employer cash. PBGC-covered plans must follow the applicable PBGC termination procedures; determine coverage rather than assuming it.

Coordinate a proposed sale or retirement with the actuary before agreeing to termination. Eligible lump-sum distributions may be rolled over to an IRA or another accepting plan, but not every payment is rollover-eligible and benefit rights must be satisfied. A pension is intended to be an ongoing program; there is no automatic three-year or five-year safe harbor for a short-lived tax strategy.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

A major risk is adopting a cash balance plan without a sustainable funding projection. Unlike discretionary 401(k) profit sharing, defined benefit plans have minimum-funding rules; a 401(k) may also have required safe harbor or other promised contributions. Missing required pension funding can create excise tax exposure under Section 4971. Treat the actuary's funding calendar as a cash obligation.

The second mistake is ignoring employee costs. If a business adds employees after establishing the plan, the required contributions for those employees can escalate quickly. The plan design should anticipate workforce changes, particularly in professional practices that hire associates and staff over time.

A combination plan must satisfy the applicable coverage, minimum participation, nondiscrimination and deduction rules, using aggregation only where permitted. Do not assume a 6% employer contribution is always the limit or that a small professional practice can choose PBGC coverage. The professional-service exception requires a qualifying employer and a plan that has never covered more than 25 active participants; a separate exception can apply to a plan covering only substantial owners. Confirm the actual facts with the administrator.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can a business owner contribute to a cash balance plan in 2026?

There is no fixed age-based contribution allowance. An enrolled actuary calculates minimum required funding and maximum deductible contributions using eligible compensation, benefits, assets, age and plan design. The 2026 $290,000 Section 415(b) dollar limit is an annual-benefit ceiling, not a contribution limit.

Can I have both a 401(k) and a cash balance plan?

Yes, if the design satisfies applicable coverage, benefit and deduction rules. The Section 404(a)(7) combined deduction calculation may apply, and 6% is not a universal cap. Contributions exceeding $300,000 are possible in some designs but must be validated; Roth amounts are not current deductions.

Who is the ideal candidate for a cash balance plan?

An owner with sustainable cash flow, eligible compensation and a willingness to fund an ongoing pension may benefit. Age, employee census, related employers, existing assets, administration and exit plans determine the result. Income above $400,000 and age over 45 are planning considerations, not legal eligibility tests.

Are cash balance plan contributions tax deductible?

Employer contributions may be deductible within Section 404 and actuarial limits. Tax-year designation, payment timing, combined-plan rules and the QBI interaction affect the final deduction and taxable income. The extended tax-return deadline does not postpone the separate minimum-funding deadline.

What happens to the money in a cash balance plan when I retire or sell my business?

Accrued benefits and funding obligations remain until properly settled. Termination requires actuarial calculations, required notices, applicable PBGC procedures and distributions, and can require additional employer funding. An eligible lump sum may be rolled over, but a business sale does not automatically terminate the plan or make every payment rollover-eligible.

How does a cash balance plan differ from a traditional defined benefit plan?

Both are defined benefit plans, but a cash balance plan uses a hypothetical account balance model rather than a formula tied to salary and years of service. Each participant has a notional account that grows by a contribution credit plus an interest credit, making cash balance plans more portable and easier to understand.
