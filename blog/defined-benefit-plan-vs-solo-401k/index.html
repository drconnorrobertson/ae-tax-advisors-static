Defined Benefit Plan vs. Solo 401(k): Which Retirement Plan Is Best for Business Owners?

Published on September 6, 2026 | Retirement Planning | AE Tax Advisors

For self-employed business owners and high-income professionals, choosing the right retirement plan is one of the most consequential tax decisions you can make. The difference between a basic retirement vehicle and an optimized plan structure can mean tens of thousands of dollars in annual tax savings, compounding over decades into a significantly larger retirement balance.

A solo 401(k) and a defined benefit plan solve different problems. The first can suit an owner-only business that needs contribution flexibility; the second is a pension commitment that may support larger deductions after actuarial review. A business with eligible employees needs a plan design that covers them, not an owner-only plan that excludes them. Compare cash demands, employee costs, and exit requirements before comparing headline contribution amounts.

Solo 401(k): The Flexible Foundation

A solo 401(k), also called an individual or one-participant 401(k), generally covers an owner with no eligible common-law employees, or the owner and spouse. Check employee eligibility, including applicable long-term part-time rules and related businesses, before using an owner-only arrangement. It offers two contribution components:

Employee deferrals: Up to $24,500 for 2026, limited by eligible compensation and coordinated with elective deferrals to other plans. This is a per-person limit, not a fresh allowance for every business. IRS Notice 2025-67 supplies the 2026 dollar limits.

Employer profit-sharing contributions: Generally up to 25% of eligible W-2 compensation for a corporate owner. A self-employed sole proprietor uses a special calculation, generally equivalent to 20% of adjusted net earnings after the deductible part of self-employment tax when the stated plan contribution rate is 25%. Do not multiply raw Schedule C profit by 25%. Use the IRS worksheet and coordinate other plans and deduction limits.

Total combined limit: Annual additions are limited to the lesser of $72,000 or 100% of eligible compensation in 2026, excluding qualifying catch-up contributions. Employer contributions and employee contributions, including any permitted after-tax contributions, share this ceiling. The annual compensation limit for plan calculations is $360,000.

Catch-up contributions: If the plan permits, participants age 50 or older can add $8,000 in 2026, bringing the potential total to $80,000. Those who attain ages 60, 61, 62, or 63 in 2026 may instead use an $11,250 catch-up, for a potential $83,250 total. The enhanced amount replaces the usual catch-up; the two are not added together.

Roth option: Roth deferrals are after-tax; qualified distributions can be tax-free. In 2026, a participant whose prior-year FICA wages from the employer exceeded $150,000 is generally required to make catch-up contributions as Roth contributions. Confirm the plan and payroll implementation. A sole proprietor without FICA wages from the business is not treated the same as an S-Corp owner with W-2 wages, and Roth amounts are not current deductions.

A solo 401(k) can have low administration costs and flexible discretionary contributions. The Form 5500-EZ asset exemption generally applies when the employer's one-participant plans have combined year-end assets of $250,000 or less; a final-year return is still required. Check the applicable filing instructions, plan terms, and deadlines rather than assuming there is never paperwork below that balance.

Defined Benefit Plan: The High-Deduction Powerhouse

A defined benefit plan is a traditional pension-style retirement plan that promises a specific annual benefit at retirement age. Instead of defining how much you contribute each year (as with a 401(k)), a defined benefit plan defines how much you will receive each year in retirement, and then calculates backward to determine the contributions needed to fund that benefit.

Maximum annual benefit: The 2026 section 415(b) dollar ceiling is $290,000 for an annual retirement benefit, not an annual contribution allowance. The applicable limit also depends on compensation, benefit form, retirement age, and service or participation adjustments. An actuary determines the permitted benefit and required funding for the actual participant.

Annual contribution range: Funding depends on age, retirement date, compensation history, existing assets, investment assumptions, and the benefit formula. A proposed $100,000 to $250,000 or larger contribution needs an individualized actuarial calculation; there is no universal “maximum contribution” in that range.

Age-based advantage: The closer you are to retirement, the larger the required annual contribution, because there are fewer years for the money to grow. This makes defined benefit plans especially powerful for business owners age 45 and older who want to shelter large amounts of income.

Mandatory funding: Unlike a 401(k) where contributions are discretionary, defined benefit plans require minimum annual contributions based on the actuarial funding schedule. This commitment is an important consideration, as income must be sufficient and consistent enough to support the required contributions.

Cash Balance Plans: The Modern Defined Benefit

A cash balance plan is a defined benefit plan with a hypothetical account for each participant. Its pay-credit and interest-credit formulas determine promised benefits; those credits are not the same as the return actually earned by plan investments. The employer bears funding risk, and the actuary recalculates contributions as assets and liabilities change.

From a contribution and deduction standpoint, cash balance plans offer the same high limits as traditional defined benefit plans. The key advantages of the cash balance structure include:

Portability: When the plan terminates or a participant leaves, the balance can be rolled over to an IRA, making it easier to access and manage.

Transparency: Participants can see a clear account balance rather than a projected future pension amount, making the plan easier to understand and track.

Flexibility in design: Actuaries can design cash balance plans with varying pay credit formulas to match the business owner's income and deduction goals.

Combining Plans: The Maximum Deduction Strategy

A business can sponsor both a 401(k) and a defined benefit or cash balance plan, but the maximums cannot simply be added together. Section 415 contribution and benefit limits, section 404 deduction limits, employee coverage, related-employer rules, and PBGC coverage must be checked together. For overlapping owner-only plans exempt from PBGC coverage, keeping employer defined-contribution amounts at or below 6% of compensation can avoid the additional combined deduction limit; employee elective deferrals are excluded from that 6% test. See the IRS combined-plan deduction explanation.

Here is a hypothetical 2026 funding budget, not a client result or contribution recommendation. Assume an owner age 52 has $200,000 of eligible W-2 compensation, also had more than $150,000 of FICA wages from this employer in 2025, has no other eligible employees or plans, and has sufficient business income and cash. Assume a PBGC-exempt pension and that an actuary separately supports a $175,000 pension contribution:

401(k) before catch-up: $24,500 pre-tax employee deferral + $12,000 employer contribution (6% of $200,000) = $36,500.

Defined benefit contribution: $175,000, assumed to be supported by the actuary and within the applicable deduction limit.

Current pre-tax/deductible amounts: $36,500 + $175,000 = $211,500. An additional $8,000 Roth catch-up produces $219,500 of total contributions but does not increase the current income-tax reduction.

If every dollar of the assumed $211,500 pre-tax/deductible amount offsets income otherwise taxed at 35%, the rate-only tax effect is $74,025. Actual savings require a full return projection, including QBI effects, brackets, state rules, and deduction limits. The $219,500 cash commitment, ongoing fees, future funding requirements, and tax on later traditional-plan distributions still matter. Do not project the same contribution or savings for ten years without updating the plan valuation.

Income Thresholds and Practical Considerations

Retirement plan selection should be based on several factors beyond contribution limits:

Income consistency: Defined benefit plans require minimum annual contributions. If your income fluctuates significantly from year to year, the mandatory contribution in a down year could create cash flow pressure. Business owners with variable income should consider this carefully and may want to set the target benefit conservatively.

Number of employees: Both solo 401(k) plans and defined benefit plans work best for businesses with no employees (or only a spouse as an employee). Adding employees to a defined benefit plan can significantly increase costs, because contributions may be required for eligible employees under the plan's nondiscrimination testing.

Time horizon: Defined benefit plans are most advantageous when maintained for at least 5 to 10 years. Short-term plans (less than three years) may raise IRS scrutiny and reduce the actuarial benefit.

Exit strategy: Plan termination requires a coordinated review of funding, vesting, distributions, participant notices, and final reporting. PBGC procedures may apply to a covered plan. Form 5310 is an optional request for an IRS determination in applicable cases, not a universal termination filing. Build termination costs and timing into the decision with your actuary and administrator.

Choosing the Right Plan for Your Situation

If your net self-employment income is under $300,000 and you want simplicity and flexibility, a solo 401(k) is likely the right starting point. It provides meaningful tax deductions with minimal administrative burden.

If income consistently exceeds $300,000 and you need more retirement funding capacity, ask for a combined-plan feasibility study rather than assuming a pension will pay for itself. Require a multi-year funding range, employee cost estimate, QBI-adjusted tax projection, and a plan for a lower-income year before adopting it.

If you are age 50 or older with high income and want to accelerate retirement savings, the defined benefit plan becomes especially attractive because the actuarial calculations allow larger annual contributions for older participants.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the maximum contribution to a solo 401(k) in 2026?

The 2026 employee deferral limit is $24,500. Annual additions are limited to the lesser of $72,000 or 100% of eligible compensation, excluding catch-up. If permitted and supported by compensation, an $8,000 age-50 catch-up permits a total of $80,000; ages 60 through 63 may instead use $11,250, for $83,250. Corporate employer contributions generally use up to 25% of eligible W-2 compensation; sole proprietors need the adjusted self-employment calculation. Other plans, deduction limits, and required Roth catch-up treatment can reduce the available pre-tax amount.

How much can I contribute to a defined benefit plan?

An actuary must calculate funding and the deductible amount for your plan. The $290,000 section 415(b) limit for 2026 is an annual benefit ceiling, not a contribution allowance, and compensation, retirement age, benefit form, and service adjustments can reduce it. Age alone does not establish a deductible contribution.

Can I have both a defined benefit plan and a solo 401(k)?

Yes, if the business and participants qualify. Coordinate benefit and annual-additions limits, employee coverage, related businesses, PBGC status, and the combined deduction rules under section 404. For overlapping PBGC-exempt plans, the employer 401(k) contribution can affect the deductible pension amount. An actuary and administrator should model both plans together before funding; their headline maximums cannot simply be added.

What is a cash balance plan and how does it differ from a traditional defined benefit plan?

A cash balance plan is a defined benefit pension that expresses benefits as a hypothetical account with pay and interest credits. Those plan credits are not the actual investment return. It still requires actuarial funding, benefit-limit and coverage testing, and ongoing employer commitments. Eligible distributions may be rolled over, subject to the plan and rollover rules.

At what income level does a defined benefit plan make sense?

Consistent income around $300,000 or more can justify investigating a pension, but it is not an eligibility threshold or a guarantee of savings. Compare the proposed deduction after QBI effects and tax brackets with cash funding, employee costs, and the article's $2,000 to $5,000 annual administration estimate. Obtain a written quote and actuarial funding range before committing.

What are the administration costs for each plan type?

This guide uses planning estimates of $0 to $500 per year for a solo 401(k) and $2,000 to $5,000 per year for a defined benefit plan; obtain a provider-specific quote. Pension work includes actuarial calculations, required annual reporting, and PBGC premiums if covered. For owner-only plans, check the aggregate $250,000 asset exemption and mandatory final-year Form 5500-EZ filing rather than assuming low assets eliminate every filing.

What happens if my income drops after I set up a defined benefit plan?

Defined benefit plans require minimum annual contributions based on the actuarial funding schedule. If your income drops significantly, the required contribution can become a financial burden. This is why income consistency is an important factor in deciding whether a defined benefit plan is appropriate. Plans can be amended or frozen if circumstances change, but there may be penalties or restrictions. Discuss contingency planning with your advisor before establishing the plan.
