My S corporation is repaying a shareholder loan after losses reduced its basis. Will I owe tax?

Published September 22, 2026 | Business Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Yes, part of the principal repayment can be taxable if pass-through losses previously reduced your basis in a bona fide loan to the S corporation and basis has not been restored. The taxable amount and its character depend on the debt-basis computation and whether the debt is evidenced by a written note or is open-account debt. A corporate bookkeeping entry called “loan repayment” does not settle either question.

Before the corporation sends the payment, have the note, shareholder advances, K-1s, and prior basis schedules reconciled. Book a Return Review Call to map the repayment-year Form 7203 and owner return before cash moves.

Three gates before treating the principal as tax-free

  1. Is there bona fide debt directly from the corporation to you? A shareholder guarantee of the corporation's bank loan alone does not create shareholder debt basis. The IRS stock-and-debt-basis guidance and Form 7203 instructions require an actual qualifying loan or payment under a guarantee.
  2. What is the basis of each debt immediately before repayment? Stock basis and debt basis are separate. Prior allowed S-corporation losses can reduce debt basis after stock basis is exhausted. A later net increase can restore reduced debt basis, with a special ordering rule favoring debt repaid in the current year before remaining restoration is allocated elsewhere.
  3. What exactly is being paid? Split principal from stated or imputed interest, distributions, compensation, and other transfers. A repayment of debt with full basis generally returns principal without gain; repayment of reduced-basis debt can recognize gain. Interest has separate reporting even if principal is fully sheltered.

Worked example: losses reduced a $100,000 note to $40,000 of basis

Suppose an owner advanced $100,000 on a bona fide written promissory note. Prior S-corporation losses used $60,000 of debt basis, leaving $40,000, and no current-year income restores it. If the corporation repays the entire $100,000 principal, the simplified gain is $60,000: cash principal received less the note's remaining basis. Stated interest is calculated and reported separately. The IRS Form 7203 instructions describe gain from repayment of reduced-basis debt and direct written-note gain to Form 8949 and Schedule D, while open-account gain is reported on Form 4797. Verify holding period, debt status, and all other facts before assigning character.

Now assume the repayment-year basis workpaper shows a $30,000 net increase that qualifies to restore this note before repayment. Basis could rise to $70,000 and simplified principal gain fall to $30,000. Do not count corporate gross revenue, a new shareholder contribution, or gain on the loan repayment itself as automatic restoration; calculate the statutory net increase and ordering on Form 7203. A partial repayment or multiple notes requires a separate allocation rather than using this full-payoff shortcut.

Written note versus open-account debt changes the return path

A written instrument must be tracked separately on Form 7203; informal open-account advances and repayments generally net within the tax year. The IRS instructions say written-note repayment gain is capital and goes to Form 8949 and Schedule D, while open-account repayment gain is ordinary and goes to Form 4797. They also contain a special rule when an open-account balance exceeded $25,000 at the prior year-end; do not infer the gain's character merely from the balance-sheet label or the threshold. Review the actual instrument, dates, and current instructions before filing.

The shareholder, not just the corporation, must maintain the basis record. The IRS lists receipt of an S-corporation loan repayment among Form 7203 filing triggers. Form 7203 also distinguishes basis-limited loss carryovers from at-risk, passive-loss, and excess-business-loss limits. One loan payment does not automatically release a loss suspended under a different rule.

What to gather before paying or amending

  • Each original note and amendment, advance and repayment bank record, interest ledger, and corporation general-ledger entries.
  • Prior Forms 1120-S, K-1s, Form 7203, and any shareholder stock- and debt-basis schedules from the first advance onward.
  • Loss carryforward workpapers, including Forms 6198 and 8582 where relevant, plus records of contributions and distributions.
  • Current-year projected K-1 income, deductions, and tax-exempt items that may affect debt-basis restoration.
  • The proposed payment date and amount, whether it retires a particular written note, and the planned stock sale or business exit if one is pending.

Common errors that change the tax bill

  • Calling every principal repayment tax-free because the corporation originally received cash.
  • Using stock basis to shelter a repayment of a different debt, or aggregating separate written notes.
  • Reporting every reduced-basis repayment as ordinary income; a formal written note can produce capital gain under the Form 7203 instructions.
  • Assuming a guaranteed bank loan created basis without an actual shareholder payment.
  • Booking a distribution as debt repayment after the year ends without a bona fide debt trail.
  • Ignoring state treatment, interest reporting, or a prior-year basis error that may require a coordinated return correction.

If the basis file is incomplete or repayment is part of an exit, Book a Return Review Call. AE can reconcile each note, current-year restoration, character, and the filing package before the transaction is reported.

The tax treatment

Return of bona fide principal is generally not income, while stated or imputed interest is income to the lender; debt basis and prior losses can complicate repayment. This is a federal income tax starting point; the contract, ownership, accounting method, and actual use can change the result. State and local treatment should be checked separately.

The decision to make before filing

Reconcile principal, accrued interest, and any debt basis reduced by prior deductions before issuing payment. The useful planning step is to resolve the classification while the underlying documents are still available, then reconcile it to the books and the prior-year return. If the transaction spans more than one year, track the opening balance and what happened to it afterward.

Illustrative example

An S corporation repays part of a shareholder's documented loan. The bookkeeper separates principal and interest and checks whether prior pass-through losses reduced debt basis. The shareholder's tax treatment may differ from a simple return of untouched principal.

Records that support the position

Keep original note, loan roll-forward, shareholder basis schedule, and payment detail. Tie amounts on the return to bank activity and the agreement. When several assets, people, or uses are involved, write down the allocation method and apply it consistently. A short dated workpaper is easier to defend than a reconstructed explanation years later.

A common reporting error

Calling a distribution loan repayment after the fact can misstate both income and basis. Review both sides of the entry: a payment can affect income, basis, liability, or an expense at different times. A correct cash total alone does not establish the correct tax character.

Where to verify the rule

Start with the IRS Form 7203 instructions and IRS S-corporation stock and debt basis guidance. Their debt-basis, restoration, and repayment rules are more directly relevant than a generic small-business publication. Check the current version for the return year at issue.
