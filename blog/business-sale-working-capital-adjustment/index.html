The Buyer Cut Our Business Sale Price After Closing. Do We Amend for the Working-Capital True-Up?

Published September 22, 2026 | Updated September 25, 2026 | Business Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Direct answer: A post-closing working-capital shortfall does not automatically mean “amend the sale-year return,” and it is not automatically an ordinary expense. First determine whether the signed agreement really reduced purchase price, when the adjustment became fixed for tax purposes, whether the original return was correct when filed, and whether the transaction was a plain stock sale, an asset sale, or a stock sale with a deemed-asset election. A later-year adjustment can require supplemental allocation reporting instead of—or in addition to—a correction of an earlier return. The seller and buyer should reconcile their positions before either files.

First decide what the payment actually adjusts

A normal working-capital clause compares closing-date current assets and liabilities with an agreed target. The true-up may increase or decrease the amount paid for stock or assets. It can also be tangled with a separate indemnity, seller-period expense reimbursement, employment payment, or escrow release. Labeling every later payment a “true-up” in the ledger does not settle its tax character. Read the contractual formula, dispute resolution, final balance sheet, and payment direction together.

When the true-up includes unpaid customer invoices from a cash-method business, determine whether the buyer acquired those receivables or the seller retained them before analyzing the price change. AE's cash-basis receivables sale guide addresses their ordinary-income character and invoice-level allocation; it is a different question from whether a later settlement changes the sale price.

For example, a seller's $300,000 refund under a formula reducing stock purchase price is not automatically a $300,000 business deduction. Conversely, a separately negotiated consulting fee does not become stock-sale proceeds merely because it is paid near closing. The return workpaper should show both the legal reason for the payment and the cash trail.

Which return or supplemental form is affected?

  1. Plain stock sale: Reconcile the shareholder's amount realized, selling costs, adjusted stock basis, gain, and any deferred or contingent consideration. A pure stock sale does not by itself call for asset-acquisition Form 8594 or Section 338 Form 8883. Whether the seller corrects a filed sale-year return or reports a later event under the applicable method depends on when the right or obligation became fixed and what was originally reported.
  2. Applicable asset acquisition: Reconcile the change in total consideration and the residual-method allocation with the buyer. The IRS Form 8594 instructions say to complete a supplemental Part III and file a new Form 8594 for each year an increase or decrease in consideration occurs. An asset-price change need not reduce goodwill dollar-for-dollar; the allocation rules and asset values must be applied.
  3. Stock sale with a Section 338 election: Recompute the seller's aggregate deemed sales price and the buyer's basis allocation as applicable. The IRS Form 8883 instructions require a supplemental statement for each year an increase or decrease in ADSP or AGUB occurs. See AE's Section 338(h)(10) seller-price guide for the election and shareholder-basis decision.
  4. Installment obligation or contingent price: Separate principal from interest and review the contract-price and gross-profit calculations. IRS Publication 537 explains that a later reduction in selling price can require recomputing the gross-profit percentage on remaining installment payments; it does not simply become a current operating expense.

Worked example: the economic change is not the filing answer

Illustration only: Owners sell S-corporation stock for an estimated $12 million. The agreement targets $1.8 million of closing working capital; the final statement supports $1.5 million. If the signed formula produces a $300,000 downward purchase-price adjustment, final economic stock consideration is $11.7 million. For a shareholder with $2 million of adjusted stock basis and no other changes, a simple pre-tax gain comparison moves from $10 million to $9.7 million. That arithmetic does not decide whether a previously filed return is amended, what year recognizes the adjustment, or how any escrow or seller note is treated.

Suppose instead the buyer acquired assets. The same $300,000 reduction also requires reviewing the asset allocation and the parties' Form 8594 supplemental reporting. If the deal made a Section 338 election, examine Form 8883 and the shareholder-level effects. If some price remains payable on a note, model the revised installment gross-profit percentage. These paths are why the settlement agreement and both tax returns matter more than the general ledger label.

Before filing or amending, make a five-document reconciliation

  • Signed purchase agreement, working-capital definition and target, closing estimate, and all amendments.
  • Final closing-date balance sheet, buyer's calculation, seller's objections, and signed settlement or arbitration result.
  • Closing statement, escrow ledger, wire and refund records, seller note, and any separately paid services or indemnity claims.
  • Original sale-year gain and basis schedules, entity and shareholder returns, and any Forms 8594, 8883, or 6252 already filed.
  • Buyer-side allocation and basis schedules, relevant state returns, and a dated explanation of when the adjustment was fixed and taken into account.

Errors that cause avoidable multi-year corrections

  • Automatically amending: a later contingent price adjustment is not the same as an error that existed on the original filed return. Determine the applicable tax year first.
  • Calling a price refund a business expense: it can distort ordinary income, stock gain, asset allocation, and buyer basis.
  • Using the wrong supplemental form: Form 8594 is for an applicable asset acquisition; Form 8883 is for a Section 338 deemed-asset transaction. A plain stock sale is a different reporting path.
  • Ignoring remaining note payments: a reduced sale price can change future installment gain percentages, while interest and ineligible assets follow their own rules.
  • Letting buyer and seller report different prices: reconcile the final settlement, allocations, and state positions before filing.

This is a federal decision framework, not a conclusion for every agreement. Accounting method, return status, purchase-price allocation, earnouts, claims, and state law can change the result.
