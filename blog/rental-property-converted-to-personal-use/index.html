I'm moving into my rental property. What happens to depreciation and tax when I sell?

Published September 22, 2026 | Updated September 24, 2026 | Real Estate Tax | AE Tax Advisors

Moving into a rental does not itself cause a sale or erase prior depreciation. Once you permanently withdraw the property from income-producing use, rental depreciation stops and personal-period costs generally stop being Schedule E deductions. If you later sell after using it as your main home, the Section 121 exclusion may help, but post-1997 rental depreciation and gain allocated to certain earlier nonqualified-use periods remain taxable.

Before you move in or list the property, have the rental history, depreciation schedule, and proposed sale date reviewed together.

First decision: is the rental truly converted, or just temporarily vacant?

A tenant's departure alone does not end rental use. A home being repaired between tenants or genuinely held ready and available for another tenant can remain in service. By contrast, a documented decision to withdraw it permanently from the rental market and occupy it personally is a conversion. IRS Publication 527 explicitly treats conversion to personal use as retirement from service. Record the last lease date, listing withdrawal, renovation purpose, and actual move-in date; do not choose a date merely to maximize a deduction.

In the conversion year, reconcile rent, deposits, utilities, insurance, repairs, taxes, and interest to the periods and uses they actually relate to. Personal-period household costs are not rental operating expenses. Some mortgage interest or property taxes may instead qualify as itemized deductions, subject to their own rules and limits; improvements may affect basis rather than a current deduction. A cost-segregation schedule also has to stop depreciating the building and each shorter-lived asset no longer used for income production. See Schedule E instructions and Publication 946 for reporting and depreciation conventions.

Second decision: what happens to past depreciation and suspended losses?

Keep every prior-year Form 4562 and asset schedule. Depreciation that was allowed or allowable during rental use reduces adjusted basis even if a prior return omitted it; living in the house later does not restore that basis. If an old depreciation schedule is wrong, resolve the correction method before computing sale gain rather than inserting an unsupported one-year catch-up. AE's rental-sale depreciation guide explains the disposition side.

Moving into the home is not a taxable disposition that automatically releases suspended passive rental losses. Track Form 8582 carryforwards separately. They may be usable against applicable passive income, while a full release generally requires a qualifying disposition of the entire activity to an unrelated person in which all gain or loss is recognized. Grouped activities and other limitations can change that analysis; IRS Publication 925 explains the disposition rule. Do not write off the carryforward or claim it all merely because Schedule E rental use ends.

Third decision: would living there for two years make the sale tax-free?

Not necessarily. The basic Section 121 home-sale exclusion generally requires ownership and main-home use for at least two years during the five years before sale, plus other eligibility conditions. Post-May 6, 1997 rental depreciation allowed or allowable is not excludable. Moreover, rental use after 2008 before the last day the property is used as a main home can produce nonqualified-use gain that is not excludable. The rule differs for a rental period after the owner's last main-home use within the five-year window; do not reverse those timelines. Prior like-kind exchanges, separate rental portions, and state treatment warrant their own review.

Illustration, not a tax estimate: An owner buys a rental for $400,000, rents it for three years, then occupies the entire property as a main home for two years before selling for $700,000. Assume $20,000 of allowable rental depreciation, no selling costs or other basis changes, and that the ownership-and-use tests are met. Adjusted basis is $380,000 and gain is $320,000. The $20,000 depreciation component cannot be excluded. Of the remaining $300,000, a three-of-five-year rental period before main-home use could allocate $180,000 to nonqualified use, leaving $120,000 potentially eligible for exclusion (subject to the applicable limit and all other facts). Simply saying “I lived there two years” would miss both taxable components. This simplified arithmetic follows the method and fact pattern illustrated in IRS Publication 523; actual day counts, basis, and character can differ.

Documents to gather before the move or sale

  • Purchase and refinance closing statements, improvement invoices, and any cost-segregation study or Form 3115 workpapers.
  • Every rental-year Schedule E, Form 4562, depreciation asset ledger, and Form 8582 suspended-loss schedule.
  • Leases, rent ledgers, listing and withdrawal dates, photographs, utilities, and records establishing the first day the home became your main residence.
  • A proposed sale worksheet with price, selling costs, adjusted basis, depreciation, ownership/use days, and any prior Section 121 or Section 1031 transaction.

Common failure points: continuing rental depreciation after permanent personal conversion; stopping it too early during a temporary vacancy; treating a move-in as a passive-loss release; or assuming Section 121 shelters depreciation and pre-residence rental gain. If instead you are turning your home into a rental, use AE's opposite-direction conversion guide—the starting depreciation basis and timing questions are different.

This is a federal framework, not an individualized filing position. Ownership through an entity, mixed personal and rental use, prior exchanges, cost-segregated assets, and state tax can change the answer. Review the actual return history and sale timeline before filing or signing a sale contract.
