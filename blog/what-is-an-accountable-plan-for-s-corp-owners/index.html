What Is an Accountable Plan and How Does It Work for S-Corp Owners?

An accountable plan is an employee-expense reimbursement arrangement that can let an S corporation reimburse its shareholder-employees and other employees for qualifying business expenses without treating the supported payments as wages. Section 62(c) and Treasury Regulation 1.62-2 require business connection, timely substantiation, and return of excess. The corporation's deduction still depends on the underlying expense and its applicable limits; for example, qualifying business meals are not automatically 100% deductible.

The Three Requirements of an Accountable Plan

The arrangement must require expenses connected to employee services, adequate accounting within a reasonable period, and return of excess within a reasonable period. IRS Publication 463 gives safe-harbor examples of substantiating within 60 days and returning excess within 120 days, but reasonableness otherwise depends on the facts. A mileage allowance still needs dates, place, and business purpose under the applicable method.

Payments under a nonaccountable arrangement are generally included in W-2 wages and subject to applicable withholding and employment taxes. If the arrangement qualifies but a particular allowance has an unreturned excess, the unsupported excess may be wages while the properly substantiated portion remains excluded. The distinction matters before correcting payroll or the S-corporation return. If W-2s were already filed, use the owner-allowance correction guide rather than treating a new plan as a retroactive cure.

Why S-Corp Owners Need an Accountable Plan

S-corporation shareholder-employees face a different reporting path from sole proprietors who deduct qualifying business expenses on Schedule C. Most employees cannot simply move unreimbursed mileage or travel onto their personal return; IRS Publication 463 identifies limited Form 2106 categories. The corporation and owner should evaluate the actual expense, reimbursement history, and tax year rather than assuming a personal deduction will repair a missing corporate process.

An accountable plan solves this problem by shifting the deduction to the S-Corp. The corporation reimburses the shareholder-employee, deducts the reimbursement as a business expense, and the shareholder-employee receives the funds tax-free. The net effect is the same as if the shareholder had deducted the expense directly, but the mechanism is through the corporate entity rather than the personal return.

Common Expenses Covered by an Accountable Plan

An accountable arrangement may cover properly substantiated business use of a personal vehicle, travel, certain meals, training, technology, and other ordinary business costs. Each category has its own substantiation and deduction limits. Use the mileage rate for the payment year rather than an outdated rate, and review home-office reimbursement using actual corporate and employee facts rather than assuming an individual's simplified home-office method automatically applies to the corporation.

How to Establish an Accountable Plan

A written policy and corporate approval are strong evidence of a consistent reimbursement arrangement, but the federal test is whether the arrangement actually requires and follows the three rules; no particular filed IRS form or magic document title creates an accountable plan. State which expenses qualify, what substantiation and timing are required, and how excess advances are returned. Keep board records and reports so the company can demonstrate how the policy operated.

Once the plan is adopted, the S-Corp should implement an expense reporting system. Each reimbursement request should include the date, amount, business purpose, and supporting documentation for every expense. The corporation reviews the submission, approves it, and issues payment from the business account. The reimbursement is recorded as an operating expense on the S-Corp's books, not as wages or officer compensation.

Accountable Plan vs. Increased Salary

Paying more salary to cover an employee's business costs can create payroll-tax expense that a qualifying accountable reimbursement would not. But compensation must still be reasonable, Social Security has an annual wage base, Medicare and Additional Medicare Tax have different rules, and salary can affect QBI, retirement benefits, and state taxes. The combined Social Security/Medicare rate must not be added to Medicare a second time; no fixed payroll-tax saving follows from a $20,000 reimbursement example. See the owner-salary and QBI tradeoff before changing pay.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an accountable plan?

An arrangement under Treasury Regulation 1.62-2 through which a business reimburses employees, including owner-employees, for business expenses without the reimbursement being treated as taxable wages. It requires a business connection, substantiation within a reasonable period, and return of excess amounts.

How does the Augusta Rule work?

IRC Section 280A(g) allows a taxpayer to rent a personal residence for 14 days or fewer per year without including the rental income in gross income. A business can deduct the rent if it is reasonable, documented with a rental agreement and a genuine business purpose, and supported by comparable rates.

Can I deduct a home office?

Yes, where a portion of the home is used regularly and exclusively as the principal place of business or for meeting clients. For owner-employees of an S corporation, reimbursement through an accountable plan is generally preferable to claiming the deduction directly.

Are business meals deductible?

Business meals with a clear business purpose are generally 50% deductible when not lavish and when the taxpayer or an employee is present. Entertainment remains nondeductible. Certain employee-related meals have different treatment, so the category matters.
