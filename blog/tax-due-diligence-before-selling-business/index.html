Tax Due Diligence Before Selling a Business: What Buyers Will Test

Published on September 17, 2026 | Exit Planning | AE Tax Advisors Editorial Team

A buyer's tax diligence is designed to find liabilities that survive closing, reduce price, or justify a larger escrow. Sellers who wait for the data-room request lose leverage because the buyer controls the timetable and frames every unresolved item as risk.

A focused sell-side tax review 12 to 24 months before a transaction can identify exposure, preserve elections, and improve the quality of earnings that supports valuation.

Entity History and Ownership

Buyers will reconcile legal formation, tax classification, ownership changes, elections, and returns. For an S corporation, they may test the original election, shareholder eligibility, one-class-of-stock requirement, distributions, and reasonable compensation. For a partnership, they may review capital accounts, Section 704(b) allocations, debt allocations, and Section 754 elections.

Unresolved inconsistencies between operating agreements, cap tables, payroll, state filings, and federal returns should be corrected before diligence begins. A qualification failure discovered during negotiations can change the transaction structure or delay closing.

Payroll and Worker Classification

Diligence commonly tests owner compensation, independent contractors, employment-tax deposits, deferred compensation, bonuses, fringe benefits, and state payroll registrations. For a corporate target, review whether management deal bonuses create a Section 280G issue only after checking the applicable company-level exemptions. Repeated Form 1099 payments to workers managed like employees can create federal and state exposure plus benefit-plan risk.

Owners should reconcile Forms W-2, 1099, 941, and state filings to the books. Document classification decisions and correct material errors before the buyer calculates a worst-case assessment. For the purchaser's different question about old payroll-tax arrears, see our buyer-side Form 941 asset-deal diligence guide on entity debt, liens, successor exposure, and closing records.

Sales Tax, Income Tax Nexus, and Unclaimed Property

A growing business may create filing obligations through employees, inventory, customers, economic activity, or acquisitions. Buyers often focus on sales tax because liability can attach to the business and collection may have been possible from customers at the time of sale.

Build a state-by-state footprint showing revenue, payroll, property, remote workers, and filing history. Evaluate voluntary disclosure or prospective registration before the issue becomes a purchase-price holdback.

Tax Attributes and Basis

Net operating losses, credits, disallowed interest, suspended passive losses, fixed-asset basis, and research-credit documentation all affect value. The buyer will test whether attributes survive a change in ownership and whether deductions were properly supported.

For sellers, stock basis determines gain in a stock sale. Asset basis and depreciation methods affect ordinary-income recapture in an asset sale. A clean tax-basis schedule should be available before negotiating the purchase-price allocation.

Transaction-Specific Modeling

Model a stock sale, asset sale, and any deemed-asset election. Separate ordinary income, capital gain, depreciation recapture, installment treatment, earnouts, rollover equity, noncompete payments, and compensation. The headline price is not the seller's after-tax proceeds.

Review letters of intent for tax language before signing. Exclusivity, rollover percentages, working-capital true-ups and their later return effect, and allocation principles can restrict later planning even when the letter is described as nonbinding.

Sell-Side Diligence File

Prepare federal, state, local, payroll, and sales-tax returns; election statements; notices; fixed-asset ledgers; basis schedules; credit studies; nexus analysis; employee and contractor data; and a list of open audits or correspondence. Add a written explanation for every unusual item before the buyer asks.

A 90-Day Sell-Side Tax Review

In the first 30 days, assemble returns, notices, elections, ownership records, payroll filings, sales-tax accounts, fixed assets, basis schedules, and credit studies. Reconcile revenue and payroll by state and identify missing filings or inconsistent ownership data.

During days 31 through 60, quantify each exposure under a supportable and a buyer-conservative case. Decide whether to amend, pursue voluntary disclosure, request late-election relief, obtain a tax opinion, or disclose the issue with a proposed escrow. Resolve items that are inexpensive to fix and document why other positions are defensible.

During days 61 through 90, build the data-room index, management explanations, transaction tax model, and draft responses to likely diligence questions. Coordinate the tax narrative with quality-of-earnings work so revenue, add-backs, owner compensation, and working-capital positions do not conflict across advisers.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should sell-side tax diligence begin?

Ideally 12 to 24 months before a targeted sale, with a focused refresh when the letter of intent is being negotiated.

What tax issue most often affects price?

There is no single issue, but sales-tax nexus, worker classification, S-corporation qualification, unsupported credits, and unclear basis frequently lead to escrows, indemnities, or price adjustments.

Should the seller agree to an asset sale?

Not without modeling. An asset sale can increase ordinary income and recapture for the seller while giving the buyer a basis step-up. The price and allocation should reflect that difference.
