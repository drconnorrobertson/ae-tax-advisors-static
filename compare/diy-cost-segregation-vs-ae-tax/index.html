Can I Do My Own Cost Segregation Study?

Yes—but the tax deduction depends on the evidence, classifications, cost reconciliation, and return implementation, not on whether software generated a report.

Book a Return Review Call

By AE Tax Advisors Team · Updated 2026-09-24

The Short Version

Yes, you can prepare your own cost segregation study. The IRS does not prescribe a license, credential, or standard report format for a preparer. But you—not the software—must substantiate the building basis, identify and classify each asset, support unit costs, reconcile the allocation to actual total cost, explain the legal authority, and place the result correctly on the tax return.

The IRS Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide describes 13 elements of a quality study. It says a preparer's expertise and experience can affect quality, even though no qualification is prescribed. A self-prepared study can be defensible when the taxpayer actually has the construction, estimating, documentation, and tax-classification expertise to satisfy those elements.

DIY software may be economically reasonable for a simple property with strong records and a modest potential benefit. A professional study becomes more compelling as the property, documentation gaps, specialty systems, deduction, prior-year correction, or examination-support needs grow. The decision should come from a break-even analysis—not a generic property-value cutoff.

The 13 IRS Quality Elements Your DIY Study Must Address

The IRS guide defines a quality study as accurate and well documented. It identifies these 13 principal elements; a downloadable report is not enough if the underlying workpapers do not support them.

IRS elementWhat your workpaper file should show
1. Preparer expertise and experienceWho performed the work and their construction, estimating, allocation, and tax-classification experience.
2. Detailed methodologyThe approach used and each step taken to identify, classify, and cost assets.
3. Appropriate documentationClosing records, plans, invoices, contracts, appraisals, photographs, and other best-available evidence.
4. Appropriate interviewsNotes from owners, contractors, architects, facility personnel, or others who know the property and costs.
5–6. Common names and numberingConsistent asset descriptions and a traceable numbering system linking the report, evidence, and depreciation schedule.
7. Legal analysisThe authority and facts supporting each Section 1245 or Section 1250 classification.
8. Unit costs and engineering takeoffsMeasured quantities, cost sources, location and date adjustments, and calculations.
9. Organized asset groupsAsset-level or logical group schedules by recovery period and placed-in-service date.
10. Cost reconciliationA bridge from allocated asset costs back to the taxpayer's total actual depreciable basis.
11. Indirect costsWhich indirect costs were included and the method used to allocate them.
12. Section 1245 listingA specific schedule of personal-property assets rather than a single unsupported percentage.
13. Related tax issuesSection 263A, accounting-method changes, sampling, placed-in-service dates, and other applicable issues.

A field inspection is recommended by the IRS guide for quality studies and is described as critical when drawings and specifications for acquired property are limited. That is more precise than saying every study without an in-person visit is automatically invalid. A remote process can still gather useful evidence, but the file must document the physical details and condition needed to reconstruct costs.

What DIY Cost Segregation Software Does

Self-service platforms generally build an allocation from the facts, photographs, documents, and property data supplied by the user. Pricing and professional review vary by product, so the engagement letter and sample report matter more than the “DIY” label.

For a simple rental with a modest depreciable basis, strong construction records, and a small expected timing benefit, a lower-cost process can make economic sense. But “better than no analysis” is not the filing standard. An unsupported allocation may be worse than retaining the building classification until adequate support exists.

The IRS guide describes six common approaches. For new construction, detailed engineering using actual cost records is generally the most methodical and accurate. For an acquired building without original cost records, a detailed engineering cost-estimate approach can reconstruct cost using documented quantities and reliable published sources. Sampling, modeling, survey, residual, and rule-of-thumb methods each have different limitations.

The study also does not answer every return question. A separate tax analysis must determine whether the depreciation is currently usable under Section 469, whether basis and at-risk limits apply, whether prior depreciation needs an amended return or Form 3115, how state depreciation differs, and what happens on sale.

Where DIY Cost Segregation Software Is Strong

  • Potentially lower study fee when the property and facts are simple
  • A structured process for gathering facts, photographs, and records
  • Potentially reasonable when the expected timing benefit is modest and expert review is available
  • Useful as a preliminary estimate before deciding whether deeper engineering work is economic

Where the model has limits

  • Quality depends heavily on what the user enters and what professional review is actually included
  • Incomplete records can undermine asset identification, cost reconstruction, and reconciliation
  • The report may not include passive-activity, basis, at-risk, state, or excess-business-loss analysis
  • Prior-year correction, return preparation, recapture modeling, and examination support may be separate services

Which One Should You Choose?

Consider a DIY process when the property is simple, the available records are strong, you or a reviewer can satisfy the IRS quality elements, the deduction is usable, and a higher professional fee would exceed the incremental present-value benefit.

Consider a coordinated professional engagement when the property has meaningful site work or specialty systems, records are incomplete, the allocation is material, prior returns may need correction, ownership or placed-in-service dates are complicated, or nobody has confirmed the loss is deductible this year.

These are not mutually exclusive. Plenty of clients use a specialist for the engineering work on a complex asset and AE Tax Advisors for the strategy, the filings, and the return. We are glad to work from someone else's study.

Worked Break-Even Example: Study Fee vs Usable Benefit

Assume an investor purchases a rental with $400,000 of depreciable building basis. A preliminary analysis—not a final study—indicates that $80,000 may move from 27.5-year property into shorter-lived classes. The investor compares a $700 self-service option with a $2,000 professional engagement.

The $1,300 fee difference is not the whole decision. If both methods correctly produce and support the same allocation, the lower-cost option may win. If the professional work identifies additional valid assets, fixes basis, prevents a return error, or includes implementation and support, the incremental value may exceed the fee. If the loss is suspended for years, discount the future tax benefit and model the expected sale rather than treating $15,360 as cash saved today.

This is an illustration, not an expected reclassification percentage or tax result. Actual recovery periods, bonus depreciation, tax rates, loss limitations, state adjustments, and recapture depend on the taxpayer and property.

Documents to Gather Before You Choose DIY

  • Closing statement, purchase agreement, appraisal, and support for the land allocation
  • Construction drawings, specifications, invoices, contracts, change orders, and payment applications
  • Renovation invoices separated from the original acquisition and by placed-in-service date
  • Current and prior depreciation schedules, Forms 4562 and 8582, and the owning entity's returns
  • Interior, exterior, roof, site, utility, and specialty-system photographs or inspection records
  • Quantity takeoffs, unit-cost source citations, location factors, depreciation factors, and reconciliation workpapers
  • Expected hold period, projected sale assumptions, passive-income forecast, and material-participation records

Common DIY Failures That Change the Return

  • Applying a rule-of-thumb percentage without identifying and costing actual assets
  • Including land in depreciable basis or failing to reconcile the allocation to the fixed-asset ledger
  • Classifying building systems as personal property without analyzing function and permanence
  • Using current replacement cost without adjusting to the acquisition date, location, condition, and actual purchase price
  • Ignoring indirect costs or allocating them inconsistently across asset classes
  • Combining acquisition and later-renovation assets despite different placed-in-service dates
  • Claiming accelerated depreciation without testing basis, at-risk, passive-activity, and excess-business-loss limits
  • Changing previously reported depreciation without determining the correct amendment or Form 3115 procedure
  • Failing to update the tax depreciation schedule asset by asset for future dispositions and recapture

How to Evaluate Any Provider, Including Us

Whichever firm you engage, these six questions separate a defensible engagement from a report that will not hold up. Ask them of DIY Cost Segregation Software, ask them of us, and compare the answers rather than the marketing.

  1. Ask which method the study uses. The IRS guide describes six common approaches and their limitations. Detailed engineering from actual cost records is generally the most methodical and accurate for new construction; acquired property may require a documented engineering cost-estimate approach. Ask which evidence, estimating sources, physical-property observations, and reconciliation support the selected method.
  2. Ask who runs the passive activity analysis. This is the question that decides whether the deduction is worth anything this year. A study can produce a $400,000 first-year deduction that is entirely suspended under IRC Section 469. Ask who is confirming that you materially participate, that your average period of customer use is what you believe, and that you clear the basis, at-risk, and excess business loss limits. If the answer is nobody, the study is a number on paper.
  3. Ask who determines the correction procedure. When depreciation has already been reported, the facts may call for a timely superseding return, an amended return, or Form 3115 with a Section 481(a) adjustment. Filing timing and whether an accounting method has been adopted matter. Confirm who will make that determination, prepare the required form, and update the fixed-asset schedule.
  4. Ask what happens if the study is examined. Audit support means different things. Ask whether the provider will produce the workpapers, respond to an information document request, and stand behind the allocation, and whether that is included or billed separately. Ask how many of their studies have been examined and what happened.
  5. Ask for the exit math, not just the year-one number. Accelerated depreciation can change the character and timing of gain on sale. Ask for an after-tax model using the expected holding period, current deduction usability, applicable recapture rules, sale assumptions, study fee, and time value of money rather than relying only on a first-year deduction estimate.
  6. Compare total cost, not study cost. A cheaper study plus a CPA who does not understand Section 469 is not cheaper. Add up the study, the return preparation, the Form 3115, and the planning work, then weigh that against the modeled after-tax benefit over your holding period. That is the only comparison that means anything.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I do my own cost segregation study?

Yes. Federal guidance does not prescribe a license, credential, or standard report format for the preparer. But the taxpayer must substantiate each asset's classification and basis. The IRS guide describes 13 quality elements, including expertise, methodology, documentation, legal analysis, engineering takeoffs, cost reconciliation, and related accounting-method issues.

Will a DIY study trigger an audit?

The IRS does not publish a rule saying a DIY study automatically triggers an examination. If the return is examined, the report and workpapers must support asset classifications, recovery periods, cost allocations, and reconciliation to the taxpayer's records. The IRS guide says conformity with its quality elements can affect the scope and depth of an examination.

How much more does a professional study produce?

There is no reliable universal percentage. The answer depends on the property, available construction records, methodology, and whether either study correctly classifies every asset. Compare the asset-level schedules and support, not a promised reclassification percentage.

Can I use DIY software and still have you handle the tax side?

Yes. We will review the allocation, tell you honestly whether it is defensible for your property, prepare the Form 3115 if the property has been held for years, run the passive activity analysis, and file the return. Where the allocation looks thin relative to the dollars at stake, we will say so.

When is a professional study clearly worth it?

A professional study is worth considering when its incremental fee is less than the present value of the additional usable deductions and risk reduction it provides. Complexity, records, specialty systems, prior depreciation, holding period, loss usability, and support scope matter more than a fixed property-value threshold.

AE Tax Advisors vs a do-it-yourself cost segregation study: which is better?

The right choice depends on the property and evidence. A self-prepared study can be supportable when the taxpayer has the construction, cost-estimating, and tax-classification expertise to meet the IRS quality elements. Professional help is generally more appropriate when records are incomplete, systems are complex, the deduction is material, prior depreciation needs correction, or implementation and examination support are needed.

How does AE Tax Advisors pricing compare with a do-it-yourself cost segregation study?

AE Tax Advisors publishes a cost-segregation fee of $1 per square foot with a $2,000 minimum. DIY pricing and included support vary. Compare total delivered cost: the study, review, Form 3115 or amendment work when required, fixed-asset implementation, loss analysis, state adjustments, and examination support.

Should I switch from a do-it-yourself cost segregation study to AE Tax Advisors?

Switching is worth considering when the work you need has moved beyond what your current arrangement is scoped to deliver — for example, when a study has been produced but nobody has determined whether the deduction is usable under IRC Section 469, or when depreciation was missed in prior years and no one has raised a Form 3115 catch-up. It is not worth switching where a do-it-yourself cost segregation study is already covering the work well.
