Collect these records before assets disappear

  • Existing fixed-asset and depreciation schedules, original purchase allocation, prior study reports, and later capital improvements.
  • Dated photographs, plans, locations, descriptions, and quantities of components expected to be removed. Record which building or system each belongs to.
  • Contractor proposals and invoices separating demolition, removal, repairs, replacement components, and newly added improvements.
  • Dates of removal and abandonment or sale, salvage proceeds, and dates the replacement assets are ready and available for use.
  • Any prior elections, insurance recoveries, casualty records, or general asset account treatment that could change the tax analysis.

Capstan says its studies provide unit-of-property designations and separately describes tangible-property analysis services. Confirm the detail and analysis your quote includes. A short depreciation recovery period does not automatically make a new expenditure a deductible repair.

Under the IRS tangible-property rules, a building’s structure and designated systems matter when testing an improvement. Recognizing a loss on a replaced component can also affect the capitalization analysis for its replacement. Do not assume both the remaining old basis and all replacement spending are immediately deductible.

Agree on the handoff before authorizing fieldwork

Ask the study team to show how pre-renovation records will connect to the post-project asset schedule. Have the preparer identify the decisions needed before removal, before filing, and after the new assets are placed in service. Confirm whether a follow-up visit, revised report, or additional analysis is included or separately quoted.

A partial disposition and a depreciation method correction are different questions. For an older property, the preparer should verify the correct procedure, any Form 3115 eligibility and section 481(a) adjustment, and the timing of elections. Do not assume a later study can recreate missing facts or cure a missed election.

For a profitable business owner holding the building in a separate entity, also check who owns each asset, which taxpayer paid the improvement costs, and how deductions reach the owner. Passive activity and other loss limitations can affect the value of accelerating deductions.

Frequently asked questions

Why consider a cost segregation study before renovation?

A pre-renovation review can preserve records of assets before they are removed. Capstan discusses this use in its study FAQ. Whether a separate study is worthwhile depends on the available records, expected tax treatment, timing, and cost; two studies are not automatically required.

Does a unit-of-property schedule decide whether an expenditure is a repair?

No. It helps organize the analysis, but the preparer must apply the tangible-property rules to the relevant building structure or system and the actual work. Betterments, restorations, and adaptations can require capitalization; available safe harbors have their own requirements.

Does replacing a component automatically create a partial disposition deduction?

No. The taxpayer must establish the disposed asset and its basis, consider prior depreciation and applicable elections, and follow the relevant timing rules. Keep the removed component, removal costs, and new replacement costs separate for the preparer’s review.

Does a cost segregation study include all renovation tax work?

Not necessarily. Ask whether the quote includes pre-removal documentation, unit-of-property designations, tangible-property analysis, disposition support, and post-renovation schedules. Name the professional responsible for tax decisions, elections, and return filing.
