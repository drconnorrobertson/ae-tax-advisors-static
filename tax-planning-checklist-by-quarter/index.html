Tax Planning Checklist by Quarter for Business Owners

By AE Tax Advisors Team | Published October 3, 2026

Why Quarterly Tax Planning Matters

Most business owners think about taxes once a year, usually in a last-minute scramble during April or a frantic December call to their accountant. That reactive approach leaves thousands of dollars on the table. Proactive, quarterly tax planning allows you to spread decisions across the calendar, respond to changes in income as they happen, and lock in savings before deadlines pass.

Tax planning is not the same as tax preparation. Preparation is backward-looking: you gather documents, fill in forms, and file. Planning is forward-looking: you project income, evaluate strategies, time purchases, and structure your business to minimize your legal tax obligation throughout the year. The difference between the two can easily be $20,000 to $100,000 or more in annual tax savings for a business owner earning $300,000 or above.

This checklist breaks the year into four quarters and gives you specific, actionable items for each period. Whether you are a sole proprietor, an S-Corp owner, a real estate investor, or a multi-entity operator, these steps will keep you on track. Print this page, share it with your bookkeeper, and review it with your tax advisor at each quarterly meeting.

Q1: January to March

The first quarter is the foundation of your entire tax year. Decisions made (or missed) in January through March set the trajectory for everything that follows. This is the time to look backward at the prior year while simultaneously building forward-looking projections.

1. Review Your Prior-Year Tax Position

Before you can plan for the current year, you need to understand where you ended up last year. Pull your prior-year income statement, balance sheet, and tax return (or draft return if it is not yet filed). Identify your effective tax rate, your largest deductions, and any items your advisor flagged for follow-up. This review creates the baseline for the current year's projections.

2. Gather and Organize Tax Documents

January is when W-2s, 1099s, K-1s, 1098s, and brokerage statements arrive. Create a centralized folder (physical or digital) and begin collecting every document as it comes in. Cross-reference your list against the prior year to make sure nothing is missing. Late or missing 1099s are a common audit trigger, so verify that every payer who should have sent a form has done so by the end of February.

3. Make Prior-Year Retirement Contributions

You have until April 15 (or the extended due date, if applicable) to make prior-year contributions to a traditional IRA or Roth IRA. If you have a SEP-IRA, you can contribute up to 25% of net self-employment income for the prior year, with the contribution deadline tied to your filing deadline including extensions. This is one of the few opportunities to go back in time and reduce your prior-year tax liability.

4. Evaluate and Update Your Entity Structure

If you operated as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC last year and your net income exceeded $60,000, it may be time to elect S-Corp status by filing Form 2553 with the IRS. The deadline to make this election effective for the current year is March 15 (or within 75 days of forming the entity). S-Corp election allows you to split income between salary and distributions, potentially saving 15.3% in self-employment tax on the distribution portion.

5. Set Up or Review Your Bookkeeping System

Accurate books are the foundation of every tax strategy. In January, reconcile all bank and credit card accounts for the prior year, close your books, and set up fresh categories for the new year. If you are still using spreadsheets or shoeboxes, migrate to a proper accounting platform such as QuickBooks Online or Xero. Clean books make quarterly projections possible and year-end planning straightforward.

6. Review Estimated Tax Payment Strategy

If you owed a large balance or received a penalty notice last year, recalculate your estimated payments for the current year. The safe harbor rule requires you to pay at least 100% of your prior-year tax liability (110% if your AGI exceeded $150,000) through withholding and estimated payments. Divide this amount into four equal vouchers, or use the annualized income installment method if your income is seasonal.

7. Review Health Insurance and Benefits Elections

If you missed open enrollment for your health insurance marketplace plan or employer group plan, check whether you qualify for a Special Enrollment Period due to a life event. For S-Corp owners, confirm that health insurance premiums are being added to your W-2 as required, which enables the self-employed health insurance deduction on your personal return.

8. Assess Qualified Business Income (QBI) Deduction Eligibility

The Section 199A deduction allows eligible business owners to deduct up to 20% of qualified business income. Review whether your business type and income level qualify. If you are in a specified service trade or business (SSTB) such as law, medicine, consulting, or financial services, the deduction phases out at higher income levels. Planning around these thresholds in Q1 gives you the full year to implement strategies such as income splitting or entity restructuring.

9. Update Your Payroll and Compensation Plan

For S-Corp owners, establish your officer compensation (reasonable salary) for the current year. The IRS requires that S-Corp shareholders who perform services receive a reasonable salary before taking distributions. Set this amount based on comparable market data and your projected business income. Also update payroll tax withholding using the IRS withholding estimator to avoid surprises at filing time.

10. Schedule Your Quarterly Tax Planning Meetings

Block four dates on your calendar for tax planning meetings with your advisor: one in January or February, one in May, one in August, and one in November. These meetings keep your plan on track, allow for mid-year course corrections, and ensure that year-end strategies are implemented before December 31. Waiting until December to call your advisor is too late for most planning strategies.

11. Review State and Local Tax Obligations

If you expanded into new states, hired remote employees, or generated revenue from new jurisdictions last year, you may have new state filing obligations. Review your nexus footprint and confirm you are registered for income tax, sales tax, and payroll tax in every state where you have a presence. Many states now have economic nexus thresholds based on revenue or transaction counts.

12. Evaluate Depreciation and Asset Schedules

Review your current fixed asset schedule and depreciation methods. Identify any assets that were disposed of, sold, or scrapped during the prior year and ensure they are properly removed from your books. For new acquisitions planned in the current year, discuss whether Section 179 expensing, bonus depreciation, or standard MACRS depreciation produces the best tax outcome over the next several years.

Q2: April to June

The second quarter revolves around filing deadlines, first-quarter estimated payments, and the first real look at how the current year is tracking against your projections. This is also an excellent time to make structural changes that will benefit you for the remaining three quarters.

1. File or Extend Your Tax Returns

Individual returns (Form 1040) are due April 15. S-Corp returns (Form 1120-S) and partnership returns (Form 1065) were due March 15 (or extended to September 15). If you cannot file by the deadline, file an extension. An extension gives you additional time to file, not additional time to pay. Estimate your liability and submit payment with the extension to avoid interest and late-payment penalties.

2. Submit Q1 Estimated Tax Payments

The first quarterly estimated payment for the current tax year is due April 15. Use Form 1040-ES and base the payment on your Q1 income projection. If your income is lumpy or seasonal, consider using the annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI) to right-size your payments rather than paying equal quarters.

3. Conduct a Mid-Year Income Review

By the end of Q2, you have nearly half a year of actual income data. Compare your year-to-date revenue and expenses against the projection you built in Q1. If income is running significantly higher or lower than expected, adjust your estimated payments and revisit your tax strategies. An income variance of more than 15% from projection is a signal to recalibrate your plan.

4. Review Insurance Coverage and Employee Benefits

Evaluate your business insurance policies (general liability, professional liability, property, cyber, and key-person life insurance). Premiums are deductible business expenses, and adequate coverage protects your assets. If you have employees, review your benefits package to ensure it remains competitive. Consider adding or upgrading a retirement plan (401(k), SIMPLE IRA, or defined benefit plan) mid-year if you have not done so already.

5. Consider Mid-Year Entity Changes

If your income has grown substantially, Q2 may be the right time to evaluate converting from an S-Corp to a C-Corp, forming a holding company, or creating a separate entity for a new business line. C-Corps pay a flat 21% federal rate and may provide tax advantages for owners who do not need to distribute all profits. Consult your advisor before making any structural changes, as the tax implications are significant and sometimes irreversible.

6. Review Accounts Receivable and Cash Flow

Uncollected receivables inflate your income on an accrual basis without putting cash in your pocket. Review outstanding invoices and pursue collections on anything over 60 days. If you have receivables that are truly uncollectible, document the bad debt and discuss the write-off with your advisor. For cash-basis taxpayers, consider the timing of billing and collections to manage taxable income.

7. Evaluate Retirement Plan Contributions and Limits

Confirm that your payroll is withholding the correct amount for employee and employer 401(k) contributions. For 2026, the employee deferral limit is subject to annual IRS adjustments. If you are over 50, you may be eligible for catch-up contributions. If you are considering a cash balance plan layered on top of your 401(k), Q2 is the time to begin the actuarial analysis and plan design.

8. Assess State Tax Credits and Incentives

Many states offer tax credits for hiring, research and development, capital investment, and job creation. These credits often require advance registration or certification and have annual funding caps. Research the credits available in your state and apply before the allocation runs out. Common programs include the Work Opportunity Tax Credit (WOTC), state R&D credits, and enterprise zone incentives.

9. Document Vehicle and Travel Expenses

If you use a vehicle for business, confirm that you are maintaining a contemporaneous mileage log. The IRS requires documentation of the date, destination, business purpose, and miles driven for each trip. If you have not been tracking mileage, start now and reconstruct Q1 records from your calendar and appointment history. Choose between the standard mileage rate and the actual expense method based on which produces a larger deduction.

10. Review Loan and Debt Structures

Examine your business loans, lines of credit, and shareholder loans. Interest on bona fide business debt is deductible, but the IRS scrutinizes shareholder loans closely. Make sure any loans between you and your S-Corp or C-Corp are properly documented with promissory notes, stated interest rates, and a repayment schedule. Reclassification of a shareholder loan as a distribution can create unexpected tax liability.

11. Plan for Midyear Bonus Payments

If your business performance is strong, consider timing bonus payments strategically. For S-Corp owners, additional salary or bonus payments increase your W-2 wages, which can help you qualify for or increase the Section 199A QBI deduction (subject to the W-2 wage limitation). For employees, mid-year bonuses may be more tax-efficient than year-end lump sums depending on withholding calculations.

Q3: July to September

The third quarter is the midpoint of the year and the ideal time for a comprehensive tax projection. By now you have six months of actual data, and the strategies you implement in Q3 will have time to take full effect before year-end.

1. Run a Mid-Year Tax Projection

This is the single most important step of Q3. Your tax advisor should prepare a full-year projection using six months of actual data and six months of estimated income and deductions. This projection reveals your anticipated tax liability, identifies gaps in estimated payments, and highlights opportunities for additional savings. Without this projection, year-end planning is just guesswork.

2. Review Q2 Estimated Tax Payments

The second quarterly estimated payment was due June 15. Verify that it was submitted on time and in the correct amount. If your mid-year projection shows that your payments are falling short of the safe harbor threshold, increase your Q3 and Q4 payments to avoid underpayment penalties. The Q3 payment is due September 15.

3. Evaluate Equipment and Capital Purchases

If your projection shows a larger-than-expected tax liability, Q3 is a strategic time to accelerate planned equipment purchases. Under Section 179, you can deduct the full cost of qualifying equipment (up to the annual limit) in the year it is placed in service. Bonus depreciation allows additional first-year deductions on eligible assets. Purchasing and placing equipment in service before September 30 ensures the deduction is captured on the current year's return without the pressure of a year-end rush.

4. Assess Cost Segregation Opportunities

If you own commercial or residential rental property, a cost segregation study can reclassify portions of the building into shorter depreciation categories (5, 7, and 15 years instead of 27.5 or 39 years). The result is accelerated depreciation deductions that can offset substantial income in the current year. Q3 is the ideal time to commission a cost segregation study so the results are ready for year-end planning. For properties placed in service in prior years, a look-back study can capture missed depreciation through a Section 481(a) adjustment filed on the current-year return.

5. Review Payroll and Reasonable Compensation

For S-Corp owners, confirm that your officer salary is on track to meet the "reasonable compensation" standard by year-end. If you set your salary too low in Q1, you may need to increase payroll in Q3 and Q4. If your salary is too high relative to business income, you may be paying unnecessary payroll taxes. Adjusting mid-year is far easier than correcting after December 31.

6. Maximize the Home Office Deduction

If you work from a dedicated home office, ensure you are claiming the appropriate deduction. The simplified method allows $5 per square foot up to 300 square feet ($1,500 maximum). The actual expense method calculates your deduction based on the percentage of your home used for business, applied against mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities, and depreciation. Gather your records now so the calculation is ready at year-end.

7. Review Inventory and Cost of Goods Sold

If your business carries inventory, perform a mid-year physical count or cycle count. Identify slow-moving, damaged, or obsolete inventory that can be written down to lower of cost or market value. Inventory adjustments reduce your cost of goods sold, which directly affects your taxable income. This is also a good time to review your inventory costing method (FIFO, LIFO, or weighted average) for tax efficiency.

8. Evaluate Research and Development Tax Credits

The Section 41 R&D tax credit is available to businesses that develop or improve products, processes, software, or formulas. Many business owners assume this credit is only for large corporations or pharmaceutical companies, but it applies broadly to any qualifying research activity. Common qualifying activities include software development, product design, manufacturing process improvements, and engineering. Document your R&D activities and expenses now while they are fresh.

9. Plan for Charitable Giving

If charitable giving is part of your tax strategy, Q3 is the time to begin planning. Donating appreciated stock instead of cash allows you to deduct the fair market value while avoiding capital gains tax on the appreciation. For larger gifts, consider establishing a donor-advised fund (DAF) that allows you to claim the deduction in the current year while distributing grants to charities over time. Corporate charitable contributions are subject to different limits than individual donations, so confirm the rules that apply to your situation.

10. Verify Contractor Classifications

The IRS has increased scrutiny on worker classification. Review all independent contractors to confirm they are properly classified and not actually employees under the common-law test. Misclassification can result in back taxes, penalties, and interest on employment taxes that should have been withheld. If you have contractors who work exclusively for you, on your schedule, using your tools, the classification may not survive an audit.

11. Review Real Estate Portfolio Tax Strategy

If you own rental properties, review your rental income and expense reports mid-year. Confirm that you are capturing all deductible expenses, including property management fees, maintenance, insurance, property taxes, and travel to inspect properties. If you qualify as a real estate professional under IRC Section 469(c)(7), your rental losses can offset active income without limitation, but you must meet the 750-hour and material participation tests. Document your hours now rather than trying to reconstruct them in April.

Q4: October to December

The fourth quarter is your last opportunity to take action on the current tax year. Every strategy discussed in Q1 through Q3 converges here. The decisions you make between October and December 31 lock in your tax position for the year, so precision and urgency are critical.

1. Hold a Year-End Tax Planning Meeting

Schedule a comprehensive meeting with your tax advisor no later than the first week of November. Bring your year-to-date profit and loss statement, balance sheet, and a list of any major transactions or changes expected before year-end. Your advisor should update the full-year tax projection and present a specific list of action items with deadlines and dollar amounts.

2. Accelerate Deductible Expenses

If your projection shows a high tax liability, consider prepaying deductible expenses before December 31. Common examples include prepaying January rent, stocking up on supplies, purchasing business insurance for the following year, and paying outstanding vendor invoices. For cash-basis taxpayers, the deduction is claimed in the year the payment is made. Be mindful of the economic substance doctrine; expenses must be ordinary, necessary, and have a genuine business purpose.

3. Defer Income Where Appropriate

If you expect your income to be lower next year (due to retirement, business slowdown, or a planned sabbatical), consider deferring income into the following year. For cash-basis taxpayers, this can be as simple as delaying the mailing of invoices until January or scheduling project completions for early January. For accrual-basis taxpayers, the options are more limited but may include deferring advance payments under Revenue Procedure 2004-34.

4. Maximize Retirement Plan Contributions

Confirm that you will reach the maximum allowable contribution for your retirement plan by December 31 (or the filing deadline for SEP-IRAs). For a solo 401(k), this includes both the employee deferral and the employer profit-sharing contribution. If you have a defined benefit or cash balance plan, ensure the required contribution is funded by the plan's fiscal year-end. Retirement contributions are often the single largest deduction available to business owners, with potential savings of $50,000 to $200,000 or more depending on the plan type and your age.

5. Purchase Equipment Before Year-End

Assets must be placed in service (not just ordered or paid for) before December 31 to qualify for current-year Section 179 or bonus depreciation. If you have been planning a vehicle purchase, technology upgrade, or equipment acquisition, complete the transaction and begin using the asset before the end of the year. For vehicles, special rules and dollar limits apply depending on the weight and use classification. Vehicles over 6,000 pounds GVWR qualify for higher Section 179 limits under the SUV provision.

6. Review 1099 Vendor Information

In January, you will be required to issue Form 1099-NEC to every contractor, freelancer, or service provider you paid $600 or more during the year. Collect W-9 forms from all vendors before December 31 to avoid a scramble in January. Missing or incorrect taxpayer identification numbers can result in backup withholding requirements and IRS penalties for incorrect information returns.

7. Implement Charitable Giving Strategies

Make charitable contributions before December 31 to claim the deduction on the current-year return. As discussed in Q3, donating appreciated securities avoids capital gains tax while providing a deduction at fair market value. If you are over 70 and a half, consider a Qualified Charitable Distribution (QCD) directly from your IRA, which satisfies your Required Minimum Distribution without adding to your taxable income. For business owners, sponsoring local events or donating inventory at fair market value can also generate deductions.

8. Evaluate Roth Conversion Opportunities

If your income is lower than usual this year (due to business losses, large deductions, or a transitional year), converting traditional IRA or 401(k) funds to a Roth IRA may make sense. You pay ordinary income tax on the converted amount now, but all future growth and withdrawals are tax-free. The key is to convert in a year when your marginal tax rate is lower than you expect it to be in retirement. Your advisor should model the conversion at various amounts to find the optimal level that fills up your current tax bracket without pushing you into the next one.

9. Review Estimated Payments for Accuracy

Your fourth and final estimated tax payment is due January 15 of the following year. Before you submit it, compare your total estimated payments and withholding against your projected tax liability. If you are short of the safe harbor threshold, increase the Q4 payment to avoid penalties. If you have overpaid, you can reduce the final payment. For W-2 employees and S-Corp owners, increasing withholding in Q4 can be more effective than estimated payments because withholding is treated as paid evenly throughout the year, even if it was all withheld in December.

10. Execute Year-End Payroll Adjustments

For S-Corp owners, finalize your officer compensation by running a year-end bonus payroll if your salary needs adjustment. This is also the deadline to make employer-matching or profit-sharing contributions for 401(k) plans on a calendar-year basis. Ensure your final payroll of the year processes before December 31 and that all payroll tax deposits are made on time.

11. Review Pass-Through Entity Tax Elections

Many states now offer a pass-through entity tax (PTET) election that allows S-Corps and partnerships to pay state income tax at the entity level, generating a federal deduction that circumvents the $10,000 SALT cap for individual taxpayers. If your state offers this election, it often must be made by a specific deadline (some require annual elections by the end of the tax year). Confirm with your advisor whether this election makes sense for your situation and ensure it is filed on time.

12. Document Everything

Before the year closes, ensure that all major transactions, agreements, and decisions are properly documented. This includes board resolutions for bonuses and retirement contributions, loan agreements between you and your entities, minutes from shareholder meetings, and written records of any elections or consents. Proper documentation is your best defense in the event of an IRS examination.

13. Assess the Impact of Expiring Tax Provisions

Tax law changes frequently, and provisions that exist this year may not be available next year. Review any tax breaks that are scheduled to expire or phase down, such as bonus depreciation percentages, specific credits, or deduction thresholds. If a provision is sunsetting, it may be advantageous to accelerate activity into the current year to capture the benefit before it disappears.

Putting It All Together: Your Annual Tax Planning Calendar

Effective tax planning is not a single event. It is a continuous process that requires discipline, communication, and timely execution. Here is a summary of the critical deadlines and action items by month:

  • January: Gather documents, review prior year, set up books, evaluate entity structure, schedule quarterly meetings.
  • February: Verify all 1099s and W-2s received, make prior-year retirement contributions, finalize entity elections.
  • March: Deadline for S-Corp election (March 15), partnership and S-Corp returns due (March 15), complete prior-year planning.
  • April: Individual returns due (April 15), Q1 estimated payment due (April 15), file extensions if needed.
  • May: Q2 planning meeting, mid-year income review, update projections, review benefits and insurance.
  • June: Q2 estimated payment due (June 15), assess entity changes, review contractor classifications.
  • July: Begin mid-year tax projection, evaluate capital purchase timing, review cost segregation opportunities.
  • August: Q3 planning meeting, finalize mid-year projection, adjust estimated payments, commission cost segregation study if applicable.
  • September: Q3 estimated payment due (September 15), extended returns due (September 15 for S-Corps and partnerships, October 15 for individuals), review real estate strategy.
  • October: Extended individual returns due (October 15), begin year-end planning, review 1099 vendor list.
  • November: Year-end planning meeting with advisor, execute equipment purchases, finalize charitable giving, evaluate Roth conversions.
  • December: Final payroll adjustments, maximize retirement contributions, accelerate deductions, defer income, document everything. All actions must be completed by December 31.

The Cost of Doing Nothing

Business owners who skip quarterly tax planning pay significantly more in taxes than they need to. Without a structured plan, common outcomes include: underpayment penalties from missed or insufficient estimated payments, missed retirement contribution deadlines that cannot be recovered, failure to capture bonus depreciation or Section 179 deductions on equipment placed in service during the year, overlooked state tax credits and entity-level elections, and a December 31 deadline that arrives with no time left to act.

The IRS does not reward procrastination, and the tax code does not offer second chances for most deadlines. A quarterly planning discipline, supported by a knowledgeable tax advisor, is the single most effective way to keep more of what you earn.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start tax planning for the year?

Tax planning should begin in January, not in December. Starting in Q1 allows you to structure your entity, set up accounting systems, make prior-year retirement contributions before the filing deadline, and establish a baseline income projection that guides decisions throughout the year.

How often should I meet with my tax advisor during the year?

At minimum, you should meet quarterly. A January kickoff meeting sets the strategy. An April or May check-in reviews filing outcomes and Q1 results. A July or August mid-year projection meeting recalibrates estimates. A November year-end planning session locks in final moves before December 31.

What happens if I miss an estimated tax payment deadline?

The IRS charges an underpayment penalty calculated at the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, compounded daily. For the 2026 tax year, this penalty rate can exceed 8%. Missing a single quarterly payment can trigger penalties on the shortfall for every quarter that follows. Filing Form 2210 with the annualized income installment method may reduce or eliminate the penalty if your income was uneven throughout the year.

Can I change my business entity structure mid-year?

Yes, but the timing matters. An LLC can elect S-Corp status using Form 2553 with a mid-year effective date, though the IRS generally prefers elections filed within 75 days of the start of the tax year. Converting from a sole proprietorship to an LLC or from an S-Corp to a C-Corp mid-year requires careful handling of short-year returns and asset transfers. The best time to evaluate and execute entity changes is Q1 or Q2, so the new structure is in place for the majority of the tax year.

How much should I set aside for estimated tax payments as a business owner?

A general rule is to reserve 25% to 35% of net business income for federal and state taxes combined. However, the precise amount depends on your total household income, filing status, deductions, credits, and state tax rates. Your tax advisor should run a projection each quarter and provide a specific voucher amount. Paying at least 110% of your prior-year tax liability (100% if AGI was under $150,000) through withholding and estimates is the safe harbor that avoids underpayment penalties entirely.

What is the most commonly missed tax deduction for business owners?

Retirement plan contributions are among the most commonly missed deductions. Many business owners contribute to a basic SEP-IRA or traditional 401(k) but never explore higher-contribution vehicles such as cash balance pension plans, which can allow deductions exceeding $100,000 per year for owners over age 50. Other frequently overlooked deductions include the home office deduction (actual expense method), cost segregation for real estate, Augusta Rule rental income exclusion under IRC Section 280A, and the qualified business income deduction under Section 199A.
