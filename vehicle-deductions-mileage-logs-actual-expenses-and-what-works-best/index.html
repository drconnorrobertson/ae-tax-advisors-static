Standard Mileage vs Actual Expenses: Compare Vehicle Methods

By AE Tax Advisors · Published 2023-12-11

Updated November 8, 2026.

The better vehicle method depends on eligibility, prior use and elections, actual cost, business mileage and future plans. Calculate both methods where permitted. The larger number is useful only if the taxpayer is allowed to use that method.

Compare what each method measures

MethodCalculationRecords
Standard mileageEligible business miles multiplied by the applicable rate for each period.Trip dates, miles, business purpose, total use and eligibility history.
Actual expensesAllowable vehicle costs allocated to business use, with depreciation or lease limits.Business-use records plus invoices, payments, basis and depreciation or lease records.

Neither method turns ordinary commuting or personal use into a deduction. Ownership through an LLC does not establish business use.

Apply the two 2026 business rates

Eligible business miles from January 1 through June 30 use 72.5 cents per mile. Miles from July 1 through December 31 use 76 cents. Keep the two periods separate.

For an eligible vehicle with 10,000 qualifying miles in each period, the standard-mileage calculation is $7,250 plus $7,600, or $14,850. Compare that with allowable actual costs for the same year and business use, including the correct depreciation limitations. These numbers illustrate a comparison, not a promised deduction.

Check eligibility before switching

For an owned car, use of the standard-mileage method in the first year it is available for business use can be important to future method choices. Prior Section 179, bonus depreciation or other depreciation methods can prevent later standard mileage. Review Publication 463 and the vehicle's complete return history.

For a leased car, choosing standard mileage generally requires continuing that method for the lease period, including renewals. Multiple simultaneously used vehicles and other restrictions also need review.

Do not double-count included costs

The standard rate incorporates vehicle operating costs and a depreciation component. Do not add a second deduction for the same fuel, insurance, repairs or depreciation. Separately permitted business parking, tolls and other items have their own rules.

Actual-expense treatment requires the supported business share of eligible costs. A financed purchase is not simply deductible when loan principal is paid; vehicle basis, depreciation and interest are different items.

Include sale and reimbursement consequences

Track basis reductions from the depreciation component of standard mileage as well as actual depreciation. A later sale or decline in business use can have tax consequences. The first-year deduction does not describe the whole ownership cost.

If an employer reimburses the use, reconcile its accountable-plan treatment and any personal payment before claiming an owner-level deduction. An employee, sole proprietor and S corporation owner do not necessarily report the same way.

Bring one comparison file to the preparer

  • Purchase or lease agreement and first business-use date.
  • Prior returns, depreciation elections and reimbursement records.
  • Business miles by date, total miles and personal use.
  • Fuel, repair, insurance, registration and other actual costs.
  • Expected replacement, sale or change in use.

See the mileage-log requirements for supporting the trips. Discuss a vehicle-method review before making an election based only on the largest current-year number.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I simply choose whichever number is higher?

Only after checking eligibility, prior elections, lease rules and future consequences.

Can I claim fuel in addition to standard mileage?

Not for fuel already included in the mileage rate; avoid deducting the same cost twice.

Does standard mileage affect basis?

Yes. Its depreciation component generally reduces vehicle basis and matters when the vehicle is sold.
