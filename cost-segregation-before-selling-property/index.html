Should You Do Cost Segregation Before Selling a Property?

An 18-month exit can change the economics. Compare the deduction you can use with recapture, study cost, and the actual sale plan.

Book a Return Review Call

Key Takeaways

  • Cost segregation before a sale may recover depreciation that should have been claimed in prior years, often through Form 3115. The strategy must be modeled with depreciation recapture, passive-loss release, transaction timing, and current-year tax rates because accelerating a deduction shortly before disposition can also accelerate ordinary-income recapture.
  • For an expected sale in about two years, compare the tax saved on deductions usable before closing with asset-level sale tax, study fees, and the time value of the early benefit. A projected deduction is not the same as spendable cash.
  • Review the purchase and sale allocations, passive-loss carryforwards, prior depreciation, possible 1031 treatment, state rules, and Form 3115 timing before paying for a study or filing a correction.

The Short Answer

Cost segregation before a sale may recover depreciation that should have been claimed in prior years, often through Form 3115. The strategy must be modeled with depreciation recapture, passive-loss release, transaction timing, and current-year tax rates because accelerating a deduction shortly before disposition can also accelerate ordinary-income recapture.

If you expect to sell the rental in roughly two years, a study can still be worthwhile—but only if the after-tax benefit survives the sale model. First ask whether the accelerated deduction is usable before closing. Then allocate the expected sale proceeds among the same assets the study would reclassify, calculate potential ordinary-income recapture, include the study and implementation fees, and compare the result with doing no study. Neither “short hold means never” nor “bonus depreciation means always” is a reliable decision rule.

Why a Late Study Can Still Matter

Tax depreciation generally follows an allowed-or-allowable concept. Failing to claim the correct amount does not necessarily preserve basis forever. A lookback study may correct the method and claim a catch-up before the asset leaves the taxpayer's hands.

That can be valuable when the deduction offsets high-rate ordinary income or when released passive losses create room to use the adjustment.

The Recapture Tradeoff

Assets reclassified into shorter lives may generate Section 1245 ordinary-income recapture on sale to the extent of gain and prior depreciation. The benefit of an ordinary deduction followed quickly by ordinary recapture may be small unless rates, timing, state treatment, or loss interactions create value.

The model should compare the no-study sale, the study with catch-up, and any sale allocation effects. Do not compare only the current deduction.

Worked 18-Month Hold: The Study Fee Can Change the Answer

Assume a defensible study moves $50,000 of depreciation into an earlier year and the owner can use all of that incremental deduction immediately. At an illustrative 35% marginal tax rate, the near-term federal tax reduction is $17,500. Assume the combined study and implementation fee is $6,000. On a taxable sale 18 months later, the gain actually allocated to the reclassified Section 1245 assets, and the depreciation allowed or allowable on those assets, determine ordinary-income recapture under IRS Publication 544; recapture is not automatically the entire study deduction.

If the asset-level sale model produces $40,000 of incremental ordinary recapture taxed at an illustrative 32%, the $12,800 sale-year tax plus the $6,000 fee exceed the $17,500 initial federal tax saving by $1,300 before discounting and other tax effects. If the supportable recapture is instead $20,000, its illustrative tax is $6,400 and the same simplified comparison leaves $5,100 before time value and other effects. These are sensitivity cases, not a promised return or a rule that a seller may choose an arbitrary allocation. The sale price must be allocated consistently with the assets and the deal facts; compare federal and state results, rate changes, regular depreciation without a study, and whether the owner can actually use the deduction.

The timing value of a deduction received now can matter, but a suspended loss may postpone it until the sale or later. A fully taxable sale of an entire passive activity to an unrelated buyer can release qualifying suspended passive losses under Publication 925; a partial, related-party, or installment disposition can behave differently. If a 1031 exchange is contemplated, do not assume every reclassified component or all recapture is deferred: Publication 544 describes asset-level recapture in like-kind exchanges. Compare a taxable sale with the proposed exchange using the actual property and boot details.

Passive Losses and Complete Dispositions

A fully taxable disposition of an entire passive activity can release suspended passive losses, subject to the transaction facts. That can make the sale year different from prior years and may change how much of a catch-up is usable.

Entity ownership, related-party sales, installment treatment, and partial dispositions can complicate the analysis. The advisor should review the actual transaction rather than assume every sale releases every loss.

A Pre-Sale Decision Checklist

Estimate closing date and sales price, identify asset allocations, gather the depreciation schedule, quantify suspended losses, and model federal and state outcomes. Confirm whether the accounting-method change can be filed for the intended year.

If the sale is imminent, coordinate the study provider, return preparer, and transaction advisor before documents are finalized. Purchase-price allocations signed at closing can affect both sides. Ask whether the proposed asset classifications and supporting records can withstand the documentation questions in the IRS Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide.

Bring the acquisition closing statement and land allocation, placed-in-service date, prior depreciation schedules, any existing study or improvement ledger, proposed sale price and contract allocations, recent returns and passive-loss carryforwards, study quote, and the expected closing date. Ask for a side-by-side no-study versus study projection that reconciles each asset's basis, depreciation, amount realized, and Form 4797 character. If the proposal relies on a catch-up, confirm the Form 3115 eligibility and filing procedure for the intended year rather than assuming the sale-year method change is automatic.

A common failure is multiplying a projected first-year deduction by today's tax rate while leaving sale-year recapture, passive-loss limits, state nonconformity, and fees out of the comparison. Another is treating all 15-year property as Section 1245 or every dollar of real-property gain as a flat 25% recapture item. Review the asset-character guide and sale-after-study guide before using a simple calculator result as a filing position.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I file Form 3115 in the year I sell?

Potentially, depending on the facts and procedural requirements. The filing and sale timeline should be reviewed before the return is prepared.

Does cost segregation always increase recapture?

Reclassification can change the amount and character of recapture. The net result depends on gain, asset allocations, prior depreciation, and the holding period.

What if I have suspended passive losses?

A qualifying disposition may release some suspended losses, which can materially change the model. Confirm the activity and transaction details with the preparer.

I may sell my rental in two years. Is a cost segregation study still worth it?

Possibly, but a short hold makes the full after-tax comparison essential. Model only deductions you can use before the sale, the section 1245 and section 1250 consequences allocated to the actual assets sold, study and implementation fees, passive-loss rules, and whether a planned exchange changes the result. A large first-year deduction alone does not prove the study pays for itself.
