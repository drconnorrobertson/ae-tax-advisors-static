The Business Owner's Guide to Section 1202 Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) Exclusion

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

Section 1202 can exclude part or all of eligible gain when a noncorporate taxpayer sells qualifying domestic C corporation stock. Start with the stock's acquisition and issuance dates: the 2025 law changed the holding periods, per-issuer dollar limit and gross-assets ceiling for newer stock. Entity status, original issuance, business activity, redemptions and records still determine whether the stock qualifies.

The Fundamentals: What Qualifies as QSBS?

QSBS generally requires original-issue stock in an eligible domestic C corporation, acquired for money, property other than stock, or services. Secondary purchases generally do not qualify. Gifts and transfers at death can preserve qualifying treatment under Section 1202(h); other transfers require separate review.

For stock issued on or before July 4, 2025, aggregate gross assets must not exceed $50 million; for stock issued after July 4, 2025, the ceiling is $75 million in 2026. The test covers the corporation and any predecessor at all times after August 9, 1993 and before issuance, and immediately after issuance including the new proceeds. Parent-subsidiary controlled-group rules also apply. Gross assets generally mean cash plus adjusted tax basis of other property, with contributed property measured using fair market value at contribution. Enterprise valuation is not the same measure.

At least 80% of assets by value must be used in one or more qualified active trades or businesses during substantially all of the holding period. This is an asset test, not an 80% gross-income or revenue test. Reasonably required working capital and qualifying investment funds expected to be used within two years for research or increased working capital can count, but after the corporation has existed for at least two years no more than 50% of its assets may qualify solely under the working-capital rule. Separate limits apply to portfolio securities and nonbusiness real estate.

Stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025 must be held for more than five years. Qualifying stock acquired after September 27, 2010 and on or before July 4, 2025 can receive a 100% exclusion. For stock acquired after July 4, 2025, the exclusion is 50% after at least three years, 75% after at least four years, and 100% after at least five years. These percentages apply to eligible gain after the per-issuer limit, not automatically to all sale proceeds. Acquisition dates reflect applicable holding-period tacking rules.

The Dollar Limit and the 10-Times-Basis Rule

The eligible-gain ceiling is the greater of the remaining per-issuer dollar limit or 10 times the adjusted basis of that issuer's QSBS sold during the tax year, ignoring additions to basis after original issuance. The dollar limit is $10 million for stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025, or $15 million for stock acquired after July 4, 2025 (the 2026 amount, with inflation adjustments beginning after 2026). Prior eligible gain from the same issuer reduces the dollar limit; mixed old and new lots require coordination, not two independent allowances. Apply the applicable exclusion percentage to the eligible gain after this limit.

Both examples use stock acquired after September 27, 2010 and on or before July 4, 2025, held more than five years, with a 100% exclusion and no prior issuer gain. They are hypothetical calculations.

Example 1: $800,000 original basis. A $12 million sale less $800,000 basis produces $11.2 million gain. The greater of $10 million or $8 million (10 x $800,000) is $10 million. Excluded gain: $10 million. Taxable gain: $1.2 million.

Example 2: $1.2 million original basis. A $25 million sale less $1.2 million basis produces $23.8 million gain. The greater of $10 million or $12 million (10 x $1.2 million) is $12 million. Excluded gain: $12 million. Taxable gain: $11.8 million.

The basis alternative matters when it exceeds the remaining dollar ceiling. Additional basis added after original issuance is disregarded for this calculation.

The Holding Period: Test the Sale Date

Stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025 must be held for more than five years. Qualifying stock acquired after September 27, 2010 and on or before July 4, 2025 can receive a 100% exclusion. For stock acquired after July 4, 2025, the exclusion is 50% after at least three years, 75% after at least four years, and 100% after at least five years. These percentages apply to eligible gain after the per-issuer limit, not automatically to all sale proceeds. Acquisition dates reflect applicable holding-period tacking rules.

The relevant holding period generally begins when stock is acquired, not when the business is formed. Option grants are not stock purchases; exercise, vesting elections, convertible instruments and statutory tacking rules can change the date analysis.

Installment sale timing: The holding-period test is applied at the sale, not when the last installment is collected. Receiving a payment after the required anniversary does not cure an earlier sale that failed the holding-period requirement.

Section 1031 does not apply to stock. A Section 1045 rollover may instead be relevant where its separate requirements are met.

The Active Business Requirement: 80% of Asset Value

At least 80% of assets by value must be used in one or more qualified active trades or businesses during substantially all of the holding period. This is an asset test, not an 80% gross-income or revenue test. Reasonably required working capital and qualifying investment funds expected to be used within two years for research or increased working capital can count, but after the corporation has existed for at least two years no more than 50% of its assets may qualify solely under the working-capital rule. Separate limits apply to portfolio securities and nonbusiness real estate.

For example, $1 million of operating revenue and $300,000 of investment income do not, by themselves, prove a failed 80% test. The analysis needs the values and uses of the assets over substantially all of the holding period.

Excluded Businesses Under IRC Section 1202(e)(3)

Section 1202(e)(3) excludes services in health, law, engineering, architecture, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services and brokerage services, plus any business whose principal asset is the reputation or skill of employees. The named service exclusions do not depend on that additional reputation-or-skill category. Banking, insurance, financing, leasing, investing, farming, specified extraction businesses, and hotels, motels, restaurants and similar businesses are also excluded. A software or manufacturing label does not establish eligibility; review the actual activities and asset use.

Leasing and the separate nonbusiness-real-estate test require careful review. Do not assume that all development businesses qualify or that every activity involving real estate is automatically the same statutory category.

C-Corporation vs. S-Corporation Planning: The QSBS Structuring Decision

Revoking an S election does not turn existing S corporation shares into QSBS and does not create a deemed new stock issuance. A genuine new original issuance by an eligible domestic C corporation must be analyzed separately. Incorporating an LLC taxed as a partnership or disregarded entity can involve qualifying original-issue C corporation stock in exchange for property, if all requirements are met. The qualifying holding period generally starts at that incorporation, and Section 1202(i) prevents pre-contribution appreciation from becoming excludable gain.

Model C corporation tax on retained earnings, shareholder tax on distributions, conversion costs and a plausible stock or asset sale. QSBS savings do not automatically outweigh those costs, and a simple S-election revocation is not a QSBS strategy.

The Rollover Opportunity: IRC Section 1045 and QSBS Reinvestment

Section 1045 may defer gain when a noncorporate taxpayer sells QSBS held for more than six months, elects rollover treatment, and buys replacement QSBS during the 60-day period beginning on the sale date. Full deferral generally requires reinvesting the amount realized, not just the gain. Deferred gain reduces replacement-stock basis; applicable holding-period tacking must be analyzed. A rollover does not automatically secure a later Section 1202 exclusion or increase its cap.

Rollover illustration: A taxpayer sells qualifying stock held more than six months for $8 million with $500,000 basis. If a valid Section 1045 election and $8 million timely replacement purchase fully defer $7.5 million of gain, replacement basis becomes $500,000. A later $25 million sale would have $24.5 million gain before selling costs. The later exclusion requires its own acquisition-date, tacking, basis and per-issuer analysis; the rollover does not promise exclusion of that entire gain.

Multi-Founder QSBS Structuring and Pro-Rata Holdings

Assume three unrelated noncorporate founders each invest $400,000 for equal original-issue shares acquired in 2015 and sell all shares in 2026 for a total $40 million, with no selling costs. Total basis is $1.2 million and total gain is $38.8 million, about $12,933,333 per founder.

If every requirement is met and no founder previously used the issuer limit, each founder's $10 million dollar ceiling exceeds 10 times their $400,000 basis. Each could exclude $10 million. Total excluded gain is $30 million and total taxable gain is $8.8 million. The cap is not one shared $10 million allowance for the entire corporation.

Recapture and Depreciation Recapture Exceptions

Section 1202 applies to qualifying shareholder stock gain, not the corporation's gain on selling assets. A corporate asset sale can trigger ordinary income, depreciation recapture and corporate income tax. Section 1202 does not shelter that corporate gain; any subsequent liquidating distribution needs a separate shareholder-level analysis.

Compare after-tax stock-sale and asset-sale proceeds, price, basis adjustments and transaction costs with counsel before agreeing to a letter of intent. A buyer's preferred structure may materially change the owner's result.

The 2025 Law: Separate Old and New Stock Lots

The current Section 1202 rules do not contain the previously described December 31, 2025 expiration. The 2025 law created acquisition-date-based tiers and raised the dollar and corporate asset limits for qualifying newer stock. Old and new lots must be tracked separately, including any tacked acquisition dates.

Stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025 must be held for more than five years. Qualifying stock acquired after September 27, 2010 and on or before July 4, 2025 can receive a 100% exclusion. For stock acquired after July 4, 2025, the exclusion is 50% after at least three years, 75% after at least four years, and 100% after at least five years. These percentages apply to eligible gain after the per-issuer limit, not automatically to all sale proceeds. Acquisition dates reflect applicable holding-period tacking rules.

The newer-stock $15 million base dollar limit is indexed for tax years beginning after 2026. Use the applicable published limit for the actual sale year rather than projecting a future indexed amount.

Key Takeaways for Business Owners

  • Use the greater of the remaining per-issuer dollar limit or 10 times qualifying basis, then apply the exclusion percentage.
  • Acquisition date controls holding-period tiers and the dollar limit; issuance date controls the gross-assets ceiling.
  • Revoking an S election does not turn existing shares into QSBS.
  • The 80% active-business requirement measures asset value, not gross income.
  • A late installment does not fix a sale before the required holding period.
  • Section 1045 requires more than six months of holding, an election and a timely replacement purchase.
  • Joint-return spouses do not automatically get two dollar limits.
  • Maintain original-issue, historical-asset, ownership and sale records before projecting savings.

The Bottom Line

Bring formation documents and tax elections, the cap table and each issuance agreement, option or convertible-instrument records, consideration and stock basis, historical balance sheets and contributed-property valuations, active-business records, redemption history, prior Section 1202 claims, trust or gift documents, state residency details, and the proposed sale timeline. Ask for a written lot-by-lot eligibility analysis and a stock-sale versus asset-sale comparison before relying on an exclusion.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Section 1202 qualified small business stock?

Section 1202 can exclude eligible gain on qualifying domestic C corporation stock. Stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025 requires more than five years; qualifying acquisitions after September 27, 2010 can receive 100% exclusion. Stock acquired after July 4, 2025 has 50%, 75% and 100% tiers at three, four and five years. The eligible-gain ceiling is the greater of the remaining $10 million or $15 million acquisition-date-based dollar limit (2026 amounts) or 10 times qualifying basis. Original issuance, active-business eligibility, stock-lot history and state tax must also be checked.

Can I use a management company to shift income to a C-Corp?

Yes, if the arrangement has substance. The corporation must actually provide services, the fee must be arm's length, and there must be a written agreement with supporting records. Section 482 permits the IRS to reallocate income between commonly controlled entities where pricing does not reflect economic reality.

Should real estate be held in a C-Corp?

Almost never. Appreciated property distributed out of a C corporation triggers gain at both the corporate and shareholder level, with no equivalent of the partnership rules permitting tax-free property distributions.

What happens if I convert a C-Corp back to an S-Corp?

The built-in gains tax of Section 1374 applies to gains that existed at conversion and are recognized within the following five-year period. This makes the C election harder to unwind than to make, which is why it should be modeled over a full holding period.

Before You Rely on a QSBS Exclusion

Bring formation documents and tax elections, the cap table and each issuance agreement, option or convertible-instrument records, consideration and stock basis, historical balance sheets and contributed-property valuations, active-business records, redemption history, prior Section 1202 claims, trust or gift documents, state residency details, and the proposed sale timeline. Ask for a written lot-by-lot eligibility analysis and a stock-sale versus asset-sale comparison before relying on an exclusion.

A rental or leasing business does not become eligible merely by converting to a C corporation; Section 1202(e) also limits nonbusiness real estate. A useful first engagement should identify which share lots may qualify, missing evidence, the remaining issuer limit, state differences and the proposed transaction structure. Confirm the written scope, responsible tax and legal advisers, timing and fees before proceeding. Request a QSBS eligibility review.
