Are Payments to a Retiring Partner Deductible to the Partnership?

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

Not automatically. If the partnership pays a retiring owner to liquidate the entire interest, Section 736 generally divides the payments between an interest in partnership property under Section 736(b) and other payments under Section 736(a). The property portion is a distribution, not a deductible expense. A Section 736(a) amount fixed without regard to partnership income is generally a guaranteed payment; an amount based on income is a distributive share. A five-year payment schedule or the label “retirement compensation” does not determine the tax result. The partnership and departing owner need one reconciled allocation before the buyout agreement and Form 1065 are finalized.

For an owner-led service firm with meaningful unbilled work, receivables, and goodwill, a wrong allocation can affect the entity's deductions, several continuing partners' K-1s, and the retiree's returns over multiple years. AE can review the agreement, asset valuation, outside basis, payment schedule, and proposed Form 1065/K-1 treatment before filing or correction.

First decide who is buying the interest

Section 736 addresses payments by the partnership in liquidation of a retiring partner's entire interest. A direct purchase by a continuing partner is an interest sale and is analyzed under the sale and Section 751 hot-asset rules, not automatically as a Section 736 redemption. Read the purchase agreement, identify the legal payer and source of cash, and reconcile the departing owner's liability relief. The Section 751 interest-sale guide covers that separate path. A funding contribution from continuing owners does not by itself answer who acquired the interest.

Separate Section 736(b) property payments from Section 736(a)

The amount paid in exchange for the retiring owner's interest in partnership property is generally a Section 736(b) distribution. It is not a guaranteed payment and does not produce a partnership expense deduction merely because cash leaves the business. For the recipient, distribution gain or loss depends on outside basis, the money distributed (including liability relief where applicable), the property received, and the distribution rules; it is not correct to call every Section 736(b) dollar “capital gain.”

Amounts outside the property category are Section 736(a). If determined with regard to partnership income, they are treated as the recipient's distributive share; if determined without regard to income, they are treated as guaranteed payments. The entity's deduction treatment and timing must follow that characterization rather than a blanket “all buyout payments are deductible” assumption. The retiree remains a partner for federal income tax purposes until the interest is completely liquidated. Review the retirement-year and later K-1s accordingly.

Test receivables, goodwill, and the type of firm

The special rule for a retiring partner's share of unrealized receivables or goodwill applies when both conditions are met: capital is not a material income-producing factor and the retiring owner was a general partner. A cash-method professional firm with earned but uncollected service fees is a common fact pattern, but the entity label alone is not the test. Under that rule, the receivable amount is not a Section 736(b) property payment. A reasonable goodwill payment specified in the partnership agreement is an exception to the goodwill exclusion; it can be treated as a property payment. A clause cannot simply rename uncollected fees as goodwill. Review the actual agreement and supportable values.

A worked allocation before a five-year payout

Assume a general partner is retiring from a qualifying service partnership for $900,000, paid as five $180,000 installments. A supported valuation attributes $450,000 to the owner's interest in partnership property, $250,000 to qualifying earned-but-uncollected receivables, and $200,000 to goodwill. If the agreement does not provide a reasonable goodwill payment, the initial classification worksheet points to $450,000 of Section 736(b) property payments and $450,000 of Section 736(a) payments. If it does provide a reasonable $200,000 goodwill payment, the worksheet may instead show $650,000 under Section 736(b) and $250,000 under Section 736(a). This is a classification illustration, not a calculation of the retiree's gain, the firm's deduction, or annual taxable income. Those require outside basis, liability changes, the allocation of each installment, whether Section 736(a) amounts depend on income, and return-year facts. Do not allocate every annual check by an assumed fixed percentage without a workpaper tied to the agreement.

What to decide before signing and filing

  1. Identify the transaction. Is the partnership redeeming the entire interest, or are continuing owners buying it directly? Preserve the agreement, payment sources, and any liability-release documents.
  2. Value the components. Obtain a supportable property valuation, unbilled-work and receivables aging, goodwill analysis, and the retiring owner's tax-basis and liability schedule.
  3. Apply the Section 736 tests. Determine whether capital materially produces income and whether the departing owner was a general partner. Read the goodwill provision as written.
  4. Map each return year. Reconcile the payment schedule to Section 736(a) distributive-share or guaranteed-payment treatment and Section 736(b) distributions. Coordinate Form 1065, all affected K-1s, and the retiree's outside-basis and cash-received records.
  5. Resolve downstream issues. Examine any Section 754/734(b) basis adjustment, continuing-owner allocations, state treatment, and estimated-tax consequences with the return preparer. Those are fact dependent.

Failure points that change the return

  • Treating a direct co-owner purchase as a redemption. The purchaser and legal form control which rule starts the analysis; interest-sale gain can also include Section 751 ordinary income.
  • Calling every check deductible. A Section 736(b) property payment is a distribution. An income-based Section 736(a) amount is a distributive share, not automatically a guaranteed-payment deduction.
  • Assuming all goodwill is Section 736(a). The service-firm/general-partner test and a reasonable agreement-provided goodwill payment matter.
  • Ignoring receivables and liabilities. Open work in process, unpaid fees, outside basis, and debt relief can move both the classification and the retiree's tax result.
  • Using one-year sale math for multi-year liquidation. The retiree may remain a partner until full liquidation; reconcile every year's K-1 and distribution ledger.

Documents to bring to a return review

Bring the signed partnership and retirement agreements and amendments; ownership and general-partner status; three years of Forms 1065 and K-1; tax-basis capital and outside-basis workpapers; liability allocations; the receivables and work-in-process aging; goodwill and other asset valuations; payment and interest schedules; and any Section 754 election or prior basis-adjustment records. If an owner has died, also obtain the estate's date-of-death valuation and basis workpapers. Death can change basis, but it does not automatically erase future Section 736(a) income or make every estate payment tax-free.

Primary sources and next step

The classification framework comes from IRS Publication 541, Liquidation at Partner's Retirement or Death, Internal Revenue Code Section 736, and the IRS Form 1065 instructions. This is a federal framework, not a conclusion about a particular agreement. Before changing the contract or claiming a deduction, ask a tax professional to reconcile the legal transaction, supported asset values, partnership and partner basis, and all affected returns. Frequently Asked Questions

Can a partnership deduct every payment to a retiring partner?

No. Section 736(b) payments for the partner's interest in partnership property are distributions, not deductible business expenses. A Section 736(a) amount determined without regard to partnership income is treated as a guaranteed payment; classification depends on the agreement and underlying assets.

Does spreading a partner buyout over five years make it deductible?

No. The payment period does not determine the Section 736(a) versus 736(b) classification. First identify who pays, the partner's property interest, qualifying receivables or goodwill, and whether the payment depends on partnership income.

Does a goodwill clause change a service-firm partner redemption?

It can. For a general partner in a partnership where capital is not a material income-producing factor, the Section 736(b) exception for goodwill does not apply to the extent the partnership agreement provides a reasonable goodwill payment. The valuation and facts still require review.
