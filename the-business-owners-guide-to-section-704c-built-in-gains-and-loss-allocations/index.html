Section 704(c) for Partnerships: Land, Cash and Built-In Gain

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

Can a partnership have a Section 704(c) allocation when it has no intangibles or depreciable assets? Yes. Depreciation is not the test. If a partner contributed nondepreciable land with fair market value different from its adjusted tax basis, the partnership must track that built-in gain or loss by property and use a reasonable Section 704(c) method when it allocates tax items. If everyone contributed only cash and there has been no book revaluation or other relevant property disparity, there may be no Section 704(c) layer to allocate. Review the contribution documents and books before putting a zero on Form 1065 or a partner's K-1. Treasury Regulation §1.704-3 defines the property-level disparity and allocation requirement.

Cash, land, and book-ups lead to different answers

  • Cash only: cash ordinarily enters at its face amount, with no book-versus-tax basis gap. Check whether the partnership already holds revalued assets or received other contributed property; a cash contribution alone does not prove the entire partnership has no Section 704(c) layers.
  • Appreciated or depreciated land: land has no annual depreciation, but a contribution with a book-tax gap creates Section 704(c) property. The disparity can remain unrecognized until a sale, taxable exchange, or certain distributions.
  • Depreciable property or intangibles: the same contribution test applies, and annual depreciation or amortization allocations can make the method choice consequential before a sale.
  • Partnership book-up or book-down: a permitted revaluation of property already owned by the partnership can create a reverse Section 704(c) layer even without a new noncash contribution.

The relevant question is whether a specific property's book value differs from its adjusted tax basis—not whether the asset appears in an intangible or depreciation account. A separate Section 704(b) capital-account issue or an agreement's special allocation is not automatically a Section 704(c) item. See AE's Section 704(b) guide for the economic-allocation side.

Built-In Gain and Loss: Property Contribution Mechanics

In a qualifying Section 721 contribution, the partnership generally takes the contributor's adjusted tax basis under Section 723, while Section 704(b) book capital reflects the property's fair market value. For example, land worth $1 million with $400,000 of tax basis brings a $600,000 built-in gain. If the partnership later sells that land for $1.2 million and there have been no other basis adjustments or special events, total tax gain is $800,000: the $600,000 precontribution layer is generally allocated to the contributing partner and the $200,000 postcontribution appreciation follows the applicable economic allocation. The land produced no depreciation deductions while held, but the Section 704(c) schedule was still needed. Character, liabilities, and later adjustments require their own review. IRS Publication 541 and Regulation §1.704-3 describe the framework.

Traditional Method vs. Remedial Method vs. Curative Method

The regulation generally recognizes the traditional method, traditional method with curative allocations, and remedial method as reasonable approaches; it does not make the traditional method an automatic answer whenever an agreement is silent. The partnership must use a reasonable method consistently for each property, and the overall combination must not shift precontribution tax consequences improperly. A nondepreciable land parcel may not generate annual depreciation disparities, but method selection can still matter at disposition or for a broader group of contributed assets. The ceiling rule can limit traditional-method tax allocations to actual partnership tax items; curative or remedial approaches address some book-tax distortions differently. Compare the agreement, prior filed returns, and property-level schedule before changing methods. Regulation §1.704-3(a)–(d) supplies the governing method rules.

Contribution Is Not a Deemed Future Sale

Section 721 generally provides nonrecognition for a qualifying property contribution. Section 704(c) preserves the precontribution book-tax difference for later allocations; it does not itself deem a sale or guarantee that no tax is triggered at contribution. Assumed liabilities, a disguised sale under Section 707, investment-company rules, or other facts can alter the result. If the partnership improves the contributed property in later years, maintain separate workpapers for the original layer and subsequent capital expenditures. If the partnership sells in year five, allocate the actual gain under the chosen reasonable method and then assess the partner-level consequences.

Partnership Distributions and Section 704(c) Adjustments

A distribution of contributed Section 704(c) property to a different partner within the statutory seven-year period can trigger recognition by the contributing partner under Section 704(c)(1)(B), measured under the applicable rules—not simply by the entire cash value distributed. A distribution of other property to a partner who earlier contributed built-in-gain property can raise Section 737; see the two-limit Section 737 distribution review before signing that deed. These rules are distinct from the later partnership sale example, and transactions involving debt, partnership-interest transfers, or related parties need specialized review. See the current partner K-1 instructions and IRS Publication 541 for the seven-year rule; some older regulatory examples still state the former five-year period.

Multiple Properties and Aggregate Contributions

Section 704(c) ordinarily applies property by property, with limited regulatory aggregation exceptions. A portfolio's $3 million net book-tax difference is not a substitute for schedules showing each parcel's value, basis, contributor, date, and chosen method. If one parcel has $2 million built-in gain and another has $1 million built-in loss, do not net them into a single $1 million parcel or automatically use gains on one asset to cure a disparity on the other. Built-in loss also has special statutory limitations protecting noncontributing partners. Reconcile any permitted grouping to Regulation §1.704-3(a)(2) before filing.

Tiered Partnerships and Pass-Through Allocation

In a tiered structure, first identify which partnership actually owns the contributed asset. A lower-tier partnership cannot sell land still held by the upper tier. If a transfer through tiers occurred, trace the original contribution, later transfer, substituted-basis property, ownership changes, and each tier's allocation method before describing who receives gain. The regulation has specific nonrecognition and tiered-partnership rules; a one-line “flow-through” assumption is not enough for the return workpaper.

Documentation and Schedule K-1 Reporting

Before Form 1065 is signed, assemble the contribution agreement, deeds, contemporaneous valuation, contributor tax-basis schedule, debt records, Section 704(b) capital accounts, property-level Section 704(c) rollforward, allocation-method workpaper, and prior K-1s. The Schedule K-1 instructions explain that item M identifies a current-year contribution of property with built-in gain or loss and an attached statement; item N reports each partner's beginning and ending net unrecognized Section 704(c) amount. Box 20 code AA reports specified current-year Section 704(c)-affected income or deduction items for non-publicly traded partnerships. A partner's total gain or loss still appears in the appropriate K-1 income or gain boxes. Do not treat item N as a current-year tax bill or as a substitute for property-level workpapers.

Worked Example: Land, Cash and Three Partners

A contributes nondepreciable land worth $5 million with $2 million of adjusted tax basis. B and C each contribute $2.5 million cash. Assume the agreement gives A 50% and B and C 25% each of postcontribution economics, the traditional method is reasonably used for this land, no debts or basis changes arise, and the land sells for $7 million. The partnership has $5 million tax gain ($7 million proceeds minus $2 million tax basis). The $3 million precontribution layer is allocated to A; the $2 million postcontribution increase is allocated $1 million to A and $500,000 each to B and C. A's total is $4 million, while B and C have $500,000 each. This illustrates allocation only—not the character, outside-basis, or individual tax result. If the agreement or later transactions differ, the computation changes.

What to resolve before filing or correcting a return

  1. Determine whether each noncash asset was contributed, purchased by the partnership, or merely revalued on its books; distinguish original from reverse Section 704(c).
  2. Reconcile valuation, tax basis, liabilities, and ownership percentages as of each contribution date; do not use a current appraisal as the historical contribution value.
  3. Choose and document a reasonable property-level allocation method, then reconcile it to the agreement and prior filed returns.
  4. Roll forward unrecognized gain or loss and test actual current-year tax items, including disposition, depreciation, and distributions; complete K-1 items M and N and box 20 code AA when applicable.
  5. If an already-filed Form 1065 or K-1 omitted the layer, determine whether the partnership must use an amended return, a superseding return, or a BBA administrative adjustment request. See AE's partnership correction guide; do not change a partner return in isolation.

The most common failure is equating “no depreciable assets” with “no Section 704(c).” The second is inventing a built-in-gain layer where partners contributed only cash and no book revaluation occurred. Both errors can distort a sale-year allocation or K-1. Federal rules and state conformity may differ; professional review should cover the actual agreement and full transaction sequence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do we need Section 704(c) allocations if the partnership owns only cash?

A cash-only partnership ordinarily has no contributed-property book-tax disparity to allocate under Section 704(c). Check for prior noncash contributions, partnership revaluations, and other special items before concluding the balance is zero.

Can Section 704(c) apply when the only contributed property is land?

Yes. Land is not depreciable, but contributed land is Section 704(c) property when its fair market value differs from the contributor's adjusted tax basis. The built-in difference generally matters when the partnership recognizes gain or loss on a later disposition.

Does contributing appreciated land cause an immediate taxable sale?

Generally, a qualifying Section 721 property contribution is not a sale merely because Section 704(c) tracks precontribution gain. Liabilities, disguised-sale rules, and other facts can change the contribution result.

Where does unrecognized Section 704(c) gain appear on a partner's K-1?

Schedule K-1 (Form 1065) item M identifies current-year contributed property with built-in gain or loss, item N reports a partner's beginning and ending net unrecognized Section 704(c) gain or loss, and box 20 code AA can report current-year tax items affected by Section 704(c). The partnership should retain property-level workpapers.
