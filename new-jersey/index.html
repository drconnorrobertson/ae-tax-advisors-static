Cost Segregation Study in New Jersey

New Jersey tax advisory for real estate investors and business owners | Updated August 2026

New Jersey pairs one of the highest personal rates in the country with an income tax structure that does not work the way the federal system does. Income is sorted into categories, and a loss in one category generally cannot offset income in another. That single rule changes the answer on many New Jersey cost segregation studies.

On the other side of the ledger, New Jersey's Business Alternative Income Tax is one of the better designed pass-through entity taxes in the country, and for owners with substantial New Jersey source business income it is usually the highest value election available.

How New Jersey Income Tax Interacts With Federal Strategy

The New Jersey gross income tax is graduated with a top rate of 10.75% on income above $1 million. There is no preferential treatment for long-term capital gains, so an appreciated property sale is taxed at ordinary New Jersey rates on the entire gain.

The corporation business tax is 9% for corporations with allocated net income above $100,000. New Jersey replaced its expiring corporate surtax with a Corporate Transit Fee of 2.5% on corporations with allocated taxable net income above $10 million, effective for tax years beginning on or after January 1, 2024, bringing the top corporate rate to 11.5% for the largest filers.

The structural feature that matters most is categorization. New Jersey's gross income tax assigns income to categories such as net profits from business, net gains from disposition of property, and net rental income. With limited exceptions, a loss in one category does not offset income in another, and New Jersey does not provide the general suspended loss carryforward that IRC Sec. 469 provides federally.

New Jersey Pass-Through Entity Tax

New Jersey enacted the Business Alternative Income Tax in 2019, effective for tax years beginning in 2020, and substantially improved it with legislation effective in 2023. Electing partnerships and S corporations pay a graduated entity-level tax on New Jersey source income, deduct it federally, and owners claim a refundable credit against their gross income tax.

The 2023 changes were significant. The tax base for partnerships was broadened so that resident partners are taxed on all income rather than only New Jersey source income, which increases the deduction available. The credit was made more usable, and the estimated payment rules were rationalized.

The election is annual and made on or before the due date of the return, which is more forgiving than California's June prepayment trap. Estimated payments are required during the year, and because the federal deduction follows the entity's payment, timing those payments inside the tax year is essential to landing the deduction in the right year.

For a New Jersey owner with $1 million of pass-through income, BAIT converts roughly $85,000 to $107,000 of state tax from a capped itemized deduction into a fully deductible entity-level expense. At a 37% federal rate that is $30,000 to $40,000 of federal tax saved for the cost of making an election correctly and paying on time.

New Jersey Depreciation Conformity

For corporation business tax purposes New Jersey decouples from IRC Sec. 168(k). Corporations add back the federal bonus deduction and compute New Jersey depreciation without regard to the special allowance. New Jersey also limits Section 179 for corporation business tax purposes rather than following the full federal limit.

For gross income tax purposes, New Jersey's treatment of depreciation follows federal rules in computing net profits and net rental income, but the category system controls whether the resulting deduction is usable. That is the practical constraint. The deduction may be computed the same way and still produce no New Jersey benefit because there is no income in that category to absorb it.

This is the same trap Pennsylvania investors face, and it catches New Jersey investors for the same reason: they assume that because the federal analysis produces a loss usable against wage income, New Jersey will follow. It does not. New Jersey does not allow a net rental loss to offset wages on the gross income tax return.

Cost Segregation Considerations Specific to New Jersey

The dominant New Jersey point is the category rule. A New Jersey investor with one rental property and large wage income should expect the state-side value of a cost segregation study to be near zero, because the rental loss has no New Jersey income in its category to offset and New Jersey does not carry it forward the way federal law does. Size the decision on the federal numbers.

The corollary is that New Jersey investors with multiple properties are in a better position. Rental income and loss within the category can be netted, so an investor with a portfolio can use an accelerated deduction on one property against rental income from others. Portfolio investors get New Jersey value where single property owners generally do not.

The third point is disposition. Net gains from disposition of property is its own category, and a large gain on sale can be a substantial New Jersey tax event with limited ability to shelter it using losses generated elsewhere. Planning the sale year, including whether a 1031 exchange makes sense, matters more in New Jersey than in a state with general loss netting.

The fourth is that New Jersey's high rate makes the BAIT election the more important lever for most business owners. If you have to choose where to spend planning attention in New Jersey, the pass-through entity tax election typically produces more certain value than the state-side portion of a depreciation strategy.

New Jersey Cost Segregation and Tax Questions

Does New Jersey allow bonus depreciation?

For corporation business tax purposes, no. New Jersey requires an addback of the federal IRC Sec. 168(k) bonus deduction and recomputes depreciation without the special allowance, and it limits Section 179 relative to the federal amount. For gross income tax purposes the computation follows federal rules, but the category system governs whether the deduction is usable.

Why can't I use my New Jersey rental loss against my wages?

New Jersey's gross income tax assigns income to categories and generally does not permit a loss in one category to offset income in another, and it does not provide the general suspended loss carryforward that IRC Sec. 469 provides federally. A net rental loss therefore does not reduce wage income on the New Jersey return, and an unusable loss is frequently lost rather than carried forward.

What is the New Jersey BAIT and is it worth electing?

The Business Alternative Income Tax is New Jersey's elective pass-through entity tax. The entity pays a graduated tax on New Jersey income, deducts it federally, and owners claim a refundable credit. For owners with substantial pass-through income it converts state tax from a capped itemized deduction into a fully deductible business expense, which is typically the largest state-level planning item available in New Jersey.

Is cost segregation worth it in New Jersey?

It depends on your portfolio. The federal benefit is unaffected and usually justifies the study on its own. The New Jersey benefit is real for investors with multiple rental properties, because losses net within the rental category, and close to zero for an investor with a single rental and large wage income.

What is New Jersey's corporate tax rate?

9% for corporations with allocated net income above $100,000, plus a 2.5% Corporate Transit Fee on corporations with allocated taxable net income above $10 million for tax years beginning on or after January 1, 2024, bringing the top combined rate to 11.5% for the largest filers.
