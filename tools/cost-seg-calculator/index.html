What Is a Cost Segregation Calculator?

A cost segregation calculator is a tool that estimates how much you could save in taxes by reclassifying components of your property into shorter depreciation categories. Under the Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 168, different types of property are assigned different recovery periods. Most buildings are depreciated over 27.5 years (residential rental property) or 39 years (nonresidential real property). A cost segregation study identifies components of the building that qualify for accelerated depreciation over 5, 7, or 15 years instead.

This calculator uses the industry-standard 35% reclassification formula. It estimates that roughly 35% of a property's purchase price can be moved from the long-life structural category into shorter-life asset classes. When paired with 100% first-year bonus depreciation, which the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) has now made permanent, the entire reclassified amount can be deducted in the tax year the property is placed in service.

How the 35% Reclassification Formula Works

The 35% formula is not arbitrary. It reflects decades of engineering-based cost segregation studies across thousands of properties. On average, approximately 35% of a building's cost is attributable to components that fall under shorter MACRS recovery periods. These components include items such as:

  • 5-year property: Appliances, carpeting, cabinetry, decorative lighting, window treatments, and certain electrical components dedicated to specific equipment.
  • 7-year property: Office furniture, specialty fixtures, and movable partitions.
  • 15-year property: Land improvements including parking lots, sidewalks, landscaping, fencing, drainage systems, and exterior signage.

The remaining 65% stays classified as structural real property at either the 27.5-year or 39-year recovery period, depending on the property type and its tax treatment.

Why Property Type Matters

The distinction between short-term rentals (STRs), long-term rentals (LTRs), and commercial properties is important for two reasons.

First, the baseline recovery period differs. Under IRC Section 168(c), residential rental property uses a 27.5-year recovery period, while nonresidential real property (including commercial buildings and certain STR properties) uses a 39-year period. A longer baseline recovery period means that more depreciation is being deferred under the standard schedule, so the benefit of accelerating it is proportionally greater.

Second, the tax treatment of losses varies. For short-term rental properties where the average rental period is 7 days or fewer, the IRS treats the activity as a business rather than a passive rental activity under IRC Section 469(c)(2). This distinction can allow STR owners who materially participate to use depreciation losses against ordinary W-2 or business income, a strategy often referred to as the STR tax loophole.

The Impact of 100% Bonus Depreciation Under the OBBBA

Prior to the OBBBA, bonus depreciation was scheduled to phase down by 20% per year starting in 2023. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act reversed this phasedown and restored 100% first-year bonus depreciation permanently for qualifying property placed in service after the enactment date. This applies to the reclassified personal property and land improvements identified in a cost segregation study.

For property owners, this means the full 35% of the purchase price that gets reclassified can now be deducted entirely in Year 1. On a $1,000,000 property, that translates to a $350,000 depreciation deduction in the first year alone, rather than spreading it across 5, 7, or 15 years. The tax savings at a combined federal and state rate can be substantial.

When Cost Segregation Makes Sense

Cost segregation is most valuable for property owners who have significant taxable income to offset. The depreciation deduction created by a cost seg study reduces taxable income, which reduces the tax owed. The strategy works especially well for:

  • High-income W-2 earners who own STR properties and qualify for the material participation exception under IRC Section 469.
  • Business owners who purchase commercial buildings for their operations or as investment properties.
  • Rental property investors with a portfolio of residential rentals who qualify as real estate professionals under IRC Section 469(c)(7).
  • Existing property owners who missed cost segregation in prior years and want to capture the benefit retroactively through IRS Form 3115.

If you have already placed a property in service without performing a cost segregation study, the IRS allows you to file Form 3115 (Application for Change in Accounting Method) to claim the cumulative missed depreciation as a single "catch-up" deduction in the current year. This is a Section 481(a) adjustment that does not require amending prior year returns.

What Happens After the Calculator

This calculator provides a reliable estimate, but a formal cost segregation study goes much deeper. A professional study involves an engineering analysis of the property's construction, detailed component identification, and a report that meets IRS Audit Techniques Guide (ATG) standards. The resulting documentation supports the reclassifications on your tax return and provides the backup needed in the event of an IRS examination.

Frequently Asked Questions

How accurate is this cost segregation calculator?

This calculator provides a reliable estimate based on the industry-standard 35% reclassification formula. Actual results from a formal cost segregation study may vary depending on property-specific factors such as construction materials, improvements, and land value allocation. The calculator is designed to give property owners a strong starting point before commissioning a full engineering-based study.

What is the 35% reclassification formula used in cost segregation?

The 35% formula is a widely used benchmark in the cost segregation industry. It estimates that approximately 35% of a property's purchase price can be reclassified from long-life structural components (27.5 or 39 years) into shorter-life asset categories (5, 7, and 15 years). This reclassification allows property owners to accelerate depreciation deductions and reduce taxable income significantly in Year 1.

Does the OBBBA make 100% bonus depreciation permanent?

Yes. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) restored and made permanent 100% first-year bonus depreciation for qualifying property. This means the entire reclassified amount from a cost segregation study can be deducted in the year the property is placed in service, with no phasedown schedule.

What types of properties qualify for cost segregation?

Most income-producing properties qualify for a cost segregation study. This includes short-term rentals (Airbnb, VRBO), long-term rental properties, commercial office buildings, retail spaces, warehouses, hotels, restaurants, medical facilities, and mixed-use properties. The property must be used for business or investment purposes under IRC Section 167.

Is there a minimum property value for cost segregation to be worthwhile?

While there is no legal minimum, most tax professionals recommend cost segregation for properties valued at $250,000 or more. Below that threshold, the study fees may outweigh the incremental tax benefit. However, the actual breakeven depends on your marginal tax rate, property type, and state tax situation.

Can I do a cost segregation study on a property I already own?

Yes. If you placed your property in service in a prior year and did not perform a cost segregation study at that time, you can file IRS Form 3115 (Application for Change in Accounting Method) to claim the missed depreciation as a one-time catch-up deduction. This is a powerful strategy for existing property owners who want to recapture years of unclaimed accelerated depreciation without amending prior returns.

How does property type affect the cost segregation calculation?

Property type determines the baseline recovery period. Long-term residential rentals use a 27.5-year recovery period under IRC Section 168(c). Short-term rentals and commercial properties use a 39-year recovery period. A longer baseline recovery period means a greater spread between the original depreciation schedule and the accelerated schedule, which can result in larger Year 1 deductions after reclassification.
