Cost Segregation Study in Virginia

Virginia made two significant changes in 2026 that pull in opposite directions for real estate investors. It made its pass-through entity tax permanent, which is unambiguously good. And it replaced rolling conformity to the Internal Revenue Code with a fixed conformity date, which adds a layer of divergence that Virginia taxpayers now have to track deliberately.

Virginia's rate structure also deserves attention because it is misleading at a glance. The top rate is only 5.75%, which sounds moderate. But it is reached at $17,000 of taxable income, which means essentially every real estate investor and business owner in Virginia is paying the top rate on every marginal dollar. Virginia functions as a flat 5.75% state for anyone this analysis applies to.

How Virginia Income Tax Interacts With Federal Strategy

Virginia's individual income tax is nominally graduated with four brackets, but the top bracket begins at $17,000 of Virginia taxable income. For any investor or business owner, the effective rate on additional income is 5.75%.

Virginia applies no preferential rate to long-term capital gains. Ordinary income, Section 1245 recapture, and unrecaptured Section 1250 gain are all taxed at 5.75%.

The consequence for cost segregation modeling is that Virginia's addback is easy to price. Every dollar of bonus depreciation added back costs 5.75 cents, with no bracket analysis and no phase-out interactions to model. On a $500,000 addback that is $28,750 in the placed-in-service year, against a federal deduction worth up to $185,000.

Virginia Pass-Through Entity Tax

Virginia adopted an elective pass-through entity tax effective for tax year 2021, and it was originally scheduled to sunset. The 2026 Amendments to the 2025 Appropriation Act removed the sunset provision, making the Virginia PTET permanent as of February 2026.

The rate is 5.75%, matching the top individual rate. Owners receive a refundable Virginia credit for their share of the entity-level tax. The election is made with a timely filed return including extensions, and Virginia also permits the election to be evidenced through estimated or extension payments, which is more forgiving than most states.

Permanence matters for Virginia owners in the same way it does for Illinois owners. Under a sunset, the PTET was an annual decision requiring re-evaluation. Now it is a structural default for profitable Virginia pass-throughs, and multi-year planning around it is worth doing.

Virginia also has a large population of federal contractors, consultants, and professionals operating through S corporations in Northern Virginia. For that group the PTET is frequently the single largest recoverable federal deduction available, and it is independent of any real estate strategy.

Virginia Depreciation Conformity

Virginia historically used fixed-date conformity, moved to rolling conformity, and has now moved back. During the 2026 General Assembly session, the 2026 Amendments to the 2025 Appropriation Act replaced Virginia's rolling conformity to the Internal Revenue Code with a fixed conformity date of December 31, 2025.

Separately and independently, Virginia continues to deconform from bonus depreciation. If depreciation was included in the computation of federal taxable income and any depreciable asset received the special bonus depreciation deduction for federal purposes in any taxable year from 2001 through 2025, depreciation must be recomputed for Virginia purposes as if those assets had never received the bonus allowance.

The two changes compound. Fixed-date conformity means Virginia will not automatically pick up future federal changes, so any new federal depreciation provision enacted after December 31, 2025 requires affirmative Virginia legislation to apply. Combined with the standing bonus deconformity, Virginia taxpayers should assume divergence is the default and check rather than assume on any federal provision enacted going forward.

Virginia does conform to the federal Section 179 limits, which provides a partial path to a Virginia deduction on qualifying property in the same way Georgia's conformity does, though Virginia's higher rate makes the analysis somewhat different.

Cost Segregation Considerations Specific to Virginia

Virginia planning centers on three points.

First, the reclassification is respected even though the bonus is not. Virginia disallows the special allowance, not the class life. Five, seven, and fifteen-year property identified in a study recovers over those lives for Virginia purposes on regular MACRS. Across a ten-year hold at a flat 5.75%, that state benefit is real and it accrues steadily.

Second, Section 179 is the Virginia workaround worth examining. Because Virginia conforms to the federal Section 179 limits, property that qualifies for Section 179 can be expensed for Virginia purposes as well as federally. As in Georgia, this requires the cost segregation report to flag Section 179 eligibility asset by asset rather than only reporting MACRS class lives. Section 179 is capped at business taxable income and cannot create a loss, so it is a partial rather than complete solution, but at a 5.75% rate the dollars are worth pursuing.

Third, the Northern Virginia market profile drives the property mix. Data centers, flex industrial, and professional office in Loudoun, Fairfax, and Prince William counties carry unusually heavy specialty electrical, mechanical, and cooling infrastructure. These are the highest-reclassification asset categories in commercial real estate, frequently exceeding 40% on purpose-built facilities. Owners of that property type in Virginia should be running studies as a matter of course, and should be paying close attention to whether their provider has the engineering depth to properly segregate mechanical and electrical systems rather than defaulting to rules of thumb.

Fourth, non-resident owners of Virginia property should note that Virginia sources rental income and gain from Virginia real property to Virginia, and requires a non-resident return. The Virginia depreciation recomputation applies to that non-resident filing as well, so an out-of-state owner still carries the dual-basis tracking burden for the Virginia portion of the portfolio.

Virginia Cost Segregation and Tax Questions

Does Virginia allow bonus depreciation?

No. Virginia continues to deconform from bonus depreciation. If any depreciable asset received the federal special bonus depreciation deduction in any year from 2001 through 2025, depreciation must be recomputed for Virginia purposes as if the bonus had never been claimed. The reclassification into shorter class lives is still respected, so Virginia recovery follows regular MACRS.

What changed with Virginia conformity in 2026?

The 2026 Amendments to the 2025 Appropriation Act replaced Virginia's rolling conformity to the Internal Revenue Code with a fixed conformity date of December 31, 2025. Virginia will no longer automatically adopt future federal changes, so new federal depreciation provisions require affirmative Virginia legislation to apply.

Is the Virginia PTET permanent now?

Yes. The 2026 Amendments to the 2025 Appropriation Act removed the sunset provision, making the Virginia elective pass-through entity tax permanent as of February 2026. The rate is 5.75% and owners receive a refundable Virginia credit. Virginia permits the election to be evidenced through estimated or extension payments, which is more forgiving than most states.

Does Virginia conform to Section 179?

Yes, Virginia conforms to the federal Section 179 limits. That creates a partial workaround: property qualifying for Section 179 can be expensed for Virginia purposes as well as federally, unlike bonus depreciation. This requires the cost segregation report to flag Section 179 eligibility asset by asset, which most standard reports do not do.

Why does Virginia's 5.75% top rate matter more than it looks like it should?

Because it starts at just $17,000 of taxable income. Virginia is nominally graduated but functions as a flat 5.75% state for any investor or business owner. Every marginal dollar of a bonus depreciation addback is taxed at 5.75%, with no bracket analysis and no phase-outs to model.
