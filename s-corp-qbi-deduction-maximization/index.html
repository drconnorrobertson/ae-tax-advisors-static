S-Corp QBI Deduction Maximization

Model reasonable compensation, retirement contributions, qualified property, and the 2026 QBI limits together before changing payroll.

Book a Call

By AE Tax Advisors Team · Billings, Montana · Updated November 6, 2026

An S-corp QBI review starts with reasonable compensation and the owner's taxable income, not a target salary ratio. Wages can reduce qualified business income while supporting a wage limit that applies only as income rises. Qualified-property UBIA, service-business restrictions, retirement contributions, and the overall taxable-income cap can change the answer.

The 2026 Thresholds and Phase-In Rules

Use taxable income before the QBI deduction, not revenue, gross profit, or QBI alone. For tax years beginning in 2026, the threshold and end of the phase-in range are:

  • Married filing jointly: $403,500 to $553,500, a $150,000 range.
  • Married filing separately: $201,775 to $276,775, a $75,000 range.
  • All other returns, including single and head of household: $201,750 to $276,750, a $75,000 range.

At or below the threshold, the W-2 wage/UBIA limit and SSTB restriction do not apply. Within the range, the wage/property limit phases in and only an applicable percentage of SSTB QBI, wages, and UBIA is counted. At the upper endpoint, the non-SSTB wage/property limit is fully effective and the SSTB applicable percentage is zero. The overall taxable-income limitation still matters. These are the 2026 amounts in Revenue Procedure 2025-32, section 4.26.

For a non-SSTB above the phase-in range, the business component generally is the lesser of 20% of QBI or the greater of 50% of properly allocable W-2 wages and 25% of those wages plus 2.5% of UBIA of qualified property. UBIA means unadjusted basis immediately after acquisition. After business loss netting and any qualified REIT/PTP component, the regular deduction is generally capped at 20% of the amount remaining after subtracting net capital gain, including qualified dividends, from taxable income before the QBI deduction. A deduction reduces taxable income; it is not a dollar-for-dollar tax credit.

Beginning in 2026, Section 199A(i) separately provides a $400 minimum deduction for an applicable taxpayer with at least $1,000 of aggregate QBI from active qualified trades or businesses. Active means material participation under Section 469. This limited floor does not make an excluded SSTB a qualified business or remove other eligibility requirements. The examples here address the regular calculation, not that minimum.

Why Two-Sevenths Is Not a Salary Recommendation

Reasonable compensation for services is a legal constraint, not a number chosen to fit a deduction. Shareholder wages are taxable compensation, while pass-through profit is generally taxable whether or not cash is distributed. Increasing salary shifts income between those categories and adds relevant employment-tax costs; it does not automatically save income tax.

In a deliberately simplified non-SSTB model, let P be profit before the owner's wages and W be those wages. Assume no other wages, payroll deductions, UBIA, phase-in, losses, retirement contributions, or taxable-income constraint. The regular business component is the lesser of 20% × (P − W) and 50% × W. The lines cross at W = 2P/7.

For P = $700,000, W = $200,000 leaves $500,000 of QBI and both tests at $100,000. At W = $150,000, the component is $75,000; at W = $250,000, it is $90,000. This is a deduction-only illustration, not the total-tax optimum. Employee wages, employer payroll tax, Social Security wage limits, Medicare, benefits, and actual compensation requirements change the result.

Zero Wages Do Not Always Mean Zero Capacity

Assume a qualifying non-SSTB owner is above the phase-in range, has $400,000 of QBI, no allocable W-2 wages, and $2,000,000 of eligible UBIA. The wage/property limit is $50,000, not zero. The tentative business component is the lesser of $80,000 and $50,000: $50,000, before the overall cap and other adjustments. This does not permit an S-corp shareholder who performs services to skip required reasonable compensation.

Qualified property is depreciable tangible property held by and available for use in the business at year-end, used at some point during the year to produce QBI, and still within its Section 199A depreciable period. Land does not qualify. That period ends on the later of 10 years after first placed in service or the last day of the last full year of the applicable recovery period, determined without ADS. It is not a test of whether more than one year of useful life remains.

UBIA is generally measured before depreciation, including bonus depreciation and Section 179, subject to the detailed acquisition and allocation rules. Ordinary calendar-year UBIA is not prorated by purchase month: eligible equipment placed in service in January or December and held at year-end can count for that tax year. Buying equipment without placing it in service is not enough, and short-year, transfer, and anti-abuse rules can alter the analysis. See Treasury Regulation 1.199A-2.

Specified Service Businesses and Related Rentals

SSTBs include defined services in health, law, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, and securities brokerage, plus specified investing, trading, and dealing activities. Engineering and architecture are excluded from the listed service fields. Real estate brokerage and direct real-property management are not automatically SSTBs under the securities-brokerage or investment-management categories.

The separate reputation or skill category is narrow: income from endorsements, licensing an individual's identity, or paid appearances. A business does not become an SSTB merely because its owner is skilled or well known. Apply the actual definitions and any de minimis rules in Treasury Regulation 1.199A-5.

A separate rental or management company is not a workaround for the SSTB limits. Under Treasury Regulation 1.199A-5(c)(2), where there is 50% or more common ownership, including applicable direct, indirect, and related-party attribution, the portion of a business providing property or services to the commonly owned SSTB is treated as a separate SSTB with respect to the related parties. Real contracts, fair-market rent, and genuine services do not switch off this rule.

For example, a commonly owned building company leasing the entire building to the owners' law practice falls under the rule. If part is leased to unrelated tenants, analyze that unrelated portion separately. Rental qualification and the Section 469 self-rental rule are separate questions; neither overrides the Section 199A related-SSTB restriction.

When Aggregation Can Combine Wages and Property

Aggregation under Treasury Regulation 1.199A-4 is optional and requires more than owning several entities. The same person or group must own at least 50% of each business, directly or by the applicable attribution rules, for a majority of the tax year including its last day. Items must be reported for tax years ending on the same date, and none of the aggregated businesses can be an SSTB. At least two of these three operational links must exist:

  • The businesses provide the same or customarily offered-together products or services.
  • They share facilities or significant centralized business elements, such as personnel, accounting, or purchasing.
  • They operate in coordination with, or reliance on, one another.

Document the qualifying facts and annual disclosures, including Schedule B (Form 8995-A) when required. Continue an aggregation consistently in later years while it qualifies. Changes in facts can require a new analysis, and a newly created or acquired qualifying business may be added. It is not freely reversible, but it is not an unconditional forever election requiring IRS consent for every factual change.

How Retirement Contributions Affect Both Tests

Distinguish an S-corp employee's elective salary deferral from an employer contribution. A traditional employee 401(k) deferral generally lowers the employee's federal taxable wages but is part of gross salary already deducted by the S-corp, not a second business deduction. It generally remains within the Section 199A W-2 wage definition under the permitted calculation methods. A Roth elective deferral does not lower current federal taxable income.

An S-corp employer retirement contribution generally reduces pass-through profit and QBI. It is not also deducted a second time by the shareholder on the individual return. Deductible self-employed retirement contributions, health insurance, and the deductible part of self-employment tax attributable to a sole proprietorship or partnership can reduce that owner's QBI. Lower taxable income may improve phase-in treatment even when QBI falls, so compare the complete return, plan limits, staff costs, and cash commitments.

What to Bring to a QBI and Salary Review

Bring the current profit-and-loss statement, prior return and QBI loss carryforwards, ownership chart, payroll and W-2 records, reasonable-compensation support, asset register with placed-in-service dates and UBIA, and retirement-plan documents. Compare the regular deduction, federal and state taxes, payroll costs, and after-tax cash across defensible scenarios. Confirm the actual scope and cost of any advisory work before proceeding.

Key Takeaways

  • Use 2026 taxable income before the QBI deduction to locate the phase-in range.
  • Zero wages can still leave capacity from 2.5% of eligible UBIA; reasonable compensation remains required.
  • Two-sevenths is a simplified deduction crossing point, not a total-tax salary target.
  • The related-SSTB rule applies at 50% or more common ownership even with genuine contracts.
  • Model employee deferrals and employer retirement contributions separately, without a double deduction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do S-corp wages increase or decrease the QBI deduction?

Deductible wages reduce business profit and QBI; shareholder wages are not QBI to the recipient. Properly allocable W-2 wages may also support the wage limit when it applies. The overall result depends on the phase-in range, qualified-property UBIA, SSTB status, and the taxable-income cap.

What is the optimal wage level for QBI purposes?

Two-sevenths of pre-wage profit is only the crossing point in a simplified model with no other wages, payroll expense, UBIA, phase-in, or other deductions, and a fully applicable 50% wage limit. It maximizes that simplified QBI component, not total tax savings. Reasonable compensation and the full return control.

Can an S-corp owner in a service business still claim QBI?

An SSTB can produce eligible QBI at or below the taxable-income threshold. Within the phase-in range only an applicable percentage of its QBI, wages, and UBIA is considered, and the SSTB percentage is zero at the upper endpoint. A commonly owned rental or management entity does not automatically escape these rules.

Does paying wages to a spouse help the wage limitation?

Reasonable wages for genuine services may count if properly allocable and timely reported. They also reduce QBI and can create payroll taxes and retirement-plan obligations. A spouse does not need to own the business to be an employee. Count only qualifying wages, without duplication across businesses.

Should the deduction change how compensation is set?

Compare defensible compensation scenarios after documenting duties, hours, and market pay. QBI can inform the comparison, but it cannot replace reasonable compensation, justify unsupported wages, or establish that a larger deduction leaves the owner with more cash.
