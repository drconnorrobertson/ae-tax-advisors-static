2027 Tax Deadlines and Remaining 2026 Payments

Key federal dates for calendar-year taxpayers. Use the sections below to separate a return-filing deadline, a tax-payment deadline and a planning cutoff; state, disaster-relief and special-taxpayer rules can differ.

When are taxes due in 2027?

April 15, 2027 is the standard federal deadline for most individuals to file their 2026 return and pay the balance due. Calendar-year partnerships and S corporations generally file by March 15, 2027. The final 2026 individual estimated payment is due January 15, 2027. A filing extension does not extend payment.

Confirm the individual filing deadline with the IRS. Check the IRS announcement for the start of electronic filing; it is a different date from the April deadline.

How to Use This Calendar

These tables assume a calendar tax year. Fiscal-year taxpayers should use the applicable form instructions and IRS Form 7004 due-date chart. Do not shift every date by the same number of months: return types and exceptions differ.

Weekend and legal-holiday rules can move filing and payment due dates. The tables summarize standard federal dates; fiscal-year returns, disaster postponements and special filing rules require a separate check. Use IRS Publication 509 and the instructions for the actual return.

For most calendar-year individual returns for tax year 2026, the federal filing and payment deadline is April 15, 2027. A filing extension generally moves the return deadline to October 15, 2027, not the payment deadline. The IRS has published the April deadline; check its filing-season announcements for when electronic acceptance opens rather than assuming an opening date from a prior year.

Extensions, Penalties, and What They Cost

An extension moves the filing deadline, never the payment deadline. Form 4868 gives individuals six additional months to file, Form 7004 does the same for business returns, and Form 8868 covers exempt organizations. Tax owed remains due on the original date, and interest under IRC Section 6601 runs from that date at the federal short-term rate plus three percentage points, compounded daily.

Filing and paying are separate duties. Failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, late-deposit and information-return penalties use different bases, rates and exceptions. File a valid extension when eligible and review payment options rather than leaving the return unfiled because cash is short. Dollar minimums change by the year a return is required to be filed.

For W-2 and 1099 penalties, check the filing year, how late the form is, the business-size cap and whether a separate payee-statement failure applies. Use the current IRS information-return penalty table; a flat historical per-form estimate can misstate the exposure.

Partnership and S corporation penalties can be assessed per partner or shareholder for each month or partial month of delinquency, even without entity-level income tax due. The applicable amount is inflation-adjusted; see the IRS CP162C guidance and the return-year instructions before calculating a penalty.

Penalty relief depends on the penalty type and the facts. Reasonable cause and First Time Abate are not interchangeable, and not every penalty qualifies. Keep proof of filing, payments, the extension and the facts supporting any relief request.

Year-End Planning Cutoffs Worth Calendaring

Separate legal deadlines from practical processing cutoffs. A custodian, payroll processor, actuary or charity may need documents or funds earlier than the statutory date. Confirm its operational deadline before relying on a last-day transaction.

Property placed in service. Bonus depreciation and Section 179 require the asset to be placed in service, meaning ready and available for its intended use, by December 31. A cost segregation study can be completed after year end for a property placed in service during the year, but the property itself must have been in service. This is the constraint that drives fourth-quarter real estate closings.

Retirement plans. Adoption, employee deferral elections, deposits and employer deduction deadlines are different. Qualifying employer-funded plans may be adopted by the return deadline, including extensions. A narrow first-year rule also permits certain sole proprietors with no other employees to make deferrals by the individual filing deadline without extensions. Get the plan administrator to confirm the rule for your plan; actuarial and employee-notice work can require earlier action.

Roth conversions. A conversion must be completed by December 31 to count for that year. There is no extension and, since 2018, no recharacterization of conversions.

Clearing pre-tax IRA balances. The pro-rata rule for a backdoor Roth is measured on December 31 balances, so a rollover into an employer plan must settle before year end.

Required minimum distributions. Due December 31, except for the first RMD year, which may be delayed to April 1 of the following year at the cost of taking two distributions in that year.

Charitable contributions. Cash gifts must be paid, and appreciated securities must be transferred and settled, by December 31. Donor advised fund contributions are deductible when the fund receives them, which makes them useful for December decisions.

Capital gains and losses. For ordinary securities sales, the trade date generally determines the sale year; do not substitute settlement date. Short sales and other transactions can differ. Review wash sales across relevant accounts and the 30-day period before and after a loss sale, not only the December statement.

Entity and payroll adjustments. Owner compensation true-ups, S-Corporation shareholder health insurance added to W-2 wages, and increased withholding to cure a quarterly estimate shortfall all require payroll to be run before year end.
