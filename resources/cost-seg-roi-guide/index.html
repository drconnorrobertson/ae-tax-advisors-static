Cost Segregation ROI Guide: Model the Benefit Before You Buy

A study can change when you deduct a property's costs. Decide whether that timing benefit is worth the fee by checking the assets, the deductions you can use and your expected exit.

What Is a Cost Segregation Study?

A cost segregation study identifies separately depreciable assets and allocates supported costs among land, the building, land improvements and personal property. Some assets may qualify for shorter recovery periods, such as 5, 7 or 15 years, rather than the building's 27.5-year or 39-year period under the general depreciation system. Moving deductions earlier may improve cash flow if those deductions can be used.

Classification depends on the asset's function, use and supporting facts. General building systems can remain structural components; a label such as flooring, cabinetry, plumbing or electrical work does not automatically establish a shorter life. A study should reconcile its allocations to depreciable basis, excluding land, and explain its classification support.

See the IRS Cost Segregation Audit Technique Guide, chapters 3 and 4, Publication 946 and Publication 527 on land and building basis.

Compare Property Facts, Not Promised Returns by Type

No property category guarantees a reclassification percentage or a profitable study. The share of eligible depreciable basis, excluding land, must come from the actual assets and supported allocation. There is no universal purchase-price cutoff.

Under GDS, residential rental buildings generally use 27.5 years and nonresidential buildings generally use 39 years. The residential definition considers income from dwelling units and excludes certain transient-use establishments. ADS can require different recovery periods. These depreciation rules are separate from the Section 469 average-stay and material-participation rules. See Publication 946, chapter 4 and Publication 925; also review STR versus LTR tax treatment.

A Hypothetical ROI Model: Gross Benefit Is Not Incremental Savings

Assume $500,000 of depreciable basis after excluding land, with $100,000 assigned to eligible components. That is an assumed 20% of depreciable basis, not a typical reclassification percentage. Assume those components qualify for 100% federal bonus depreciation, the deduction is fully usable, and a constant 37% federal marginal rate applies to the entire difference. No state benefit is assumed.

For this arithmetic example only, assume the no-study deduction on those same components would be $2,500 in the comparison year. This is an illustrative baseline input, not a MACRS rate or a conclusion about any property. A preparer must calculate the actual baseline using the asset class, method, convention, dates and prior deductions.

Illustration only: same assets, same year, fully usable deductions
StepCalculation
Gross tax effect on selected components$100,000 × 37% = $37,000
Incremental deduction over baseline$100,000 − $2,500 = $97,500
Incremental current-year tax effect$97,500 × 37% = $36,075
After the assumed $3,000 study fee$36,075 − $3,000 = $33,075

The $3,000 fee is an example, not an AE quote. The $33,075 figure is before implementation costs and any tax treatment of fees; it is not net economic ROI. If the incremental deduction is fully suspended, its current-year tax effect may instead be $0 while the fee is still paid. Tax brackets, other deductions and credits can change the result, so a full return projection is better than one rate multiplied by a deduction.

For an economic comparison, include the study and implementation costs, when deductions become usable, reduced depreciation in future years, changing tax rates, an appropriate discount rate and the expected sale. Earlier depreciation reduces adjusted basis; sale allocations and Section 1245 or Section 1250 rules can change the amount and character of taxable gain. Do not assume a sale erases the earlier deduction or that a 1031 exchange automatically defers every reclassified asset. See Publication 544, depreciation recapture.

Can You Use the Deduction?

Depreciation is a deduction, not a dollar-for-dollar tax credit. A qualifying nonpassive loss may offset other income, but material participation does not remove all other limits. Review these issues before treating an estimated deduction as spendable cash:

  • Basis and at risk: Confirm the owner's applicable basis and amount at risk, including financing and entity-level facts.
  • Passive activity: Determine the activity's status, participation and available passive income. An STR exception from the rental-activity definition does not by itself establish a nonpassive loss.
  • Excess business loss: For noncorporate taxpayers, Section 461(l) can limit otherwise allowable business losses after the other loss limits.
  • Personal use: Rental/personal allocation and dwelling-unit limitations can restrict deductions.
  • State treatment: Check each state's conformity, additions and recovery rules separately. A federal bonus deduction does not establish a matching state deduction.

See Publication 925 on passive and at-risk rules and Publication 527 on personal-use rental property. Ask the preparer to show both the current usable amount and any suspended carryforward.

Bonus Depreciation: Check the Original Dates and Elections

Eligible components acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025 generally qualify for 100% federal bonus depreciation. Acquisition rules, including relevant written binding contracts and self-construction rules, used-property requirements, elections and exclusions still matter. Property required to use ADS is generally ineligible for Section 168(k) bonus depreciation.

Older property must use the rules applicable to its original acquisition and placed-in-service dates. Commissioning a study now does not reset those dates or apply today's bonus rate retroactively. The July 4, 2025 enactment date is not the general acquisition and placed-in-service cutoff.

Without bonus depreciation, a $100,000 eligible component is recovered under its applicable MACRS method and convention. The annual deductions are not generally equal. A valid 100% bonus deduction accelerates eligible basis, but the resulting tax benefit still depends on deduction usability.

Sources: Publication 946, chapter 3, 2025 Form 4562 instructions and Notice 2026-11 on bonus depreciation and acquisition rules.

The Form 3115 Lookback Strategy

A prior-year property may qualify for a catch-up depreciation correction. Where a qualifying accounting-method change is available, Form 3115 may produce a negative Section 481(a) adjustment generally taken in the year of change. This is not an automatic benefit for every old property.

Review prior depreciation, original dates, elections, ownership and the applicable method-change eligibility rules. Some errors require an amended return or another correction route. Confirm the current automatic-change procedure, required attachments, filing deadlines and duplicate-copy requirements before filing. A current study does not revive an expired election or establish that a catch-up deduction can offset a high-income year.

Sources: Publication 946, correcting depreciation, Form 3115 instructions, Section 481(a), and Revenue Procedure 2025-23, section 6.01. The preparer should check subsequent modifications and the procedure applicable to the filing year.

The lookback approach is especially valuable for investors who have built portfolios over time and never had cost segregation studies performed on their earlier acquisitions. In our experience, it is not unusual for an investor with three or four properties to discover over $150,000 in previously unclaimed depreciation that can be captured in one year through Form 3115 lookback studies.

When Should You Commission a Study?

  • Before a purchase or return-filing decision: Get a preliminary model and confirm the placed-in-service date. Buying the report earlier does not itself make an asset ready and available for use.
  • After an improvement: Review invoices, the work performed, capitalized basis and the improvement's own placed-in-service date.
  • When reviewing prior returns: Identify the permitted correction route and usable benefit before paying for a lookback study.
  • Before a sale: Compare the timing benefit with remaining depreciation, sale allocations and recapture. A short holding period can change the decision materially.

What Does a Cost Segregation Study Cost?

Study fees typically range from $2,500 to $7,500 for residential and small commercial properties, and $7,500 to $15,000 or more for larger commercial, hospitality, or multi-family properties. At AE Tax Advisors, our studies are priced at approximately $1 per square foot, with the value of a study depending on usable deductions, implementation costs and the expected holding period.

A hypothetical $3,000 study that generates $45,000 in year-one tax savings would have a 15:1 gross tax-effect-to-study-fee ratio if the assumed savings were available. That ratio is not a net or lifetime return. Ask whether Form 3115 work, return implementation, state adjustments, revisions and audit support are included or separately priced.

Documentation and Audit Support

The IRS publishes an Audit Technique Guide for reviewing cost segregation studies. Professional preparation and clear documentation help support the claimed deductions, but the IRS can still examine the return and challenge particular classifications or costs. The guide is examination guidance, not approval of a particular report or a guarantee of an audit outcome.

Ask who prepared the study, how costs were determined, whether allocations reconcile to depreciable basis and how classifications are supported. Get the provider's audit-support scope, exclusions and any additional charges in writing. The IRS guide, chapters 3–5 describes study review, while the IRS audit overview explains return selection and examination.

Bring These Records and Scope Questions

  • Property records: Closing statement, purchase contract, land allocation support, square footage, improvement invoices and original acquisition and placed-in-service dates.
  • Tax history: Depreciation schedules, prior returns, bonus/ADS elections, earlier studies and suspended-loss schedules.
  • Use and owner facts: Rental and personal-use days, participation records, entity ownership, financing, current income and planned sale or exchange timing.
  • Written deliverables: Asset-level classifications, cost support, basis reconciliation and schedules the return preparer can use.
  • Responsibilities: Who prepares any Form 3115, updates depreciation, evaluates federal/state loss limits and files the return; identify separate fees and audit-support terms.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the typical ROI of a cost segregation study?

There is no guaranteed or universal cost-segregation ROI. Compare the usable incremental tax benefit with the study and implementation costs, including the effect of future deductions and an expected sale. A gross tax-effect-to-fee ratio is not a net or lifetime return.

Which property types benefit most from cost segregation?

The benefit depends on eligible depreciable basis, asset mix, deduction usability, tax rates and the expected holding period. Property type alone does not establish the return. Confirm the building recovery period separately from the passive-activity classification.

Can I do a cost segregation study on a property I purchased years ago?

A prior-year property may qualify for a catch-up depreciation correction. Where a qualifying accounting-method change is available, Form 3115 may produce a negative Section 481(a) adjustment generally taken in the year of change. Eligibility, prior elections, filing procedures and loss limits must be checked. Some errors require a different correction route, and a current study does not apply today's bonus rate retroactively.

How does bonus depreciation affect cost segregation ROI?

Eligible components acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025 generally qualify for 100% federal bonus depreciation. Qualification, acquisition rules, elections and exclusions still matter. Older property must use the rules applicable to its original acquisition and placed-in-service dates; commissioning a study now does not reset those dates. The tax benefit also depends on whether the resulting deduction is usable.

Is there a minimum property value for a cost segregation study to make sense?

There is no universal property-value cutoff. A preliminary model should consider depreciable basis, potential eligible components, deduction usability, the study fee, implementation cost and expected holding period.

Does a cost segregation study increase my risk of an IRS audit?

The IRS can examine a cost segregation study and the deductions claimed from it. Its Audit Technique Guide explains how examiners review asset classifications, cost allocations and supporting records; it does not approve a specific study or guarantee an audit outcome. Ask for documented costs, a reconciliation to depreciable basis, support for the asset classifications and a clear description of the provider's audit-support scope.
