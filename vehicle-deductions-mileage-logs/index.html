IRS Mileage Log Requirements: What to Record and Keep

By AE Tax Advisors · Published 2024-01-03

Updated November 8, 2026.

A vehicle deduction needs evidence of business use. Keep a record identifying the date, business destination and purpose, and business miles, together with total annual vehicle use. An app can help collect this information, but automated mileage alone does not establish why a trip was business-related.

Build a usable trip record

RecordWhat it establishes
Date and destinationWhen and where the trip occurred.
Business purposeThe client, meeting, delivery, property task or other specific business reason.
Business mileageThe distance attributable to the qualifying trip.
Total annual useThe denominator needed to distinguish business from personal vehicle use.
Supporting recordsCalendars, invoices, appointment records, receipts and other corroboration.

Record trips at or near the time they occur. IRS Publication 463 discusses adequate records and sufficient corroborating evidence. A blanket estimate such as 80% business use is not a substitute for substantiation.

Separate commuting and personal detours

Ordinary travel between home and a regular work location is generally commuting. A qualifying principal home office or temporary work location can change the analysis, but working at home occasionally does not make every departure a business trip.

Split mixed trips where appropriate. Identify personal segments rather than logging an entire holiday or family errand as business because one stop involved work.

Use the correct 2026 rate for the trip date

For eligible business use under the standard-mileage method, the rate is 72.5 cents per mile from January 1 through June 30, 2026, and 76 cents from July 1 through December 31, 2026. A full-year total cannot simply be multiplied by one of those rates without separating the periods.

For example, 6,000 eligible miles in each half of the year produces $4,350 plus $4,560, or $8,910, before any separately permitted items. This is an illustration of the rate calculation, not proof that a taxpayer qualifies for the method.

Keep records for the method actually used

Standard mileage and actual expenses have different cost calculations, but both need supported business use. Actual expenses also require the relevant fuel, repairs, insurance, registration, depreciation or lease records.

Do not combine standard mileage with a second deduction for the same fuel, depreciation or other costs already included in the rate. Separately eligible parking and tolls require their own business-purpose support.

Repair gaps with evidence, not invented entries

If records are incomplete, preserve calendars, location records, customer invoices and other reliable evidence. Review whether they substantiate the actual use under the rules. Do not fabricate daily entries or assume a reconstructed percentage will be accepted.

For a damaged or lost record, document what happened and the evidence used to reconstruct it. Review reimbursement records as well so the same expense is not deducted twice.

Use the standard-mileage versus actual-expense comparison for the separate method decision. Request a vehicle-expense review with the log, annual mileage, vehicle history and reimbursements.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a mileage app prove business use?

It can record trips, but the business purpose and correct classification still need support.

Can I use one mileage rate for all of 2026?

No. Eligible business miles use 72.5 cents for January–June and 76 cents for July–December.

Can I deduct commuting to my regular office?

Ordinary commuting generally is personal. Home-office and temporary-location exceptions require separate analysis.
