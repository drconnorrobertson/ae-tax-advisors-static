The Business Owners Guide To Section 1031 Like Kind Exchanges And Deferral Strategy

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

IRC Section 1031 can defer gain on a qualifying exchange of investment or business real property. It does not guarantee permanent tax savings or a fresh purchase-price basis. For a hypothetical $2 million realized gain, the current tax deferred depends on gain character, depreciation history, the owner's income, NIIT applicability, and state law; applying a blanket 37% rate is not a sound projection. Compare the cash left for investment after a taxable sale with an exchange, including financing, transaction costs, and future tax on deferred gain.

Like-Kind Requirement and Real Estate Qualification

Current Section 1031 exchanges are limited to qualifying real property held for investment or productive use in a trade or business. Land, rental buildings, commercial property, and qualifying real-property interests can be like-kind even when used differently; a leasehold of 30 years or more can be like-kind to a fee interest. Personal-use homes, dealer inventory, and ordinary partnership interests do not qualify. U.S. real property is not like-kind to foreign real property. Foreign-to-foreign exchanges may qualify if all other conditions are met, so location is not irrelevant.

45-Day Identification Window and 180-Day Close Period

Identify replacement property in signed writing within 45 calendar days after transferring the relinquished property. Deliver an unambiguous identification to the QI or another permitted participant; an unsigned email is not enough. The three-property rule allows up to three properties without a value cap. The 200% rule allows more properties if their aggregate value is at most twice the relinquished property's value. Receive replacement property by the earlier of 180 calendar days or the transfer-year federal return due date, including extensions. For a June 1 transfer, day 45 is July 16 and day 180 is November 28. The return due date may shorten the latter period. Ordinary weekends, holidays, and lender delays do not extend either deadline, but applicable IRS relief can postpone them. Confirm the precise notice and eligibility before relying on relief.

Qualified Intermediary Selection and Fees

For the usual delayed exchange, engage a qualified intermediary before closing and execute an agreement satisfying Treasury Regulation 1.1031(k)-1(g)(4), including restrictions on receiving, borrowing, or pledging exchange funds. The qualified intermediary typically charges $500 to $1,500 to facilitate an exchange. Treat that range as a planning estimate, not a verified quote; ask about transaction complexity, extra properties, wire fees, and state requirements. A QI is a safe harbor, not mandatory for every possible structure such as a direct simultaneous exchange. Disqualified-person rules generally cover recent agents and specified related persons, with exceptions for prior exchange services and routine financial, title-insurance, escrow, or trust services. Do not assume every prior relationship disqualifies a provider or that every QI is federally licensed and bonded. Verify the proposed provider and funds safeguards.

Boot and Gain Recognition

Cash, non-like-kind property, and net debt relief after permitted offsets can produce boot. Recognized gain is generally limited to the lesser of realized gain or boot after eligible exchange expenses; separate recapture rules also need review. Simplified illustration: a $2 million debt-free property exchanged for a $1.8 million replacement plus $200,000 retained cash creates $200,000 cash boot before costs, with recognition capped by realized gain. A $1 million old mortgage replaced by $800,000 debt does not automatically create $200,000 mortgage boot: $200,000 of additional cash paid can offset the debt reduction. Buying a higher-value replacement or borrowing more does not by itself erase cash retained. Ask for a complete sources-and-uses schedule before closing.

Reverse Exchanges and Build-to-Suit Structures

The usual forward exchange transfers the relinquished property before receiving replacement property. Revenue Procedure 2000-37, as modified by Revenue Procedure 2004-51, provides a reverse-exchange safe harbor through an exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT) and a qualified exchange accommodation arrangement (QEAA). The EAT holds qualified indications of ownership and is treated as beneficial owner; it is not simply another name for the QI. Sign the QEAA within 5 business days after transfer to the EAT, identify relinquished property within 45 days when replacement property is parked, and complete the required transfer and combined parking within 180 days, subject to applicable relief. The safe harbor excludes replacement property you owned in the preceding 180 days. Reverse exchanges are more expensive (EAT fees add $2,000 to $4,000 to standard intermediary fees); that existing estimate requires an actual provider quote, and financing, insurance, and entity costs may be additional. Improvement exchanges also require review of what has been completed before the property is transferred to you.

Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) Investments and Passive 1031s

Certain Delaware statutory trusts qualify as replacement real estate under Revenue Ruling 2004-86; qualification depends on the trust's restrictions and federal tax classification, not the DST name alone. The investor may be treated as owning a share of the underlying real estate rather than an ordinary partnership interest. DSTs can provide passive ownership but have limited investor control and liquidity. The existing 1% to 3% annual fees estimate is not a universal or verified sponsor charge: review the actual offering, acquisition and disposition charges, financing, reserves, and ongoing fees before comparing returns. Obtain tax and investment advice on the proposed interest and its risks.

Tax-Deferred Exchanges and Basis Carryover

Deferred gain is preserved in replacement-property basis. Simplified illustration: property originally cost $1 million, has a $400,000 adjusted basis after depreciation, and is exchanged at a $2 million value for a $2 million replacement. With no boot, costs, added investment, or separate recapture, realized and deferred gain are $1.6 million. Replacement basis is $400,000, not $1 million or $2 million. Additional cash invested, debt changes, exchange costs, and recognized gain can change the calculation. Separate the carryover and excess basis for depreciation planning; repeated qualifying exchanges continue deferral rather than resetting the gain to zero.

Case Study: Multi-Property Portfolio 1031 Exchange

A real estate investor owns three properties generating $400,000 combined gain and desires to consolidate portfolio into one larger property. The investor executes a 1031 exchange: sells all three properties (closing dates within 45 days), identifies one replacement property (larger, higher-quality property), and closes on replacement property within 180 days of the first closing. The $400,000 realized gain is deferred. The replacement property has higher depreciation basis (the investor's basis is $2.4 million if property was purchased for higher price, and the $400,000 deferred gain is embedded in basis). The consolidated property generates higher monthly cash flow and lower management burden. The deferred gain remains until the investor later sells this property (without exchange), at which point all accumulated deferred gain is recognized.

Frequently Asked Questions

What property qualifies for a 1031 exchange?

Both properties must be qualifying real property held for investment or business use. A rental house may be like-kind to a warehouse or raw land. Personal-use residences and property held primarily for sale do not qualify. U.S. real property is not like-kind to foreign real property, but foreign-to-foreign exchanges may qualify if the other requirements are met.

What is boot in a 1031 exchange?

Boot includes cash, non-like-kind property, and net debt relief after permitted offsets. Additional cash paid can replace debt, so equal replacement borrowing is not mandatory. A more expensive replacement or extra borrowing does not by itself erase cash retained. Recognized gain is generally limited to the lesser of realized gain or boot after eligible exchange expenses, with recapture rules reviewed separately.

Does a 1031 exchange defer depreciation recapture?

A qualifying real-property exchange can defer gain attributable to depreciation, but full deferral is not automatic. Boot and the separate Section 1245 and Section 1250 recapture rules can require current recognition. Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain for individuals has a maximum 25% federal rate; it is not a flat 25% ordinary-income tax on all depreciation. Track deferred gain and carryover basis for a later taxable disposition.

Can I 1031 exchange a short-term rental?

Yes, provided it is genuinely held for investment or business use rather than personal enjoyment. Significant personal use undermines the position, and the exchange must be structured before closing on the sale, not afterward.
