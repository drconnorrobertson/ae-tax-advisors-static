Cost Segregation Complete Guide 2026

By AE Tax Advisors Team • October 3, 2026

What Is Cost Segregation?

Cost segregation is an engineering-based tax strategy that identifies and reclassifies personal property assets and land improvements from the structural components of a building. Rather than depreciating the entire cost of a building over 27.5 years (residential) or 39 years (commercial), a cost segregation study isolates components that qualify for accelerated depreciation over 5, 7, or 15 years under the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS).

The concept is rooted in the landmark 1997 Hospital Corporation of America v. Commissioner decision, in which the Tax Court ruled that taxpayers could use engineering studies to separate building components into shorter-lived asset classes. Following that case, the IRS published its Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide (ATG), which remains the authoritative framework for how studies should be conducted and documented.

In practical terms, a cost segregation study reclassifies between 20% and 40% of a building's depreciable basis into shorter recovery periods. For a $1 million commercial property, that could mean $200,000 to $400,000 in components moved from 39-year straight-line depreciation into 5-year, 7-year, or 15-year categories. With 100% bonus depreciation now restored under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), those reclassified components can be fully deducted in the year the property is placed in service.

Understanding MACRS Asset Classes

The IRS assigns every depreciable asset a recovery period under MACRS. Cost segregation works by moving components out of the long-life structural category and into the following shorter classes:

MACRS ClassRecovery PeriodExamples of Reclassified Components
5-Year Property5 yearsAppliances, carpet, window treatments, certain electrical circuits dedicated to equipment, decorative lighting, accent walls
7-Year Property7 yearsOffice furniture, fixtures, certain millwork, specialty equipment, security systems, telephone wiring
15-Year Property15 yearsLand improvements: parking lots, sidewalks, landscaping, fencing, retaining walls, exterior lighting, signage, drainage systems
27.5-Year Property27.5 yearsResidential rental building structure (walls, roof, foundation, standard HVAC, standard plumbing, standard electrical)
39-Year Property39 yearsCommercial building structure (same structural components as above, applied to nonresidential property)

The key distinction is between components that serve the building itself (structural, long-life) and components that serve the activity conducted within the building (personal property, short-life). A dedicated electrical circuit powering restaurant kitchen equipment, for example, serves the business activity and qualifies as 5-year property, while the main electrical panel serving the building's general lighting remains 39-year structural property.

Bonus Depreciation in 2026: The OBBBA Restoration

Bonus depreciation is the multiplier that transforms cost segregation from a good strategy into a powerful one. Under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017, bonus depreciation was set at 100% for property placed in service between September 27, 2017, and December 31, 2022, with a scheduled phase-down of 20% per year beginning in 2023. By 2025, the rate had fallen to 40%, and full expiration was expected by 2027.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law in 2025, changed the landscape entirely. OBBBA restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying property placed in service after January 19, 2025, with no scheduled phase-down. This means every dollar of 5-year, 7-year, and 15-year property identified in a cost segregation study can be deducted in full during the year the property enters service.

For property owners, the math is straightforward. A $750,000 commercial building with $225,000 in reclassifiable components generates a first-year depreciation deduction of $225,000 (instead of approximately $5,769 per year under 39-year straight-line). At a combined federal and state marginal tax rate of 37%, that translates to approximately $83,250 in first-year tax savings.

Which Properties Qualify for Cost Segregation?

Cost segregation applies to virtually any building or improvement that is depreciable under MACRS. Qualifying property types include:

  • Residential rental property (27.5-year): Single-family rentals, duplexes, apartment buildings, condominiums held for rent, and manufactured housing used as rental property.
  • Commercial property (39-year): Office buildings, retail centers, restaurants, medical facilities, warehouses, self-storage facilities, hotels, and mixed-use buildings.
  • Short-term rental property: Airbnb, VRBO, and other properties with average rental periods of 7 days or fewer. These are classified as 39-year nonresidential property under IRC Section 168(e)(2)(B) because they provide transient lodging.
  • Tenant improvements: Leasehold improvements made by tenants or landlords, including build-outs, renovations, and interior modifications.
  • New construction: Properties built to specification, where detailed construction cost data is available.
  • Acquired property: Buildings purchased on the secondary market, where the study allocates the purchase price among components.
  • Renovations and improvements: Capital expenditures for remodeling, additions, or upgrades to existing buildings.

The IRS does not impose a minimum property value for cost segregation eligibility. However, as a practical matter, the study should generate tax savings that substantially exceed its cost. At AE Tax Advisors, we generally recommend a cost segregation study for properties with a depreciable basis of $250,000 or more. Below that threshold, the reclassifiable components may not justify the expense of the study.

Components Commonly Reclassified

A thorough cost segregation study examines every building system and component. The following categories represent the most common reclassifications:

Electrical Systems (5-Year and 7-Year)

Dedicated circuits serving specific equipment (kitchen appliances, IT server rooms, medical equipment), decorative lighting fixtures, accent lighting, outlet circuits for movable equipment, and low-voltage wiring for data, telephone, and security systems. The general building electrical distribution (panels, main feeders, general lighting circuits) remains structural.

Plumbing (5-Year and 7-Year)

Dedicated plumbing serving specific equipment such as commercial kitchen sinks, ice makers, coffee stations, and laboratory fixtures. Water heaters serving specific activities, gas piping to appliances, and specialty drainage systems. Standard building plumbing (main water supply, waste lines, general restroom plumbing) remains 27.5-year or 39-year property.

HVAC Components (5-Year and 7-Year)

Supplemental HVAC units serving specific rooms or equipment (server room cooling, kitchen exhaust hoods, bathroom exhaust fans), ductless mini-splits serving individual zones, and thermostats. The central HVAC system (furnace, main ductwork, condensing units serving the building as a whole) remains structural.

Interior Finishes (5-Year)

Carpet and carpet padding, vinyl plank flooring, decorative tile, specialty flooring, wallcoverings, accent walls, crown molding above standard grade, wainscoting, and decorative ceiling treatments.

Cabinetry and Fixtures (5-Year and 7-Year)

Kitchen cabinets, bathroom vanities, countertops, built-in shelving, closet organization systems, appliances (ranges, refrigerators, dishwashers, washers, dryers), and mirrors.

Land Improvements (15-Year)

Parking lots and driveways, sidewalks and walkways, landscaping (trees, shrubs, sod, irrigation systems), retaining walls, fencing, exterior lighting (parking lot lights, pathway lights, decorative exterior fixtures), signage, swimming pools, patios, decks, and site drainage systems.

The Cost Segregation Process: Step by Step

A properly conducted cost segregation study follows a structured methodology consistent with the IRS ATG. Here is how the process works at AE Tax Advisors:

Step 1: Initial Assessment and Data Collection

We review the property details, purchase price or construction costs, closing statements (HUD-1 or ALTA), appraisals, and prior tax returns. For new construction, we collect the general contractor's AIA payment schedule and detailed invoices. For acquisitions, we use the purchase price and allocate costs using the residual method.

Step 2: Property Inspection and Documentation

Our engineering team conducts a physical inspection of the property (or reviews detailed photographs and floor plans for remote studies). Every component is identified, measured, and classified according to its function, attachment method, and relationship to the building structure.

Step 3: Cost Estimation and Allocation

Using construction cost databases (RSMeans, Marshall & Swift), actual invoices, and engineering judgment, we assign a cost to each identified component. The total allocated costs must reconcile to the building's depreciable basis.

Step 4: Asset Classification

Each component is classified into the appropriate MACRS recovery period based on IRC Section 168, Treasury Regulation Section 1.48-1(e), and relevant case law. We apply the six-factor test from Whiteco Industries (1975) to determine whether each component is Section 1245 personal property or Section 1250 real property.

Step 5: Report Preparation

We prepare a comprehensive report that includes a property description, methodology statement, detailed asset listing with costs and classifications, supporting calculations, photographs, and a depreciation schedule. The report is designed to satisfy IRS audit standards.

Step 6: Tax Return Integration

We provide the depreciation schedules to your tax preparer for integration into Form 4562 (Depreciation and Amortization). For lookback studies, we prepare the Form 3115 and calculate the Section 481(a) adjustment.

Types of Cost Segregation Studies

The IRS recognizes multiple methodologies for conducting cost segregation studies. The appropriate approach depends on the property type, available documentation, and cost basis.

Detailed Engineering Approach

This is the gold standard. Engineers physically inspect the property, review construction documents, and individually cost every component using actual invoices or construction cost databases. The IRS ATG considers this the most reliable methodology. AE Tax Advisors uses this approach for all studies, as it provides the strongest audit defense.

Residual Estimation Approach

This method estimates the cost of building shell components (structural elements) and assigns the residual to shorter-lived property. It is sometimes used when detailed construction records are unavailable, such as for older properties. While accepted by the IRS, it generally produces less precise results than the detailed engineering approach.

Sampling or Modeling Approach

For large portfolios of similar properties (such as apartment complexes or hotel chains), a detailed study is performed on a representative sample, and the results are applied across the portfolio. The IRS permits this approach when the properties share substantially similar construction, layout, and finish levels.

IRS Audit Technique Guide Requirements

The IRS published its Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide to establish quality standards for studies. A compliant study must include the following elements:

  • A detailed description of the property and its use
  • The qualifications of the study preparer (engineering credentials, experience with cost segregation)
  • A clear statement of the methodology used
  • An explanation of how costs were determined and allocated
  • A complete listing of all assets with their MACRS classifications
  • Proper legal citations (IRC sections, Treasury Regulations, case law) supporting each classification
  • Reconciliation of total allocated costs to the building's depreciable basis
  • Documentation of the property inspection, including photographs

Studies that fail to meet these standards are vulnerable to IRS challenge. The most common deficiency is inadequate documentation of the methodology and legal basis for asset classifications. At AE Tax Advisors, every study we produce is ATG-compliant and includes full legal citations for every reclassification decision.

Lookback Studies and the Form 3115 Catch-Up

One of the most powerful aspects of cost segregation is that it is not limited to newly acquired properties. If you own a property that was placed in service in any prior year and you have been depreciating it on a straight-line basis over 27.5 or 39 years, you can perform a lookback cost segregation study and claim the cumulative missed depreciation in a single tax year.

The mechanism is IRS Form 3115, Application for Change in Accounting Method. By filing Form 3115 with your current-year tax return, you report a Section 481(a) adjustment that captures all of the depreciation you would have claimed in prior years had the cost segregation study been in place from the beginning. There is no need to amend prior-year returns, and there is no statute of limitations concern because the adjustment flows through the current year.

Consider a property purchased in 2020 for $500,000 with $175,000 in reclassifiable 5-year and 15-year components. Without cost segregation, you claimed approximately $12,820 per year in depreciation (straight-line over 39 years). With cost segregation, the accelerated depreciation on the reclassified components would have totaled approximately $175,000 in year one (under 100% bonus depreciation). A lookback study performed in 2026 captures the difference as a single Section 481(a) deduction, producing a substantial tax benefit without touching any prior returns.

Passive Activity Rules and Cost Segregation

The accelerated depreciation generated by cost segregation is subject to the passive activity loss (PAL) rules under IRC Section 469. For most taxpayers, rental real estate is a passive activity, and passive losses can only offset passive income. This means the large first-year depreciation deduction from cost segregation may be suspended if the taxpayer does not have sufficient passive income to absorb it.

However, there are three important exceptions that allow taxpayers to use cost segregation losses against active income:

1. Real Estate Professional Status (REPS)

Under IRC Section 469(c)(7), a taxpayer who qualifies as a real estate professional can treat rental activities as nonpassive. To qualify, the taxpayer must spend more than 750 hours per year in real property trades or businesses and more than half of their total personal services in those activities. Additionally, the taxpayer must materially participate in each rental activity (or elect to group all rental activities under Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-9(g)). When REPS is established, cost segregation losses can offset W-2 income, business income, and all other active income.

2. The Short-Term Rental Loophole

Under IRC Section 469(j)(10) and Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii), a rental activity with an average rental period of 7 days or fewer is not treated as a rental activity for passive loss purposes. This means short-term rental properties (Airbnb, VRBO, and similar platforms) are classified as active business activities when the owner materially participates. Material participation requires meeting one of the seven tests under Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-5T, the most common being 500 hours of participation per year or 100 hours if no other individual participates more.

This is one of the most powerful combinations in the tax code. A W-2 earner who owns a short-term rental and materially participates can use cost segregation losses to offset their salary, potentially generating six-figure tax savings in the first year.

3. The $25,000 Active Participation Allowance

Under IRC Section 469(i), taxpayers who actively participate in rental real estate (a lower standard than material participation) can deduct up to $25,000 in rental losses against active income, subject to a phase-out beginning at $100,000 of modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) and fully phasing out at $150,000 MAGI. For high-income taxpayers, this exception is generally unavailable.

Cost Segregation for Renovations and Partial Asset Dispositions

Cost segregation is not limited to the initial purchase of a property. When you renovate or improve a building, the capitalized costs of the renovation are independently eligible for cost segregation. A $200,000 kitchen renovation in a commercial restaurant, for example, may include $80,000 to $120,000 in 5-year and 7-year property (cabinets, countertops, specialty plumbing, dedicated electrical, flooring, and appliances).

Equally important is the concept of partial asset disposition under Treasury Regulation Section 1.168(i)-8. When you replace a building component (such as a roof, HVAC system, or flooring), you can elect to dispose of the old component and deduct its remaining undepreciated basis as a loss in the year of replacement. Without this election, the old component continues to depreciate alongside the new one, resulting in duplicate basis that overstates the building's value on your books.

Cost segregation studies support partial asset dispositions by establishing the original cost basis of the replaced component. If a cost segregation study identifies $15,000 in flooring that was originally classified as 5-year property and the flooring is replaced after 3 years, the remaining basis of the old flooring can be written off as a disposition loss.

Section 179 Compared to Cost Segregation

Section 179 of the IRC allows taxpayers to immediately expense the cost of qualifying property, similar to bonus depreciation. For 2026, the Section 179 deduction limit is approximately $1,250,000 (adjusted annually for inflation). While Section 179 and cost segregation can work together, there are important differences:

  • Applicability to real property: Section 179 applies to qualified improvement property, roofs, HVAC, fire protection, alarm systems, and security systems placed in service after the building was originally placed in service. It does not apply broadly to all building components identified in a cost segregation study.
  • Business income limitation: Section 179 deductions cannot exceed taxable business income. Bonus depreciation through cost segregation has no such limitation and can create or increase a net operating loss (NOL).
  • Rental property restrictions: Section 179 cannot be used for property held for the production of rental income (with limited exceptions for certain real property). Cost segregation with bonus depreciation applies fully to rental properties.

For most real estate investors, cost segregation with bonus depreciation is the superior approach. Section 179 is best utilized for equipment and business personal property within the annual deduction limits.

State Conformity Considerations

While cost segregation is a federal tax strategy, state income tax treatment varies. Most states conform to federal MACRS depreciation schedules, but several notable exceptions exist:

  • California: California does not conform to federal bonus depreciation. Taxpayers must use the standard MACRS depreciation schedules (without bonus) for California income tax purposes. Cost segregation still provides a benefit by moving components into shorter recovery periods, but the full first-year expensing is unavailable at the state level. An addition adjustment is required on the California return.
  • New York: New York partially conforms to bonus depreciation but requires modifications for certain property types.
  • Pennsylvania: Pennsylvania does not allow bonus depreciation for personal income tax purposes.
  • Other states: Several other states (including New Jersey, Minnesota, and Hawaii) have varying degrees of nonconformity with federal bonus depreciation rules.

At AE Tax Advisors, we analyze state conformity as part of every cost segregation engagement. If your property is located in a nonconforming state, we calculate both the federal and state depreciation schedules to ensure accurate reporting and maximum combined tax savings.

Who Should Get a Cost Segregation Study?

Cost segregation is most valuable for taxpayers who meet one or more of the following criteria:

  • You own depreciable real property with a cost basis of $250,000 or more.
  • You have recently acquired, constructed, or renovated a building.
  • You own property that was placed in service in prior years and have never performed a cost segregation study (lookback opportunity).
  • You qualify as a real estate professional under IRC Section 469(c)(7).
  • You own a short-term rental property and materially participate in its operation.
  • You have significant active or passive income that could be offset by accelerated depreciation.
  • You are planning to hold the property for at least several years (to avoid depreciation recapture in the near term).

Who Should NOT Get a Study?

Cost segregation may not be appropriate in the following situations:

  • The property's depreciable basis is below $250,000 and the potential reclassification does not justify the study cost.
  • You are planning to sell the property within one to two years. Accelerated depreciation increases the depreciation recapture (taxed at 25% under IRC Section 1250) upon sale, which can eliminate the tax benefit.
  • You are a passive investor with no passive income and no path to real estate professional status or the short-term rental exception. The accelerated losses would be suspended indefinitely under IRC Section 469.
  • The property is tax-exempt or owned by a tax-exempt entity (no depreciation deductions are available).
  • You are in a very low marginal tax bracket and the present value of accelerated deductions does not produce meaningful savings.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is cost segregation and how does it reduce taxes?

Cost segregation is an engineering-based tax strategy that reclassifies building components from 27.5-year or 39-year property into shorter recovery periods (5, 7, or 15 years). By accelerating depreciation deductions into earlier years, property owners reduce taxable income and improve cash flow. Under current 100% bonus depreciation rules, qualifying components can be fully deducted in the first year the property is placed in service.

Who qualifies for a cost segregation study?

Any taxpayer who owns, constructs, or renovates residential rental property, commercial property, or improvements to leased property may qualify. The property must have a depreciable cost basis of at least $250,000 to justify the cost of the study from a return-on-investment perspective. Properties placed in service at any time remain eligible through a lookback study using IRS Form 3115.

How much does a cost segregation study cost at AE Tax Advisors?

AE Tax Advisors charges $1 per square foot for cost segregation studies. For a 2,000-square-foot property, the fee would be $2,000. There is no minimum engagement fee, and the study typically generates tax savings of 10 to 30 times its cost.

What is the current bonus depreciation rate for 2026?

Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed in 2025, bonus depreciation has been restored to 100% for qualifying property placed in service after January 19, 2025. This means all 5-year, 7-year, and 15-year MACRS property identified in a cost segregation study can be fully expensed in year one. There is no scheduled phase-down under the current law.

Can I do a cost segregation study on a property I have owned for several years?

Yes. A lookback cost segregation study allows you to reclassify components on properties placed in service in any prior year. You file IRS Form 3115 (Application for Change in Accounting Method) to claim the cumulative catch-up depreciation as a Section 481(a) adjustment in the current tax year. There is no need to amend prior returns, and the full catch-up deduction is taken in a single year.

Does cost segregation work for short-term rental properties?

Yes, and short-term rentals can be especially advantageous. If the average rental period is 7 days or fewer, the property is not treated as a rental activity under IRC Section 469. This means owners who materially participate can use the accelerated depreciation to offset W-2, business, and other active income without needing real estate professional status. This combination of cost segregation and the short-term rental exception is one of the most powerful tax strategies available to individual investors.
