Will contributing an appreciated, mortgaged building to a partnership trigger tax?

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

Short answer: Often not on the property contribution itself: Section 721 generally defers gain when an owner transfers real estate to a partnership for a partnership interest. But a mortgage can shift among owners under Section 752. The contributor must compare the net deemed debt relief with adjusted outside basis; a net deemed cash distribution exceeding basis can create current gain. Cash paid back to the contributor, a linked refinance, or other sale-like facts can produce a different result under the disguised-sale rules. Do not deed a valuable rental or commercial building to a multi-member LLC on the assumption that “Section 721 means zero tax.” See IRS Publication 541 and the Form 1065 instructions.

The buyer of this advice is an owner admitting investors or combining property portfolios, not someone seeking a definition of Section 721. Before title and financing move, AE can review the deed, loan, tax basis, partner debt shares, cash flows, and draft Form 1065 treatment together.

Decide which transaction actually occurs

A contribution in exchange for a partnership interest is different from a sale of the building, an owner selling a partnership interest, or a transfer to a corporation. Confirm the receiving LLC is taxed as a partnership and identify any cash, note, or other consideration the owner receives. Under Section 721, the contributing owner's initial outside basis generally starts with adjusted tax basis of contributed property, not its appraisal. The partnership ordinarily takes carryover inside basis under Section 723. A $5 million appraised building with $2 million adjusted tax basis therefore does not become a $5 million depreciable asset just because a new partner buys in. Liabilities, other property contributed, income, distributions, and special rules change the final schedules. Publication 541 explains both basis layers.

Run a net-debt worksheet before signing the deed

  1. Reconcile the building's adjusted tax basis: land and depreciable components, improvements, dispositions, and depreciation allowed or allowable.
  2. Identify every debt on or connected to the property, whether the partnership assumes it or takes the building subject to it, and the debt balance at transfer.
  3. Compute the contributor's post-transfer share of each partnership liability under the recourse, nonrecourse, and qualified-nonrecourse rules. Ownership percentage alone does not determine recourse liability share; guarantees and economic risk of loss matter. The IRS partnership guide describes those tests.
  4. Compare the decrease in the contributor's liability share with any increase in their share of partnership liabilities, then carry the net Section 752 amount through outside basis and Section 731's cash-over-basis test. Separately test cash distributions and disguised-sale facts.

Neither “the bank released me” nor “the LLC assumed the whole mortgage” alone calculates the taxable amount. A contributor may retain a share of partnership debt for tax purposes; another partner's personal guarantee may change the recourse allocation. The legal loan documents and the tax allocation workpaper must agree on the facts, although their labels need not produce the same tax conclusion.

Worked example: appreciation is not the same as debt-triggered gain

Assume an owner contributes a building worth $3 million with $700,000 adjusted tax basis and a $1 million mortgage to a new two-owner partnership. Assume, solely for this illustration, that the debt is allocated $400,000 back to the contributor under the applicable Section 752 rules, there is no cash paid, no other basis item, and no disguised sale. The contributor's net liability decrease is $600,000 ($1 million less the retained $400,000 share). Initial outside basis of $700,000 falls to $100,000. On those assumptions, the contribution produces no immediate gain merely because value exceeds basis or because the mortgage exceeds the property's basis. If the contributor's retained share were instead $200,000, the illustrative net decrease would be $800,000 and could create $100,000 of current gain under the deemed-distribution and basis rules. A different debt allocation, another asset or cash contribution, or another existing partnership-basis item changes that answer. The partnership's building basis is a separate inside-basis calculation; none of these numbers is a guaranteed tax saving.

Screen the exceptions and future allocations

Disguised sale: A planned cash payment, debt-funded distribution, or other transfer from the partnership to the contributor may be treated as sale consideration under Section 707 and its regulations. The two-year presumption is a fact-sensitive starting point, not an automatic taxable rule or a safe harbor after two years. IRS guidance on disguised sales and debt-financed distributions explains why the source of the distribution and the partner's allocable debt share matter. Do not pre-label a refinance payout “tax-free return of capital.”

Built-in gain: Section 704(c) generally preserves the pre-contribution book-tax difference for allocation when the partnership later has tax items from that property. The traditional, curative, or remedial method is not simply a preference for the easiest spreadsheet; depreciation ceilings, later sales, and the partners' economics matter. See AE's Section 704(c) allocation guide and the Form 1065 instructions.

Investment-company and multi-step transactions: Section 721 has an investment-company exception; it does not mean that every entity holding investments fails. A prearranged distribution of contributed property to another partner, a contribution followed by cash, or a transfer to a corporate-taxed entity requires its own analysis. Later distributions also implicate Section 731, Section 704(c)(1)(B), and Section 737. For distributions rather than contributions, use AE's property-distribution guide.

Documents to gather and errors to catch

Bring the purchase closing statement, fixed-asset and depreciation schedules, current appraisal, title and proposed deed, mortgage and guarantee agreements, lender consent, payoff or refinance term sheet, formation and operating agreements, proposed capital accounts and Section 704(c) method, partner ownership and profit shares, cash-transfer schedule, prior returns, and an outside-basis rollforward. Ask the preparer to reconcile the property basis and loan balances to the opening Form 1065 balance sheet, Schedule K-1 liability reporting, each partner's outside-basis schedule, and any state transfer-tax or property-tax reassessment consequences.

  • Counting the entire mortgage as taxable boot: Section 752 debt relief must be netted with the contributor's properly computed retained liability share.
  • Assuming every one-third owner gets one-third of a recourse loan: Actual economic risk of loss controls recourse allocations; nonrecourse rules are different.
  • Using appraisal as new depreciable basis: A qualifying Section 721 transfer generally carries tax basis into the partnership.
  • Ignoring a linked cash transfer: Disguised-sale rules can apply even when the deed and distribution have different dates.
  • Calling deferred gain eliminated: Section 704(c), debt changes, later dispositions, and partner-level basis must remain tracked after closing.

This is a federal decision framework, not a conclusion for any particular deed or liability arrangement. Before transferring title or filing the first partnership return, reconcile the legal transfer and tax workpapers with the lender, closing counsel, and return preparer. For a contribution involving a planned payout or a low-basis property, book a review of the exact transaction documents and projected Form 1065/K-1 entries.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Section 721 make a mortgaged building contribution tax-free?

Not automatically. A property-for-partnership-interest contribution generally qualifies for nonrecognition, but a net decrease in the contributor's share of liabilities can be treated as a cash distribution and cause gain if it exceeds outside basis. Cash transfers and disguised-sale facts need separate review.

Does the full mortgage count as taxable consideration?

No. Section 752 requires an analysis of the contributor's debt share before and after the transfer. The post-transfer share depends on recourse, nonrecourse, guarantees, and other facts, not just the mortgage face amount or ownership percentage.

Does a new partner get a fair-market-value depreciation basis?

A qualifying Section 721 contribution generally gives the partnership carryover tax basis in the contributed property. The appraisal and capital-account book value do not themselves create a fair-market-value tax basis for depreciation.

Can a refinance distribution change the contribution's treatment?

Yes. A linked cash or debt-funded transfer to the contributing owner can raise Section 707 disguised-sale issues. Timing, debt tracing, the owner's allocable liability share, and the full transaction documents must be reviewed.
