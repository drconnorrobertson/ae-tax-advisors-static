The Business Owner's Guide to Section 199A Qualified Business Income (QBI) Deduction

AE Tax Advisors · Tax Planning & Strategy

The QBI deduction can reduce eligible business income on an owner's federal return, but the result depends on the tax year, business type, wages, property, losses, and the rest of the return. This guide separates the 2026 rules from hypothetical examples so you can identify what needs review.

What the QBI Deduction Does

Section 199A allows eligible noncorporate taxpayers to deduct qualified business income from sole proprietorships, partnerships, S corporations, and other qualifying pass-through arrangements. The deduction was made permanent by the OBBBA. It is generally calculated at the owner level, is available whether the owner itemizes or takes the standard deduction, and does not reduce self-employment tax.

For a non-SSTB above the phase-in range, the business component generally is the lesser of 20% of QBI or the greater of 50% of properly allocable W-2 wages and 25% of those wages plus 2.5% of UBIA of qualified property. UBIA means unadjusted basis immediately after acquisition. After business loss netting and any qualified REIT/PTP component, the regular deduction is generally capped at 20% of the amount remaining after subtracting net capital gain, including qualified dividends, from taxable income before the QBI deduction. A deduction reduces taxable income; it is not a dollar-for-dollar tax credit.

Beginning in 2026, Section 199A(i) separately provides a $400 minimum deduction for an applicable taxpayer with at least $1,000 of aggregate QBI from active qualified trades or businesses. Active means material participation under Section 469. This limited floor does not make an excluded SSTB a qualified business or remove other eligibility requirements. The examples here address the regular calculation, not that minimum.

Conditional illustration: $1,000,000 of QBI could support a $200,000 regular business component if the applicable wage/property, SSTB, loss, and taxable-income rules permit it. If all $200,000 offsets income otherwise taxed at 37%, the federal income-tax reduction is $74,000. Neither the deduction nor that tax rate follows from revenue alone.

2026 Income Thresholds by Filing Status

Use taxable income before the QBI deduction, not revenue, gross profit, or QBI alone. For tax years beginning in 2026, the threshold and end of the phase-in range are:

  • Married filing jointly: $403,500 to $553,500, a $150,000 range.
  • Married filing separately: $201,775 to $276,775, a $75,000 range.
  • All other returns, including single and head of household: $201,750 to $276,750, a $75,000 range.

At or below the threshold, the W-2 wage/UBIA limit and SSTB restriction do not apply. Within the range, the wage/property limit phases in and only an applicable percentage of SSTB QBI, wages, and UBIA is counted. At the upper endpoint, the non-SSTB wage/property limit is fully effective and the SSTB applicable percentage is zero. The overall taxable-income limitation still matters. These are the 2026 amounts in Revenue Procedure 2025-32, section 4.26.

Build QBI from Net Business Income

Start with qualified domestic business income, deductions, gains, and losses, including applicable carryforwards. Deductible employee and owner-employee wages, employer payroll costs, and business retirement contributions reduce the business's net income. S-corp reasonable compensation and a partner's guaranteed payments for services are not QBI to the recipient. Investment capital gains and losses, most dividends, and interest not properly allocable to a business are excluded; business-related interest may qualify. Farming is not categorically excluded, although agricultural cooperative rules may apply.

Do not use cash distributions as the QBI figure. Reconcile the Section 199A information on each Schedule K-1 with deductible owner-level items, loss limitations, and carryforwards before combining businesses.

W-2 Wages and UBIA: Three Conditional Examples

These examples assume a non-SSTB above the phase-in range, stated QBI after operating deductions, properly allocated wages and UBIA, no loss carryforwards, and a nonbinding overall taxable-income cap:

  • High-wage business: $2,000,000 QBI, $1,200,000 wages, and zero UBIA. The wage limit is $600,000, but 20% of QBI is $400,000. The business component is $400,000, not $600,000. If the business were an SSTB such as a consulting practice at this income level, its SSTB component would instead be zero.
  • Capital-intensive business: $2,000,000 QBI, $400,000 wages, and $8,000,000 UBIA. Compare $200,000 with $100,000 + $200,000 = $300,000. The $300,000 limit is below 20% of QBI ($400,000), so the business component is $300,000.
  • No wages, qualifying property: $400,000 QBI, zero wages, and $2,000,000 UBIA produce a $50,000 property-based limit and a $50,000 business component, rather than zero.

Within the phase-in range, apply the partial limitation rather than these fully phased-in examples. For a non-SSTB with $100,000 QBI, zero wages and zero UBIA, and taxable income halfway through the range, the regular component is $20,000 less a 50% reduction of $20,000, or $10,000, before the overall cap. SSTBs require the additional applicable-percentage calculation.

Which Property Counts and for How Long?

Qualified property is depreciable tangible property held by and available for use in the business at year-end, used at some point during the year to produce QBI, and still within its Section 199A depreciable period. Land does not qualify. That period ends on the later of 10 years after first placed in service or the last day of the last full year of the applicable recovery period, determined without ADS. It is not a test of whether more than one year of useful life remains.

UBIA is generally measured before depreciation, including bonus depreciation and Section 179, subject to the detailed acquisition and allocation rules. Ordinary calendar-year UBIA is not prorated by purchase month: eligible equipment placed in service in January or December and held at year-end can count for that tax year. Buying equipment without placing it in service is not enough, and short-year, transfer, and anti-abuse rules can alter the analysis. See Treasury Regulation 1.199A-2.

SSTB Classification and the Related-Party Rule

SSTBs include defined services in health, law, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, and securities brokerage, plus specified investing, trading, and dealing activities. Engineering and architecture are excluded from the listed service fields. Real estate brokerage and direct real-property management are not automatically SSTBs under the securities-brokerage or investment-management categories.

The separate reputation or skill category is narrow: income from endorsements, licensing an individual's identity, or paid appearances. A business does not become an SSTB merely because its owner is skilled or well known. Apply the actual definitions and any de minimis rules in Treasury Regulation 1.199A-5.

A separate rental or management company is not a workaround for the SSTB limits. Under Treasury Regulation 1.199A-5(c)(2), where there is 50% or more common ownership, including applicable direct, indirect, and related-party attribution, the portion of a business providing property or services to the commonly owned SSTB is treated as a separate SSTB with respect to the related parties. Real contracts, fair-market rent, and genuine services do not switch off this rule.

For example, a commonly owned building company leasing the entire building to the owners' law practice falls under the rule. If part is leased to unrelated tenants, analyze that unrelated portion separately. Rental qualification and the Section 469 self-rental rule are separate questions; neither overrides the Section 199A related-SSTB restriction.

Changing to a C corporation does not restore the QBI deduction: corporate income is outside Section 199A. Corporate and shareholder taxes, compensation, distributions, exit treatment, and the cost of reversing an election require a separate entity analysis.

Aggregation: Eligibility Before Arithmetic

Aggregation under Treasury Regulation 1.199A-4 is optional and requires more than owning several entities. The same person or group must own at least 50% of each business, directly or by the applicable attribution rules, for a majority of the tax year including its last day. Items must be reported for tax years ending on the same date, and none of the aggregated businesses can be an SSTB. At least two of these three operational links must exist:

  • The businesses provide the same or customarily offered-together products or services.
  • They share facilities or significant centralized business elements, such as personnel, accounting, or purchasing.
  • They operate in coordination with, or reliance on, one another.

Document the qualifying facts and annual disclosures, including Schedule B (Form 8995-A) when required. Continue an aggregation consistently in later years while it qualifies. Changes in facts can require a new analysis, and a newly created or acquired qualifying business may be added. It is not freely reversible, but it is not an unconditional forever election requiring IRS consent for every factual change.

Aggregation does not always increase the deduction. Suppose three eligible businesses each have $400,000 QBI, $150,000 wages, and $1,000,000 UBIA. Each business's limit is the greater of $75,000 or $62,500, so the three components total $225,000 without aggregation. Aggregating produces $1,200,000 QBI, $450,000 wages, and $3,000,000 UBIA; the combined limit is also $225,000. There is no deduction increase in this symmetric example.

Cost Segregation Does Not Create Additional UBIA

Cost segregation allocates existing acquisition basis among property components; it does not create a second basis for the same dollars. Assume a building has $2,000,000 of eligible UBIA excluding land, and $800,000 is reclassified into eligible shorter-lived components. Total eligible UBIA remains $2,000,000, not $2,800,000, while all components qualify.

With $200,000 of W-2 wages, the two tests are 50% × $200,000 = $100,000 and 25% × $200,000 + 2.5% × $2,000,000 = $100,000. The limitation remains $100,000 before and after that reclassification. Faster depreciation can reduce current QBI, and different component recovery periods can affect future UBIA eligibility. New qualifying acquisitions or improvements require their own analysis.

Retirement, Rental, and Multi-Year Coordination

Distinguish an S-corp employee's elective salary deferral from an employer contribution. A traditional employee 401(k) deferral generally lowers the employee's federal taxable wages but is part of gross salary already deducted by the S-corp, not a second business deduction. It generally remains within the Section 199A W-2 wage definition under the permitted calculation methods. A Roth elective deferral does not lower current federal taxable income.

An S-corp employer retirement contribution generally reduces pass-through profit and QBI. It is not also deducted a second time by the shareholder on the individual return. Deductible self-employed retirement contributions, health insurance, and the deductible part of self-employment tax attributable to a sole proprietorship or partnership can reduce that owner's QBI. Lower taxable income may improve phase-in treatment even when QBI falls, so compare the complete return, plan limits, staff costs, and cash commitments.

Real estate professional status is not required for rental QBI. A rental can qualify as a Section 162 trade or business, through the Revenue Procedure 2019-38 safe harbor, or under the applicable self-rental rule for property rented to a commonly controlled individual or pass-through business. A rental to a C corporation does not qualify automatically under that self-rental rule. Section 469 passive-loss treatment and Section 199A qualification are separate tests. Real estate professional status alone also does not establish material participation in every rental or make every rental loss deductible.

The Revenue Procedure 2019-38 rental safe harbor requires separate books and records, qualifying rental-service hours, required contemporaneous records, and an attached statement. An enterprise in existence for less than four years needs at least 250 hours in the year; an enterprise in existence for at least four years needs 250 hours in at least three of the five years ending with the current year. Qualifying services may be performed by owners, employees, agents, or contractors. Personal-use residences, triple-net leases, and specified commonly controlled rentals are excluded from this safe harbor. Failure to meet the safe harbor does not by itself rule out qualification under another permitted route.

Income timing must follow the taxpayer's accounting method and constructive-receipt rules. Section 409A governs qualifying nonqualified deferred-compensation arrangements; it is not a general election to defer business receipts or turn wages into QBI. Moving an SSTB owner from $2,000,000 of taxable income to $1,600,000 still leaves that owner above every 2026 SSTB phase-in range. Model both years before treating deferral or an accelerated deduction as helpful.

Prepare for a QBI Review

Bring the prior-year return and Forms 8995 or 8995-A, current income forecast, K-1 statements, payroll reports, ownership chart, related-party agreements, fixed-asset register, and retirement-plan records. Ask which restriction actually limits the deduction and compare after-tax cash rather than the deduction alone. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Section 199A qualified business income deduction?

Eligible noncorporate owners generally deduct up to 20% of QBI, subject to business-level limits, loss netting, and a cap based on taxable income before QBI less net capital gain, including qualified dividends. A separate $400 minimum applies beginning in 2026 to eligible taxpayers with at least $1,000 of aggregate active qualified-business QBI.

What is a specified service trade or business?

SSTBs include defined service fields such as health, law, accounting, and consulting. The reputation or skill category is limited to endorsements, licensing an individual's identity, and paid appearances. SSTB eligibility phases out over the applicable taxable-income range; it does not disappear immediately above the starting threshold.

How do W-2 wages limit the QBI deduction?

For a non-SSTB above the phase-in range, the business component generally cannot exceed the greater of 50% of allocable W-2 wages or 25% of those wages plus 2.5% of qualified-property UBIA, and cannot exceed 20% of QBI. The limitation phases in within the range. Zero wages can still leave capacity through eligible UBIA.

Does rental real estate qualify for the QBI deduction?

It can qualify as a trade or business, through the rental safe harbor, or under applicable commonly controlled self-rental rules. Real estate professional status is not required. The safe harbor includes books, records, a statement, and 250 rental-service hours annually for newer enterprises or in three of five years for enterprises at least four years old. Related-SSTB restrictions remain separate.
