13 Tax Planning Mistakes for Business Owners With Real Estate

By AE Tax Advisors Team · Updated November 6, 2026

For a profitable business owner with real estate, expensive tax problems often come from disconnected records or decisions made without considering the owner-level return. The size of any correction depends on the facts. This guide does not claim that every owner overpays $50,000 or that a standard strategy produces a guaranteed refund.

Use the following issues to prepare a defined second-opinion review, not to conclude that your current CPA made a mistake. A good review may confirm existing treatment.

1. Treating S-corporation savings as profit times 15.3%

The Social Security component has an annual wage base, Medicare has different rules, and an owner's other wages can affect the comparison. Reasonable shareholder compensation, payroll costs, benefits, state treatment and income-tax effects also matter. Multiplying all distributions or a salary reduction by 15.3% is not a reliable savings estimate. See IRS self-employment tax guidance.

2. Selecting owner pay to reach a desired deduction

Document the shareholder's actual services, responsibilities and compensation evidence. There is no universal 50/50 split or salary threshold that protects every owner. A compensation study supports a facts-based position; it does not guarantee immunity from examination.

3. Ignoring employees when choosing retirement plans

An owner-only plan is not automatically available when eligible employees or related employers are involved. For 2026, the regular 401(k) deferral limit is $24,500 and the defined-contribution annual additions limit is $72,000, subject to compensation and plan rules; qualifying catch-ups are separate. A cash balance design requires actuarial and employee-coverage review. Compare funding obligations and fees, not only the largest projected owner contribution. IRS limits and Publication 560 provide the framework.

4. Confusing a property deduction with usable cash savings

A study must support asset classifications and reconcile to basis. It is not an IRS-approved refund product. A property LLC's loss may remain passive even when self-rental income is nonpassive. Review basis, at-risk, passive-loss, excess-business-loss and state limits before multiplying a proposed deduction by a tax rate.

5. Assuming an old study uses today's depreciation rules

The relevant acquisition and placed-in-service dates, historical methods and elections determine the analysis. A newly commissioned report does not reset those dates or guarantee a current-year catch-up. Review method-change preparation before deciding whether to amend or file Form 3115.

6. Applying outdated QBI thresholds or moving income between related entities

For 2026 married filing jointly returns, the Section 199A threshold is $403,500 and the phase-in range ends at $553,500. Other filing statuses differ. Taxable income before the QBI deduction, SSTB status, wages, property and other rules affect the calculation. Separating an SSTB's management or property activity into a related entity does not automatically avoid the restrictions. See the IRS 2026 threshold table.

7. Buying an asset just to obtain a write-off

Tax deductions do not make an unnecessary purchase free. Section 179 and bonus depreciation have separate requirements, limits and elections. Vehicle business use, passenger-auto limits and heavy-SUV restrictions require their own analysis. Assess business need, financing, current deduction use and future disposition consequences using Publication 946.

8. Assuming every prior-year problem is an amendment

The available route may be an amendment, superseding return, accounting method change, partnership administrative adjustment request, prospective action or no change. Refund claims generally use a three-years-from-filing or two-years-from-payment rule, whichever is later, with payment lookback restrictions and exceptions. Some missed elections and funding deadlines cannot simply be repaired by amendment. Form 1040-X instructions are a starting point for individual returns.

9. Using a cash-method threshold without testing eligibility

The small-business gross-receipts test is inflation adjusted and can require aggregation across related entities. Tax-shelter and other exclusions matter. A business below a headline threshold does not automatically qualify for every accounting simplification. Changing an established method can require consent and a Section 481(a) adjustment.

10. Treating estimated payments as the final tax bill

Safe-harbor payments and the ultimate liability are different calculations. Include payroll withholding, business and rental income, gains, states and payment timing in the projection. No universal percentage of business profit is right for every owner's cash reserve.

11. Assuming a state election works the same everywhere

Pass-through entity tax elections, credits, deadlines, resident treatment and federal conformity vary by state. Model the owner and entity together, including other-state credits and state depreciation differences. Do not use an outdated federal SALT cap or assume every owner benefits equally from an election.

12. Calling ordinary product improvement qualified research

The research credit requires satisfaction of specific activity, expense and documentation requirements. Developing a new recipe, process or software feature alone does not establish eligibility. Payroll-tax credit eligibility also has separate conditions. See IRS research-credit resources.

13. Keeping totals without supporting evidence

Maintain records of acquisition cost, business purpose, business use, participation and required substantiation. Mileage rates can change within a year: the current IRS table lists 72.5 cents for business miles January–June 2026 and 76 cents July–December. The correct rate still does not replace a business-mileage record.

What to ask a reviewer to deliver

Request the facts reviewed, positions supported, missing evidence, available procedures, actual deadlines, proposed implementation and total scope. Separate deductions, deferrals, permanent savings and refunds. Published examples or an engagement alone do not establish realized results.

Frequently asked questions

Does every business owner have recoverable overpayments?

No. A review may confirm current treatment. Any correction or savings estimate requires the actual facts, records, eligible procedures and deadlines.

Is a cost segregation study an IRS-approved refund?

No. A study supports asset classifications; the preparer must apply tax rules and evaluate deduction usability. A study is not IRS approval or a guaranteed refund.

How should I compare a proposed tax plan with its fee?

Compare supported incremental outcomes, timing, current deduction use, implementation cost, future consequences and assigned responsibilities. Do not compare a gross deduction with the fee as if it were cash savings.
