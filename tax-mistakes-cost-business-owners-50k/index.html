Tax Mistakes That Cost Business Owners $50K+ Every Year

By AE Tax Advisors Team · October 3, 2026

Most business owners pay more in taxes than they legally owe. Not because they are careless, but because their tax strategy was never built to match the complexity of their business. The average small business owner earning $300,000 or more in annual net income leaves between $50,000 and $150,000 on the table over a three-year period simply by following default tax practices rather than proactive planning.

Below, we break down the 13 most expensive tax mistakes we see across our nationwide client base, along with the specific dollar amounts at stake and the strategies to correct each one. If even two or three of these apply to you, the cumulative impact could easily exceed $50,000 per year.

1. Operating as a Sole Proprietorship Instead of an S-Corp

Cost: $15,000 to $40,000 per year in unnecessary self-employment tax.

When you operate as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity, every dollar of net profit is subject to the 15.3% self-employment tax (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare). For a business generating $250,000 in net income, that translates to roughly $35,000 in SE tax alone.

By electing S-Corp taxation (filing Form 2553 with the IRS), you split your income into two categories: a reasonable W-2 salary and shareholder distributions. Only the salary portion is subject to FICA taxes. If your reasonable salary is $100,000 and you take $150,000 as distributions, you eliminate SE tax on that $150,000, saving approximately $23,000 per year.

The threshold where S-Corp taxation typically makes sense is around $80,000 to $100,000 in consistent net profit, after accounting for the added payroll and compliance costs (generally $2,000 to $5,000 annually for payroll processing, quarterly filings, and a separate business return).

2. Not Having a Retirement Plan Beyond a Basic IRA

Cost: $20,000 to $100,000+ in missed deductions annually.

A traditional IRA allows a maximum contribution of $7,000 per year ($8,000 if you are age 50 or older). For a business owner earning $300,000 or more, that is a rounding error. The real opportunity lies in employer-sponsored plans designed for high-income business owners:

  • SEP-IRA: Up to 25% of compensation, capped at approximately $69,000 for 2026.
  • Solo 401(k): Up to $23,500 in employee deferrals plus 25% employer match, totaling roughly $69,000 (plus a $7,500 catch-up for those 50 and older).
  • Defined Benefit / Cash Balance Plan: Contributions of $100,000 to $350,000+ per year, depending on age and plan design. A 55-year-old business owner with consistent high income can often deduct $250,000 or more annually.

The gap between a $7,000 IRA deduction and a $250,000 cash balance plan deduction is enormous. At a 37% marginal federal rate, that is a difference of roughly $90,000 in annual tax savings. Even moving from a SEP-IRA to a properly layered Solo 401(k) plus cash balance plan can yield $30,000 to $60,000 in additional deductions.

3. Taking Too Little or Too Much S-Corp Salary

Cost: $10,000 to $30,000 in wasted payroll taxes or IRS penalties.

Setting your S-Corp salary is a balancing act. Set it too low, and the IRS can reclassify your distributions as wages, triggering back taxes, interest, and penalties. Set it too high, and you are voluntarily paying FICA taxes on income that could have been distributed tax-free at the payroll level.

A business owner earning $400,000 who pays herself a $200,000 salary instead of a defensible $130,000 salary is overpaying approximately $10,700 in unnecessary FICA taxes each year. Conversely, an owner paying a $40,000 salary on $400,000 of income is practically inviting an audit. The IRS has won multiple cases (such as Watson v. Commissioner) where unreasonably low salaries were reclassified.

The solution is a formal reasonable compensation analysis benchmarked against Bureau of Labor Statistics data, industry surveys, and geographic norms. This documentation costs a few hundred dollars and provides audit protection worth tens of thousands.

4. Missing Cost Segregation on Rental Properties

Cost: $50,000 to $200,000+ in delayed deductions.

Real estate investors who rely on straight-line depreciation (27.5 years for residential, 39 years for commercial) are leaving significant deductions on the table. A cost segregation study, conducted by qualified engineers, reclassifies building components into shorter depreciation categories:

Component CategoryRecovery PeriodExamples
Personal property5 yearsAppliances, carpeting, decorative fixtures
Fixtures and systems7 yearsSpecialty electrical, security systems
Land improvements15 yearsParking lots, landscaping, fencing, sidewalks
Structural components27.5 or 39 yearsFoundation, roof structure, walls

For a $1 million rental property, a cost segregation study typically reclassifies 25% to 40% of the building's cost basis into the shorter categories. Combined with bonus depreciation (currently at 100% under the OBBBA), this can generate $100,000 to $200,000 in first-year deductions rather than spreading them over nearly three decades.

If you purchased rental property in 2023, 2024, 2025, or 2026 and never had a cost segregation study performed, you can still benefit by filing a change in accounting method (Form 3115) to claim the catch-up depreciation without amending prior returns.

5. Not Doing Year-End Tax Planning

Cost: $10,000 to $50,000 in missed opportunities.

Tax planning is not a once-a-year event at filing time. By the time you sit down with your CPA in March or April, the tax year is closed and most planning opportunities have expired. Effective year-end planning (conducted in October through December) includes:

  1. Accelerating deductions into the current year through prepaying expenses, purchasing equipment before December 31, and maximizing retirement plan contributions.
  2. Deferring income when possible by delaying invoicing, adjusting project timelines, or timing asset sales.
  3. Harvesting investment losses to offset capital gains.
  4. Making charitable contributions through donor-advised funds or qualified charitable distributions.
  5. Reviewing entity structure for the upcoming year (S-Corp elections for the new year must be filed by March 15).

A business owner earning $500,000 who accelerates $80,000 in equipment purchases and contributes $100,000 to a cash balance plan before year-end can reduce their taxable income by $180,000, saving approximately $66,000 in combined federal and state taxes.

6. Ignoring Amended Return Opportunities

Cost: $20,000 to $100,000 in recoverable overpayments.

Many business owners assume that once a tax return is filed, it is final. In reality, you have three years from the original filing date (or two years from the date the tax was paid, whichever is later) to file an amended return and claim a refund.

Common items recovered through amended returns include:

  • Missed depreciation deductions on assets placed in service in prior years.
  • Retroactive S-Corp elections (late election relief under Rev. Proc. 2013-30).
  • Unclaimed business deductions such as home office, vehicle, or travel expenses.
  • Incorrect filing status or entity classification.
  • Overlooked credits, including the R&D credit and energy-efficient property credits.

We routinely recover $20,000 to $100,000 across two to three amended years for clients who were previously working with preparers focused solely on compliance rather than optimization.

7. Using the Wrong Accounting Method

Cost: Variable, but timing differences can cost thousands annually.

The choice between cash basis and accrual basis accounting has real tax consequences. Cash basis recognizes income when received and expenses when paid, giving you more control over timing. Accrual basis recognizes income when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of cash flow.

For businesses under the $30 million gross receipts threshold, the cash method is generally preferred because it provides greater flexibility to time income and deductions. However, certain businesses (C-Corporations above the threshold, tax shelters, and some inventory-heavy operations) are required to use accrual.

Switching accounting methods requires IRS approval via Form 3115, and the timing of a method change can itself create a taxable event (a Section 481(a) adjustment). Done correctly, a method change can accelerate deductions. Done poorly, it can trigger an unexpected tax bill.

8. Missing the QBI Deduction or Structuring It Incorrectly

Cost: $10,000 to $30,000 per year.

The Qualified Business Income deduction under IRC Section 199A allows eligible pass-through business owners to deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income. For a business owner with $200,000 in QBI, that is a $40,000 deduction, saving approximately $14,800 at the 37% bracket.

However, the deduction phases out for specified service trades or businesses (SSTBs, which include law, medicine, consulting, accounting, and financial services) at taxable income thresholds of $191,950 for single filers and $383,900 for married filing jointly (2026 figures). Above these thresholds, the deduction can be reduced or eliminated entirely.

Strategic responses include:

  • Splitting SSTB and non-SSTB activities into separate entities when the facts support it.
  • Managing taxable income through retirement plan contributions, charitable giving, and timing strategies to stay below phase-out thresholds.
  • Ensuring W-2 wages and depreciable property basis (the "wage and basis" limitation) are sufficient to support the full deduction at higher income levels.

9. Failing to Maximize Section 179 and Bonus Depreciation on Equipment

Cost: Tens of thousands in deferred deductions.

Section 179 allows businesses to expense the full cost of qualifying equipment and property in the year it is placed in service, up to $1,250,000 for 2026 (the limit is adjusted annually for inflation). Bonus depreciation under the OBBBA currently allows 100% first-year expensing on qualifying assets.

Business owners who miss this opportunity typically do so because they purchase equipment late in the year and their preparer does not properly elect Section 179, or because they are unaware that vehicles over 6,000 pounds GVWR (such as certain SUVs and trucks) qualify for accelerated depreciation. A qualifying heavy vehicle can generate $28,900 or more in first-year deductions under Section 179, with additional depreciation available in subsequent years.

For businesses investing $100,000 to $500,000 in equipment, furniture, or technology each year, the difference between depreciating those assets over five to seven years versus expensing them immediately can represent $20,000 to $80,000 in tax savings in year one.

10. Not Planning for Estimated Taxes

Cost: Penalties plus significant cash flow disruption.

The IRS imposes underpayment penalties when taxpayers fail to pay at least 90% of the current year's tax liability (or 100% of the prior year's liability, 110% for high-income earners) through withholding or estimated payments. The penalty rate fluctuates with federal short-term interest rates and has been running between 7% and 8% annually in recent quarters.

Beyond penalties, poor estimated tax planning creates cash flow crises. A business owner who does not set aside funds quarterly may face a $40,000 to $80,000 tax bill in April with insufficient reserves. This leads to payment plans (which accrue interest), unnecessary borrowing, or worse, tapping retirement accounts and triggering additional taxes and early withdrawal penalties.

The fix is straightforward: quarterly income projections, automated tax set-asides (we recommend a separate savings account holding 30% to 35% of net business income), and timely estimated payments each quarter.

11. Ignoring State Tax Planning and SALT Workarounds

Cost: Varies by state, but often $5,000 to $25,000 or more.

The $10,000 federal cap on state and local tax (SALT) deductions hit business owners in high-tax states particularly hard. However, more than 30 states now offer a pass-through entity tax (PTET) election that effectively bypasses the SALT cap. The entity pays state income tax at the entity level, generating a federal deduction that is not subject to the $10,000 limitation, and the owners receive a credit on their individual state returns.

Business owners who operate in multiple states face additional complexity, including nexus rules, apportionment formulas, and varying state conformity to federal tax law. Failing to evaluate state-level planning can result in double taxation, missed credits, or penalties for non-filing in states where you have economic nexus.

12. Missing the R&D Tax Credit

Cost: $10,000 to $50,000+ per year in unclaimed credits.

The Research and Development (R&D) tax credit under IRC Section 41 is not limited to laboratory research. It applies to any business that develops or improves products, processes, software, formulas, or techniques. This includes manufacturers refining production methods, software companies building new features, construction firms developing proprietary techniques, and even food and beverage companies creating new recipes.

The credit is calculated as a percentage of qualified research expenses (QREs), including wages for employees performing qualified activities, supplies consumed in research, and contract research costs. For small businesses (under $5 million in gross receipts), the R&D credit can even offset payroll taxes, providing value to pre-revenue startups and early-stage companies.

Many business owners miss this credit because their CPA does not ask the right questions about their day-to-day operations, or because they assume "research" means white lab coats and PhDs. In reality, if your business is solving technical challenges through a process of experimentation, you likely qualify.

13. Poor Recordkeeping Leading to Lost Deductions

Cost: $5,000 to $30,000+ in unsubstantiated deductions.

The IRS requires adequate records to substantiate every deduction. Under IRC Section 274, certain expenses (meals, travel, entertainment, vehicle use, and listed property) require contemporaneous written records showing the amount, date, place, business purpose, and business relationship of each expense.

Without proper documentation, legitimate deductions become indefensible during an audit. Common casualties include:

  • Vehicle expenses: Without a mileage log, the IRS can disallow the entire deduction. For a business owner driving 25,000 business miles per year at the 2026 standard rate of $0.70 per mile, that is a $17,500 deduction at risk.
  • Home office deduction: Without records of exclusive and regular business use, a $5,000 to $15,000 annual deduction evaporates.
  • Meals and travel: Receipts without notations of business purpose are routinely disallowed. For owners spending $10,000 to $20,000 annually on business meals, the loss of this deduction (currently 50% deductible) costs $5,000 to $10,000 in writeoffs.

The solution is a cloud-based expense tracking system (such as QuickBooks, Expensify, or Dext) paired with a habit of logging purpose and attendees at the time of each expense. Retroactive reconstruction of records is expensive, unreliable, and often insufficient for audit defense.

The Cumulative Impact

Any one of these mistakes can cost a business owner $10,000 to $50,000 per year. When three or four compound together (which is the norm for owners without proactive advisory), the cumulative overpayment reaches $50,000 to $150,000 or more over a rolling three-year window. That is money that could be reinvested in growth, allocated to retirement, or distributed to your family.

The difference between reactive tax compliance (filing accurate returns after the fact) and proactive tax advisory (planning and restructuring throughout the year) is the difference between paying what the IRS calculates and paying what you legally owe. Those two numbers are rarely the same.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can an S-Corp election save me on self-employment taxes?

Most business owners earning $150,000 or more in net profit save between $15,000 and $40,000 per year in self-employment taxes by electing S-Corp status. The savings come from splitting income between a reasonable W-2 salary (subject to FICA taxes) and distributions (which are not subject to FICA). The exact amount depends on your total net income and what qualifies as a reasonable salary in your industry.

What is cost segregation and how much can it save on rental properties?

Cost segregation is an IRS-approved engineering study that reclassifies components of a building (appliances, flooring, landscaping, electrical, plumbing) from 27.5-year or 39-year depreciation schedules into 5-year, 7-year, or 15-year categories. For a property purchased at $500,000 or more, this typically accelerates $50,000 to $200,000 or more in deductions into the first year of ownership, creating significant tax savings and improved cash flow.

Can I amend prior year tax returns to recover overpayments?

Yes. You can file amended returns (Form 1040-X or 1120-X) for any tax year within three years of the original filing date. Common recoverable items include missed depreciation, unclaimed deductions, incorrect entity classification, and overlooked credits. Our clients typically recover between $20,000 and $100,000 in overpayments across two to three prior years.

What retirement plans offer the largest tax deductions for business owners?

A defined benefit or cash balance plan allows business owners to deduct $100,000 to $350,000 per year, depending on age and plan design. By comparison, a SEP-IRA caps at roughly $69,000 for 2026, and a Solo 401(k) caps at around $69,000 plus a $7,500 catch-up contribution for those age 50 and older. The gap between a basic IRA contribution ($7,000) and a properly structured cash balance plan can represent $20,000 to $100,000 or more in missed deductions annually.

How do I know if my S-Corp salary is set correctly?

The IRS requires S-Corp owners to pay themselves a reasonable salary based on industry norms, geographic location, experience, and time spent in the business. Setting it too low triggers audit risk and potential reclassification of distributions as wages (plus penalties). Setting it too high wastes $10,000 to $30,000 in unnecessary payroll taxes. A compensation study benchmarked against BLS and industry data is the safest way to set the right number.

What is the QBI deduction and why do business owners miss it?

The Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction under IRC Section 199A allows eligible business owners to deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income, potentially saving $10,000 to $30,000 or more per year. Business owners miss it because of income phase-out thresholds, specified service trade or business (SSTB) limitations, and improper entity structuring. With the right planning, many owners who think they are phased out can restructure operations to recapture part or all of the deduction.
