Reasonable Compensation for S-Corp Owners

Officer salary is the most litigated number on an S-Corporation return. Here is how the IRS tests it, which factors carry weight, how a defensible study is built, and what it costs when the figure is wrong in either direction.

Why Reasonable Compensation Draws IRS Attention

The entire tax benefit of an S-Corporation depends on one line: how much of the profit is called wages. Wages carry 15.3% combined FICA up to the Social Security wage base and 2.9% Medicare above it. Distributions carry none. Every dollar moved from the wage column to the distribution column saves employment tax, and the IRS knows exactly where that incentive points.

The legal hook is IRC Section 3121(d)(1), which defines a corporate officer who performs more than minor services as an employee, combined with Revenue Ruling 74-44, which holds that distributions paid to a shareholder-employee in lieu of reasonable compensation are wages. The Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration has repeatedly identified officer compensation as a significant area of underreporting, and examiners have automated tools to flag returns where Form 1120-S reports substantial ordinary business income with little or no officer compensation on line 7.

The pattern that draws scrutiny is simple to describe: a profitable single-shareholder S-Corporation, meaningful distributions, and a salary that is small, round, or zero. In Watson v. United States, an accountant with an ownership interest in a CPA firm paid himself $24,000 while taking roughly $200,000 in distributions. The Eighth Circuit upheld the reclassification of $91,044 per year as wages. In Glass Blocks Unlimited v. Commissioner, an owner who took payments while reporting no salary at all had those payments treated as wages even though the company was operating at a loss. The lesson from both is the same. The absence of a documented, market-based salary is what turns a defensible position into a losing one.

The Factors the IRS and Courts Actually Apply

IRS Fact Sheet 2008-25 lists the factors examiners use, and they track the factors courts have applied for decades. No single factor controls, and the weight given to each shifts with the facts.

Training and experience. A surgeon, an engineer, and a licensed contractor each command a different market wage. Credentials, years in the field, and specialization all raise the reasonable figure.

Duties and responsibilities. What does the owner actually do? An owner who sells, delivers the service, manages staff, and runs finance is performing several roles, and reasonable compensation reflects the blend. An owner who has delegated operations and only sets strategy performs a narrower role at a lower wage.

Time and effort devoted to the business. Hours matter. A part-time owner working 15 hours per week does not require a full-time salary, and documenting reduced hours is one of the more effective ways to support a lower number.

Dividend history. A company that pays large, regular distributions and small wages presents the classic pattern examiners look for.

Payments to non-shareholder employees. If a non-owner employee performing similar work is paid $140,000, an owner performing more work at $60,000 is difficult to defend. Internal comparables are persuasive evidence in both directions.

Timing and manner of paying bonuses. Bonuses paid to owners in proportion to stock ownership, particularly at year end, look like disguised distributions. Bonuses tied to performance metrics look like compensation.

What comparable businesses pay. External market data for the same role, industry, geography, and company size is the backbone of any defensible analysis.

Compensation agreements and formulas. A written employment agreement set in advance, at arm's length terms, is far stronger than a number chosen at tax time.

One further consideration applies to businesses with significant capital: the profit attributable to invested capital, equipment, real property, and the labor of other employees is properly treated as a return on the business rather than compensation for the owner's services. A capital-intensive manufacturer can support a lower officer wage relative to profit than a pure service firm where the owner is the product.

How a Defensible Compensation Study Is Built

We build reasonable compensation figures using RCReports, the analysis platform most commonly cited in this area, supported by Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics data and industry compensation surveys. The output is a written report, not a number in an email, and it is retained with the corporate records.

The analysis begins with a role breakdown. Rather than asking what a business owner earns, which is not an occupation, we decompose what the owner actually does into recognized occupational categories and allocate time across them. A dental practice owner might spend 60% of working hours as a general dentist, 20% as a general and operations manager, 10% in sales and marketing, and 10% in administrative support. Each of those roles has published wage data by percentile and metropolitan area. The weighted blend, adjusted for experience level and hours worked, produces the compensation figure.

Three approaches are used depending on the facts. The cost approach builds the wage from the roles performed, as described above, and fits most owner-operated businesses. The market approach compares the owner's total compensation against comparable positions at similar companies, and works best where clean external comparables exist. The income approach starts from the return the business should generate on its invested capital and intangible assets and treats the residual as compensation, which is most useful for capital-intensive or highly profitable companies where the cost approach would understate the owner's value.

The report documents the inputs, the data sources, the date of the analysis, and the resulting range. That range, not a single point, is what gives the position defensive strength. If an examiner proposes a different number, the conversation starts from a documented methodology instead of from nothing.

What Happens When Compensation Is Too Low

The exposure is not limited to the tax that should have been paid. When the IRS recharacterizes distributions as wages, the assessment stacks.

Employment tax. Both the employer and employee share of Social Security and Medicare on the reclassified amount, which is 15.3% up to the wage base and 2.9% above it, plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax where the thresholds are met. Federal and state unemployment tax may also apply.

Failure to deposit penalty. IRC Section 6656 imposes up to 15% of the underdeposited amount depending on how late the deposit is.

Failure to file penalty. Amended Forms 941 and 940 carry their own penalties under IRC Section 6651.

Accuracy-related penalty. IRC Section 6662 adds 20% of the underpayment where there is a substantial understatement or negligence.

Interest. Running from each original deposit due date, which for a multi-year exam is years of compounding.

Personal liability. The trust fund portion of employment tax can be assessed against responsible persons individually under IRC Section 6672. Corporate form does not shield the owner from that assessment.

A three-year exam that reclassifies $80,000 per year commonly produces a total assessment in the $50,000 to $70,000 range once penalties and interest are added, against total FICA savings that may have been $35,000. There is also a knock-on effect most owners do not consider: understated wages reduce the owner's Social Security earnings record, lowering eventual retirement benefits, and they reduce the wage base used in the Section 199A qualified business income limitation, which can cost part of the QBI deduction for higher-income owners.

What Happens When Compensation Is Too High

Overpaying is less dangerous but it is not free, and it is more common than most owners expect, usually because a payroll figure was set years ago and never revisited.

The direct cost is unnecessary FICA. Every dollar of wages above the defensible figure and below the Social Security wage base carries 12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare that a distribution would have avoided. On $50,000 of excess salary, that is roughly $7,650 per year of tax paid for no reason.

There are secondary effects. Excessive compensation reduces pass-through income, which reduces the base for the Section 199A deduction. It inflates the wage base used for workers compensation premiums and state unemployment tax, both of which are experience-rated. And in the rare case where a related C-Corporation is involved, unreasonably high compensation can be recharacterized as a disguised dividend, producing the reverse of the usual problem.

There is one situation where a higher salary is deliberately correct. Retirement plan contribution limits are driven by W-2 compensation. A solo 401(k) employer contribution is capped at 25% of wages, and a defined benefit or cash balance plan contribution scales with compensation history. When the owner is funding a cash balance plan, the income tax deduction generated by a larger contribution can exceed the additional payroll tax, particularly for wages above the Social Security wage base where only the 2.9% Medicare rate applies. That tradeoff has to be modeled, not assumed.

Documentation, Timing, and Annual Review

Reasonable compensation is not a one-time decision. It changes as the business grows, as the owner's role shifts, and as market wages move. We review the figure annually as part of tax planning, before the year closes rather than after.

The file should contain four things: the written compensation analysis with its data sources and date, a board resolution or written employment agreement setting the salary in advance of the year it applies to, payroll records showing the salary was actually paid on a regular schedule through a payroll system with proper deposits, and contemporaneous notes on the owner's role and hours, particularly where hours are part-time or the role has changed.

Timing matters more than owners expect. A salary set in December to true up a year that has already happened is weaker evidence than a salary set the prior December and paid across twelve periods. Regular payroll also avoids the failure to deposit penalties that catch owners who run a single large year-end payroll and miss the deposit schedule that amount triggers.

One correction we make often: owner health insurance premiums paid by the S-Corporation for a more-than-2% shareholder must be included in Box 1 wages under IRC Section 1372 and Notice 2008-1, though they are exempt from FICA. Those premiums count toward the reasonable compensation figure and are deducted by the shareholder above the line. Businesses that leave this off the W-2 lose the self-employed health insurance deduction entirely.

We prepare a documented compensation analysis, set the figure in writing before the year begins, and review it annually as your role and the market change. If you are already under examination, we handle the response.
