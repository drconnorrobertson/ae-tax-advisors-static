Cost Segregation Study in Texas

Texas has no personal income tax, which changes what a cost segregation study is for. In an income tax state the study produces both a federal and a state deduction. In Texas it produces a federal deduction only, and the entire planning conversation shifts to how that federal deduction interacts with the rest of your return.

That makes Texas one of the cleanest states in the country to plan in. There is no addback schedule, no conformity date to track, no second depreciation schedule to carry for thirty years. There is also no state-level offset available, so the federal analysis has to carry the whole decision.

How Texas Taxation Interacts With Federal Strategy

Texas imposes no individual income tax. The Texas Constitution requires a statewide vote to enact one, and a 2019 amendment raised that barrier further. For an investor or business owner, the top marginal rate on ordinary income is the federal 37% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax where it applies, with nothing on top.

What Texas does impose is the franchise tax, a gross receipts style margin tax. The rate is 0.75% of taxable margin for most entities and 0.375% for retailers and wholesalers. Entities with annualized total revenue at or below the no tax due threshold, currently $2.47 million, owe nothing, and since 2024 those entities no longer file a No Tax Due report at all.

Texas also has among the highest property tax rates in the country, with effective rates commonly between 1.5% and 2.2% depending on the county and school district. For most Texas real estate investors, property tax is the largest recurring state and local cost by a wide margin, and property tax protest and valuation strategy delivers more annual savings than anything on the income side.

Texas Pass-Through Entity Tax

Texas has no pass-through entity tax and does not need one. The PTET workaround exists to convert individual state income tax, which is subject to the federal SALT cap, into an entity-level deduction. Texas owners have no state income tax to convert.

That is a genuine structural advantage. A Texas business owner with $1 million of pass-through income keeps the full federal deduction stack with no state layer at all, where a New Jersey owner in the same position is managing more than $100,000 of state tax and a PTET election to make it deductible.

The franchise tax is not an income tax and is not creditable or deductible in the same way. It is an ordinary and necessary business expense deductible on the federal return, which is the correct treatment, and it is computed on margin rather than net income so depreciation generally does not reduce it.

That last point is the one Texas investors most often get wrong. Taxable margin is total revenue less the greater of cost of goods sold, compensation, 30% of revenue, or a $1 million fixed deduction. Depreciation flows into the calculation only through cost of goods sold for entities that qualify to use it. A cost segregation study on a Texas rental property does not reduce the franchise tax.

Texas Depreciation Conformity

Because Texas does not tax personal income, there is no state depreciation schedule for individuals, partnerships, or S corporations passing income through to Texas residents. The federal schedule is the only schedule. Bonus depreciation under IRC Sec. 168(k) and Section 179 expensing apply in full with no addback and no recomputation.

For franchise tax purposes, entities using the cost of goods sold deduction generally follow federal depreciation for goods produced or acquired for sale. Real estate rental activity does not typically qualify for the cost of goods sold subtraction, so the question rarely arises for an investor.

The practical result is the simplest depreciation posture in the country. One schedule, one basis, one recapture calculation on sale. Texas investors who later move to a decoupling state should note that the basis difference follows the property, and that a mid-life relocation creates the dual schedule problem they avoided at purchase.

Cost Segregation Considerations Specific to Texas

The first Texas consideration is that everything rides on the federal side. Without a state deduction to add, the study has to justify itself on federal acceleration alone. It usually does. A $1.5 million Texas short-term rental with a 28% reclassification produces roughly $420,000 of accelerated deduction, worth about $155,000 federally at the top rate in the placed-in-service year.

The second is that Texas is a heavy short-term rental market, and the seven day average stay rule under the Sec. 469 regulations is where most Texas planning value sits. A property with an average guest stay of seven days or less is not a rental activity for passive loss purposes, so an owner who materially participates can use the loss against wage and business income. Texas has no state layer to complicate that analysis.

The third is property tax interaction. A cost segregation study allocates purchase price among building components for federal income tax purposes and does not change the appraisal district's assessed value. The two are separate systems. Investors sometimes worry that a study invites a reassessment, and it does not, because the appraisal district does not receive the study.

The fourth is entity structure. Texas LLCs and series LLCs are inexpensive and well supported by statute, and the absence of an income tax means entity choice in Texas is driven by liability, financing, and federal tax considerations rather than by state rate arbitrage. That is a simpler decision tree than in California or New York, and it is easy to over-engineer.

Texas Cost Segregation and Tax Questions

Does Texas have a state income tax?

No. Texas imposes no individual income tax, and a constitutional amendment approved in 2019 raised the barrier to enacting one. Texas does impose a franchise tax on entities, computed on taxable margin rather than net income, with no tax due for entities at or below the current revenue threshold of $2.47 million.

Does a cost segregation study reduce Texas franchise tax?

Generally no. The franchise tax is computed on taxable margin, which is revenue less the greater of cost of goods sold, compensation, 30% of revenue, or $1 million. Depreciation enters only through cost of goods sold, and real estate rental activity does not typically qualify for that subtraction, so accelerated depreciation does not reduce the franchise tax.

Does Texas allow bonus depreciation?

Yes, in the sense that there is nothing to disallow. With no personal income tax there is no Texas depreciation schedule for individuals or pass-through owners, so federal bonus depreciation under IRC Sec. 168(k) and Section 179 expensing apply in full with no addback and no state recomputation.

Will a cost segregation study raise my Texas property taxes?

No. A cost segregation study allocates purchase price among components for federal income tax depreciation and is not filed with or shared with the county appraisal district. Property tax assessment is a separate system based on the district's own valuation methods, and the study does not trigger a reassessment.

Is cost segregation worth it in Texas?

Usually yes for properties above roughly $500,000, and the analysis is simpler here than almost anywhere. The entire benefit is federal, so the decision comes down to whether you have income the accelerated deduction can offset, which for short-term rental owners who materially participate typically includes wage and business income.
