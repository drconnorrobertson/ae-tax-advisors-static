Cost Segregation Study in Florida

Florida tax advisory for real estate investors and business owners | Updated August 2026

Florida has no personal income tax, so for the individual investor and the pass-through owner a cost segregation study is a purely federal exercise. There is no state schedule, no addback, and no reconciliation. That is the situation for the large majority of Florida real estate investors.

Florida corporations are in a different position. The corporate income tax decouples from bonus depreciation and requires an addback that is recovered in equal installments over seven years. That distinction between the pass-through owner and the C corporation is the entire Florida conformity story.

How Florida Taxation Interacts With Federal Strategy

Florida imposes no individual income tax, and the state constitution prohibits one. There is also no estate tax and no state-level capital gains tax. For an individual investor, the marginal rate on rental income and on gain from a Florida property is the federal rate plus the 3.8% net investment income tax where applicable.

The Florida corporate income tax applies to C corporations and to entities taxed as corporations at 5.5% of Florida taxable income, which starts from federal taxable income with Florida adjustments. Partnerships and S corporations generally do not pay it, although Florida partnerships with corporate partners file an information return.

Florida also imposes a 6% state sales tax plus county surtax on commercial rent, one of the few states to tax commercial leases. The rate has been reduced repeatedly and applies to rental of commercial real property, not residential. For an investor holding retail, office, or industrial property in Florida this is a real operating cost that belongs in the underwriting.

Florida Pass-Through Entity Tax

Florida has no pass-through entity tax and no need for one. The PTET exists to move individual state income tax to the entity level so it escapes the federal SALT cap. Florida individuals have no state income tax to move.

This is one of the clearest advantages of Florida domicile for a business owner. Where a New York owner is running a PTET election, tracking credits, and managing addbacks to capture a federal deduction on state tax they had to pay anyway, a Florida owner simply does not pay the state tax.

The planning question for Florida owners is usually the opposite one: they have income sourced to other states. A Florida resident with rental property in Georgia, an operating business in New Jersey, or a syndication interest generating multistate K-1 income files nonresident returns in those states and may benefit from electing those states' PTETs on the income sourced there.

Mapping which states permit a nonresident owner to participate in a PTET election, and whether the resulting credit is usable, is where most of the state-level value for a Florida owner actually sits.

Florida Depreciation Conformity

For personal income tax purposes there is nothing to conform to. Individuals, partners, and S corporation shareholders resident in Florida use federal depreciation with no state adjustment, including full bonus depreciation under IRC Sec. 168(k) and Section 179 expensing at federal limits.

For corporate income tax purposes, Florida decouples. A corporation adds back the federal bonus depreciation deduction and then subtracts one seventh of the addback in each of the seven following years, including the year of the addback. The result is that Florida spreads the benefit rather than denying it, and the corporation recovers the full amount if it holds the asset long enough.

Florida generally conforms to federal Section 179 expensing for corporate purposes, which makes Section 179 more attractive than bonus depreciation for Florida C corporations placing qualifying property in service. That is a real planning lever, and it is one of the few places where the Section 179 versus bonus depreciation election is driven by state rather than federal considerations.

Cost Segregation Considerations Specific to Florida

The first Florida consideration is short-term rental density. Florida has one of the largest short-term rental markets in the country, and the seven day average stay rule under the Sec. 469 regulations is the single most valuable provision for Florida investors. A property with an average guest stay of seven days or less is not a rental activity for passive loss purposes, so a materially participating owner can apply the accelerated deduction against wage and business income.

The second is hurricane and casualty interaction. Florida properties experience storm damage and rebuild cycles, and a cost segregation study creates the component detail that makes a partial asset disposition election possible. When a roof or an HVAC system is replaced, the study lets you write off the remaining basis of the retired component instead of depreciating two roofs at once.

The third is that Florida property tax assessment is unaffected by the study. The county property appraiser values property under Florida law, and Save Our Homes and the non-homestead assessment caps limit annual increases. A federal cost segregation allocation is not filed with the appraiser and does not prompt a reassessment.

The fourth applies to Florida C corporations only: because the state addback is recovered one seventh per year, a corporation that expects to dispose of the property inside seven years should model the remaining unrecovered addback at disposition. Florida allows the remaining balance to come back, but the timing of the recovery is worth knowing before you sell.

Florida Cost Segregation and Tax Questions

Does Florida tax rental income?

Not at the individual level. Florida imposes no personal income tax, so rental income earned by an individual, a partner, or an S corporation shareholder is not taxed by Florida. Florida does impose a 5.5% corporate income tax on C corporations, and it imposes sales tax on commercial rent.

Does Florida allow bonus depreciation?

For individuals and pass-through owners the question does not arise, because Florida has no personal income tax. For corporate income tax purposes Florida decouples: the corporation adds back the federal bonus deduction and subtracts one seventh of the addback in each of seven years, so the benefit is spread rather than denied.

Does Florida have a pass-through entity tax?

No, and it does not need one. The PTET workaround converts individual state income tax into a federally deductible entity-level expense, and Florida individuals owe no state income tax. Florida residents with income sourced to other states should look at whether those states permit a PTET election on that sourced income.

Is cost segregation worth it on a Florida short-term rental?

Frequently yes. Florida has one of the deepest short-term rental markets in the country, and a property with an average guest stay of seven days or less is not treated as a rental activity under the passive loss regulations. An owner who materially participates can use the accelerated deduction against wage and business income, and there is no Florida addback to reduce it.

Will a cost segregation study affect my Florida property taxes?

No. The study is a federal income tax allocation of purchase price among building components. It is not filed with the county property appraiser and has no bearing on assessed value, which is determined under Florida property tax law and subject to the applicable assessment caps.
