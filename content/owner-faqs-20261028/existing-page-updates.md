# PTET Elections in 2026: Is the Election Still Worth It?

A pass-through entity tax election can still help a business owner in 2026. The answer depends on the owner's personal deduction, entity income, state credits and payment requirements. A useful projection compares the complete return with and without the election.

## Start with the current SALT rules

For 2026, the individual state and local tax deduction limit is generally $40,400, or $20,200 for married filing separately, before the income-based reduction. The limit decreases by 30% of modified adjusted gross income above $505,000, with separate-return adjustments, and cannot fall below the applicable $10,000 floor ($5,000 for married filing separately). See [Section 164](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/164) and the [IRS's 2026 tax-law overview](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-access/p6079_accessible.pdf).

That changes the comparison. An owner who can already deduct more personal state taxes may gain less from shifting a payment to the business. An owner whose cap has been reduced may have a different result. Itemizing, other deductions and the household's full income all matter.

## What the entity-level payment changes

[IRS Notice 2020-75](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-20-75.pdf) describes the federal deduction for qualifying state income taxes imposed on and paid by partnerships or S corporations. The payment reduces non-separately stated entity income rather than using the owner's individual SALT deduction limit.

The state side must also work. Identify which owners qualify, how their credits are calculated and whether another state recognizes the payment. For owners with several entities, map the election and payment separately for each one.

## Ask for a two-column projection

Use the same income forecast in both columns:

| Review item | Without election | With election |
|---|---|---|
| Entity income | Existing treatment | Qualifying entity payment reflected |
| Personal deductions | Actual usable SALT deduction | Remaining personal SALT deduction |
| State tax | Owner liability and payments | Entity liability plus owner credit or other relief |
| Other federal effects | Baseline | Recomputed QBI, AMT and applicable deduction limits |
| Cash timing | Owner payment schedule | Entity payment and owner-credit timing |
| Net result | Total tax and fees | Total tax and fees |

Multiplying the proposed PTET payment by the top federal rate is a screening estimate, not the final savings calculation. Ask the preparer to identify the incremental benefit after all interactions and additional compliance costs.

## Bring these records to the review

Gather the last entity and individual returns, a current profit forecast, ownership percentages, owner residences, state-source income, prior election confirmations and payment receipts. Include personal property-tax estimates and information about other businesses.

Ask for a calendar naming the person responsible for each election, payment, entity return and owner credit. Save the submission confirmations with the tax workpapers.

## Common questions

### Does the larger SALT deduction eliminate PTET planning?

No. It changes the baseline. Request an updated projection instead of renewing an election solely because it helped last year.

### Can I elect for a year that has already ended?

Check that state's election rules for that year. A federal amended return cannot by itself create a valid state election. California, for example, expressly requires its election on a timely original return.

### What if I live in one state and own a business in another?

Model both states together, including how the resident state treats the other state's entity tax and any owner credit.

# Amended Return or Form 3115: How to Correct Missed Deductions

Finding a missed deduction is the beginning of a tax review. Before filing a correction, identify what happened, which taxpayer reported it, and whether the issue is an error, an election or an accounting method. That determines the procedure and the year affected.

## When an amended return may fit

A prior return may need amendment when income, deductions, credits or other reported information was wrong. Individuals generally use Form 1040-X. Refund claims generally have a three-year-from-filing or two-year-from-payment deadline, whichever is later, with additional rules and exceptions. Early filing and special circumstances can affect the calculation. Use the [IRS amended-return guidance](https://www.irs.gov/filing/file-an-amended-return) to identify the applicable filing window.

The filing deadline and the amount refundable are separate questions. Review actual filing dates, tax payments and the refund lookback before assuming an older year can produce a refund.

A different business structure would not automatically have been available retroactively. If an election was missed, investigate its own relief rules before changing entity treatment on filed returns.

## When depreciation requires a different procedure

[IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) distinguishes corrections from accounting-method changes. Using an impermissible depreciation method on two or more consecutively filed returns generally establishes a method requiring a change procedure. Mathematical errors and other specified corrections can follow a different path.

A Form 3115 accounting-method change may include a Section 481(a) adjustment to prevent income or deductions from being duplicated or omitted. Eligibility matters more than which form seems convenient.

| Issue found | Starting point for review |
|---|---|
| Incorrect number or omitted income item | Determine whether an amended return is required |
| Depreciation method used consistently but incorrectly | Evaluate an accounting-method change |
| Wrong placed-in-service date | Review correction rules; do not assume it is a method change |
| Late or revoked depreciation election | Review the election's specific procedures |
| Entity error affecting owners | Coordinate entity, owner and state reporting |

## Keep the catch-up adjustment separate from current depreciation

Illustration only: assume a qualifying method change takes effect in 2026. The preparer reconciles depreciation allowed or allowable through the end of 2025 with depreciation previously claimed, subject to the applicable adjustment rules. The 2026 depreciation calculation is separate. Including 2026 in both the historical adjustment and current depreciation would duplicate a deduction.

A negative Section 481(a) adjustment generally decreases income in the year of change. Positive adjustments generally use a different spread period, with exceptions. The [Form 3115 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i3115) explain the general adjustment and filing framework; current procedures must also be checked.

A large depreciation correction may still produce a suspended loss rather than immediate cash savings. Recompute the relevant basis, at-risk, passive-activity and other loss limits before estimating a refund or reduction in tax.

## Build the correction file first

Bring the originally filed federal and state returns, subsequent amendments, depreciation schedules, acquisition closing statements, improvement invoices, ownership records and tax-payment history. Include any cost segregation report and prior Forms 3115.

Ask the reviewer for a short written explanation of:

- What was reported and what should have been reported.
- Which correction procedure applies and why.
- Which entities, owners, tax years and states are affected.
- How the proposed deduction changes actual tax and carryovers.
- Who files each form and retains the supporting evidence.

For a method change, confirm the current automatic-change eligibility, designated change number, required attachments and duplicate-copy requirements. [AE's Form 3115 and cost segregation service guide](https://www.aetaxadvisors.com/form-3115-cost-segregation/) explains the related engagement discussion.

## Common questions

### Does a cost segregation study always require amending old returns?

No. The study's findings must be matched to the filing history and applicable correction procedure. A study alone does not decide which form to file.

### Can Form 3115 guarantee a refund?

No. It addresses an accounting method. The usable tax effect depends on the return and applicable limitations.

### How long will a correction take?

Preparation depends on the records and filing package. For IRS processing, consult its current amended-return guidance rather than relying on a fixed turnaround promise.

### The owner is planning a QSBS stock sale

Qualified small business stock can make a C corporation attractive for a future stock sale. Start with the dates and eligibility records rather than assuming every owner receives a $10 million exclusion after five years.

The 2025 law introduced different rules for stock acquired after July 4, 2025. Subject to all requirements, that stock can qualify for a 50% gain exclusion after at least three years, 75% after four years and 100% after five years. Earlier stock follows its applicable acquisition-date rules. These are exclusion percentages, not tax rates. See the [IRS explanation of the expanded QSBS rules](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/understanding-the-working-families-tax-cuts-business-tax-provisions-youtube-video-text-script).

For the newer stock, the per-issuer eligible-gain limit generally uses the greater of a $15 million dollar limit or ten times qualifying basis, with statutory reductions and coordination for prior sales. Earlier stock generally uses the older $10 million dollar framework. The $15 million limit and applicable $75 million gross-asset threshold have inflation adjustments beginning after 2026. The asset test uses issuance timing and statutory asset measurement; it is not simply today's business valuation. See [Section 1202](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1202).

For stock issued after July 4, 2025, the gross-asset threshold is $75 million before the post-2026 inflation adjustments; stock issued on or before that date uses $50 million. A domestic C corporation, original issuance, qualifying assets and active-business requirements still matter. Certain businesses are excluded. Buying stock from another shareholder generally does not meet the original-issue requirement, although specific transfer exceptions exist. The [IRS Schedule D instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sd) summarize qualification requirements.

For an owner deciding on entity structure now, timing is practical. Stock first acquired after July 4, 2025 ordinarily cannot meet a three-year holding period during 2026. The newer exclusion tiers are relevant to planning a later exit, not an automatic exclusion for a sale this year. Special holding-period rules require their own review.

Before choosing or converting an entity, gather the capitalization table, subscription and issuance records, asset-test workpapers, contribution documents and business-activity history. Ask for a lot-by-lot analysis identifying the applicable dates, basis and exclusions, followed by a comparison of the proposed stock sale with an asset sale. A forecast should also address state treatment and the owner's cash needs.

A C corporation's potential exit benefit must be weighed against tax on operating earnings and distributions. An S corporation's pass-through treatment may better fit an owner who regularly withdraws profits. The answer comes from the operating and exit models together.
