[S-Corp Planning]
S Corp 60/40 Salary Rule: What to Use Instead|Build the salary from the owner's work|Begin with a role inventory: billable work, sales, management and administration. Assign time to each role and compare compensation for similar duties in the relevant market. A company with substantial staff-generated revenue may have a different labor profile from a solo consultant even when both show the same profit. Save the compensation sources and explain adjustments for experience, location and hours. A ratio can summarize the eventual result, but should not be the reason for choosing it.|Suppose two companies each have $300,000 before owner compensation. Applying 60% produces $180,000 in both. That arithmetic tells us nothing about whether one owner works 45 hours weekly and the other works eight. The missing fact is the value of each owner's services.|List duties and weekly hours;Separate owner-produced revenue from staff and capital;Retain dated compensation comparisons|Does the IRS approve a universal salary percentage?|No. An unsupported percentage does not establish reasonable compensation for the services the shareholder actually performs.
S Corp Salary When Profit Increases|Identify what caused the additional profit|Compare the current year with the year used in the last compensation review. Separate increased owner hours from higher prices, new employees, reduced overhead and new equipment. A larger tax profit can also reflect the expiration of depreciation deductions. None of these changes has the same implication for the value of the owner's labor. Update the salary memorandum when responsibilities materially change, and ask payroll to implement the supported amount prospectively or address any required correction.|A business increases profit from $200,000 to $400,000 after hiring a sales team. If the owner's role remains supervision, the profit increase alone does not establish that wages should double. If the owner instead doubles billable hours, the salary evidence changes.|Reconcile prior and current profit drivers;Update the owner job description;Review payroll already paid before changing it|Should salary automatically equal the same percentage every year?|No. Reassess actual duties and compensation evidence rather than preserving a ratio when the business changes.
S Corp Distributions Before Payroll: Correction Steps|Reconstruct payments before choosing a correction|Prepare a dated list of draws, company-paid personal costs, wages and reimbursements. Share it with both the return preparer and payroll provider. They need to assess whether payments require wage treatment and which payroll deposits, returns or information statements are affected. A year-end journal entry by itself does not prove that payroll reporting was completed. Avoid backdating payment records or assuming that labeling the next transfer payroll resolves earlier periods.|An owner receives twelve $5,000 draws and no wages. The resulting $60,000 payment history needs a compensation review; it is not automatically all salary or all distributions. Track the final classification and the corresponding payroll filings together.|Export every shareholder transfer;Identify service periods and existing wage records;Retain corrected filings and payment confirmations|Can I fix missing payroll with only a bookkeeping entry?|No. Bookkeeping, payroll reporting and actual tax deposits must be coordinated under the facts of the correction.
Part-Time S Corp Owner Salary: Duties and Hours|Compare part-time services with an appropriate role|Document the work performed for this corporation separately from work for another employer. A part-time manager might perform high-value sales or technical services even with limited hours. Conversely, an owner who only reviews reports has a different role from a full-time operator. Compensation comparisons should reflect the actual mix of services and time, rather than simply copying a full-time salary or choosing zero because another job provides a W-2.|An owner works ten hours each week in the corporation and forty at an outside employer. The outside salary does not pay for services to the corporation. A compensation review should address those ten hours and their responsibilities on their own merits.|Keep a corporation-specific work record;Describe delegated and retained responsibilities;Adjust comparable roles for actual time and duties|Does having another full-time job remove the wage issue?|No. Services and payments in the S corporation require their own analysis even when another employer already pays wages.
Different S Corp Owner Salaries: Equal Ownership|Separate the wage policy from shareholder economics|Prepare a responsibilities matrix for each owner, including revenue production, supervision, administration and time away from the business. Explain the wage differences with service-related evidence. Review distributions and shareholder rights separately with the preparer and legal adviser, because wages should not be used to disguise ownership arrangements. Equal ownership is relevant to shareholder economics, but does not establish that each individual performs equally valuable work.|Two 50% owners receive $90,000 and $40,000 in wages. The difference could reflect substantially different duties, but stock ownership alone cannot validate either amount. Retain the work comparison and review shareholder distributions as a separate schedule.|Map each owner's duties and hours;Support individual wage levels with comparisons;Review distribution rights independently of payroll|Can equal shareholders receive different wages?|Yes, when their services support the difference. Equal ownership does not require identical compensation for different work.
Zero S Corp Salary in a Loss Year|Review cash transfers and noncash deductions|Determine why the business reports a loss and whether the owner still received cash or personal benefits. Accelerated depreciation can produce a tax loss in a business with positive operating cash flow. A company with no payments and no available cash presents different facts from one distributing cash while reporting a paper loss. Have the adviser examine the services and payment history before treating the loss as a wage exemption.|A company has $80,000 of operating profit before a $100,000 depreciation deduction and makes owner transfers. Its $20,000 tax loss does not establish that the transfers can all bypass compensation analysis. The cash and service records still matter.|Bridge operating results to taxable loss;List distributions and company-paid personal costs;Document owner services and cash constraints|Does depreciation-generated loss automatically permit zero wages?|No. Noncash deductions do not resolve the treatment of payments to an owner who performs services.
S Corp Election for Real Estate Agents: Recurring Profit|Model the commission business across several years|Start with net commission income after ordinary operating expenses, rather than gross closings. Then compare reasonable compensation, employer payroll costs, preparation fees, state obligations and ongoing administration under each classification. Use a normal year, a strong year and a weak year. Rental holdings should be evaluated independently from the commission activity. Confirm election timing and eligibility before changing how payments or contracts are handled.|An agent has $250,000 net income in an exceptional year but expects $110,000 in a typical year. A model based only on the exceptional year can overstate recurring benefits. Compare all three scenarios before choosing the effective date.|Prepare recurring-profit scenarios;Obtain a role-based compensation estimate;Include state and annual compliance costs|Should one large closing determine my entity election?|It should inform the forecast, but a recurring-profit and compliance-cost comparison is more useful than a one-year headline.
Higher S Corp Salary: Payroll Tax and Retirement Tradeoffs|Compare the complete household result|Start with a supported compensation range. Within the defensible facts, ask the preparer to model wages, employer payroll costs, retirement contribution capacity and the qualified business income deduction together. Include the cost of staff contributions where applicable. A larger retirement deduction also commits cash to a retirement account, so compare after-tax liquidity as well as the current tax bill. Avoid optimizing one line of the return in isolation.|Compare a proposed $20,000 salary increase with the additional payroll expense and any actual increase in permitted employer retirement funding. The full $20,000 is not a new tax saving, and the retirement contribution is a separate cash commitment.|Establish reasonable compensation first;Request side-by-side total-tax projections;Compare retirement funding with cash available outside the plan|Does more salary always mean less total tax?|No. Payroll costs, retirement limits, QBI and other household facts can move the overall result in different directions.
S Corp Shareholder Health Insurance: Payroll Handoff|Reconcile premiums with year-end wage reporting|Identify who paid each premium, whether the corporation reimbursed the shareholder and whether the shareholder is subject to the more-than-2% rules. Send that schedule to payroll before W-2 preparation. The business payment, wage inclusion and possible individual deduction are connected but separate steps. Eligibility for subsidized coverage through another employer can affect the individual deduction. Do not assume that entering an insurance expense completes the owner reporting.|An owner pays $12,000 personally and later receives a documented corporate reimbursement. Reconcile the same $12,000 through the corporate books, applicable W-2 reporting and individual deduction review so it is neither omitted nor deducted twice.|Compile premium invoices and payment dates;Confirm ownership and other employer coverage eligibility;Reconcile reimbursement with W-2 reporting|Is a corporate insurance expense enough by itself?|No. Special shareholder payroll treatment and the individual's deduction requirements need a coordinated review.
S Corp Salary Documentation: Compensation Review File|Keep a reproducible compensation memorandum|A useful file explains the owner's work, the market evidence used and the reason the selected wage fits. Include changes in staffing, locations and billable responsibilities since the previous review. Preserve the dated sources rather than only a software-generated recommendation. If the owner performs several jobs, show how time was allocated among them. Revisit the memorandum when the business model changes, instead of treating a prior-year conclusion as permanent.|A physician performs clinical care three days weekly and manages a second location on another day. Comparing only a full-time clinician salary misses the management role and actual schedule. Describe both before selecting the compensation evidence.|Create a dated duties and time summary;Save compensation data and adjustments;Document changes from the prior review|What makes a salary review useful beyond a single number?|It should explain the duties, comparisons and adjustments well enough for another reviewer to reproduce the reasoning.
[Tax Compliance]
Estimated Taxes on Uneven Business Income|Match the payment analysis to dated income|Build a period-by-period profit schedule and include household wages, investment income and other activities. Ask the preparer whether the annualized income installment method changes the required installments compared with the regular method. This requires more than an annual profit total. Retain the underlying timing records and actual payment confirmations. Federal and state calculations can differ, so a federal payment schedule should not silently become the state schedule.|A business earns little early in the year and completes a large project in the final quarter. Dividing the eventual annual tax into four equal numbers does not explain when the income arose. A dated income schedule permits a more accurate installment analysis.|Prepare income and deductions by installment period;Reconcile household withholding and payments;Compare regular and annualized installment calculations|Can I just divide December's final tax estimate by four?|That may not reflect the timing of income. The annualized method may be relevant when earnings are uneven.
Estimated Tax Safe Harbor vs April Balance Due|Calculate penalty protection and cash reserve separately|Ask for two figures: the amount needed under an applicable payment safe harbor and the projected final tax liability. The difference is a funding issue even if the installment schedule avoids an underpayment penalty. When current income rises, update the reserve without assuming that prior-year-based payments cover the full new bill. Confirm which safe harbor applies and retain the prior return used in the calculation.|Suppose a projection shows $150,000 of final tax and $90,000 of credited payments. The remaining $60,000 still needs funding even if the payment pattern met an applicable safe harbor. This arithmetic does not establish safe-harbor eligibility.|Identify the applicable safe-harbor calculation;Forecast current-year total tax;Reserve the difference after all credited payments|Can I owe a large balance without an estimated-tax penalty?|Yes. Meeting a payment safe harbor and fully paying the year's final tax are different outcomes.
Spouse W-2 Withholding for Business Income|Coordinate withholding at the household level|On a joint-return projection, reconcile both spouses' income and withholding before choosing additional payments. Use the latest paystub and remaining payroll dates to estimate what an updated withholding election can actually collect. Consider cash available from each source and keep federal and state elections separate. An anticipated bonus or payroll adjustment should not be counted as paid withholding until payroll confirms it occurred.|A projection identifies a $24,000 funding gap with six paychecks remaining. An additional $4,000 each paycheck would close that arithmetic gap if payroll can implement it and the projection remains accurate. The appropriate penalty analysis still needs its own review.|Collect current paystubs for both spouses;Confirm remaining payroll dates and changes;Reconcile actual year-end withholding to the projection|Does extra withholding reduce the amount of taxable business profit?|No. It prepays household tax; it does not create a deduction against business income.
S Corp Retained Earnings and Personal Estimated Tax|Plan for taxable income without cash distributions|Reconcile expected K-1 income with wages, shareholder basis and the corporation's cash needs. The operating company may retain cash for inventory or hiring while shareholders still face tax on allocated income. Discuss a distribution policy early enough to avoid a surprise personal funding shortfall. Retained cash also does not convert an S corporation into a C corporation tax regime. Keep the corporate forecast and household tax forecast connected.|The company retains $100,000 of current earnings for expansion. A shareholder's tax calculation can still include their allocated taxable income even though no cash distribution arrives. The amount of tax depends on the complete household return.|Project K-1 taxable income;Compare corporate cash needs with owner tax funding;Record distributions separately from taxable profit|Does leaving cash in the corporate bank account defer shareholder tax?|Generally, no. Pass-through taxable income and the timing of shareholder distributions are separate matters.
Business Tax Reserve: Revenue vs Taxable Profit|Bridge the bookkeeping profit to the tax forecast|Start with reconciled operating results. Adjust for tax depreciation, nondeductible spending, owner payments and other book-tax differences, then combine the result with household income. A percentage-of-revenue rule can be a temporary cash-management habit, but should be tested against the forecast. Update the reserve after acquisitions, asset sales and major margin changes. Avoid counting a retirement contribution as both an unpaid future plan and an expense already funded.|Two businesses collect $1 million. One has $100,000 profit and the other $400,000 before tax adjustments. An identical revenue-based reserve ignores that difference and can leave one household underfunded while unnecessarily restricting the other's cash.|Reconcile the profit and loss statement;Identify book-tax adjustments and household income;Update the reserve after material changes|Is a flat percentage of sales a reliable tax calculation?|No. It can support cash discipline, but taxable profit and the household projection determine the actual funding need.
Missed Estimated Tax Payment: What a Later Payment Fixes|Retain the original installment timeline|List each installment's due date, payment date and credited amount. A catch-up payment can stop an unpaid balance from continuing, but it does not automatically make earlier installments timely. Ask whether withholding, annualization or other applicable exceptions change the computation. Avoid altering the transaction history to make the schedule appear even. The preparer needs the dates the taxing authority actually credited payments.|An owner omits an installment and sends a larger payment months later. The annual total may look sufficient while the period-by-period analysis still identifies an underpayment. Preserve both confirmations and ask for the actual penalty calculation.|Build a dated payment ledger;Identify when the related income arose;Evaluate annualization and available exceptions|Does paying double at the next deadline erase every earlier penalty?|Not automatically. Installment timing and applicable exceptions determine the result, rather than the annual total alone.
Business Return Extension vs Tax Payment Deadline|Map the entity return and owner payment obligations|Identify the corporation, partnership and individual filings involved and distinguish their filing extensions from payment requirements. A pass-through extension may delay the final K-1 without delaying the owner's need to estimate tax. Reconcile extensions with estimated balances, payment confirmations and later updated information. Do not assume a single extension covers every state, entity and household filing in a multi-entity structure.|A partnership expects to extend its return while the partners need their individual estimates. The firm can prepare provisional tax information for payment planning and update it when the K-1 is final. The payment estimate is not a substitute for the completed entity return.|List each return and original deadline;Estimate balances before payment deadlines;Retain extension and payment confirmations separately|Does an extended partnership return automatically extend a partner's payment date?|No. The partner must evaluate their own payment obligations even when final pass-through information is delayed.
Rental Sale Estimated Taxes: Gain vs Closing Cash|Prepare a sale projection before distributing proceeds|Use the sale price, selling costs, historical adjusted basis and depreciation records to calculate the tax items. Reconcile mortgage payoff separately because it affects cash proceeds without serving as the tax basis. Include any exchange structure, suspended losses and other household items in the preparer's model. Schedule the appropriate federal and state payments based on the timing and character of the sale income.|A $600,000 sale with $30,000 selling costs and $350,000 adjusted basis produces a simplified $220,000 gain before other adjustments. A $450,000 loan payoff affects remaining cash, but does not replace that gain calculation.|Obtain the settlement statement and asset history;Separate mortgage payoff from adjusted basis;Update installment and cash-reserve schedules|Can a sale create tax even when the lender takes most proceeds?|Yes. Cash after debt payoff and taxable gain measure different things.
Applying Last Year's Refund to Estimated Tax|Verify the overpayment election and account credit|Read the filed return to see whether the overpayment was designated for refund, applied forward or split. Then check the account record and any offsets before reducing new installments. An expected carryforward is not available twice, and an amended return can complicate the timeline. Use the applicable estimated-tax instructions to determine how the credited overpayment enters the payment calculation.|A $15,000 overpayment is split into a $5,000 refund and $10,000 applied forward. Only the designated $10,000 belongs in the next-year credit schedule, subject to actual account processing. Do not also count the refunded cash as a payment.|Read the filed refund election;Match it to account credits and offsets;Include each credited dollar once in the payment ledger|Can I count the full overpayment if part was refunded?|No. Separate the refunded portion from the amount actually elected and credited toward the following year.
Midyear Tax Projection for Multiple Businesses|Build one household model from separate activity records|Request current books for each company and property, year-to-date payroll, known investment events and prior carryforwards. Distinguish current income from prior-year distributions and financing. Mark uncertain assumptions explicitly, such as an unsigned asset purchase or an unapproved retirement contribution. The projection should show what changes if those assumptions fail, alongside a payment schedule and a cash reserve. Update it after major transactions.|An owner receives income from two S corporations and three rentals. A projection based only on the largest company can miss a rental sale or a suspended loss. Keep separate activity schedules feeding the same household model.|Collect every activity's current financials;Reconcile carryforwards and payments;Show committed and proposed planning separately|Should a proposed purchase be treated as a completed deduction?|No. Model it as an assumption until the relevant transaction and eligibility facts are established.
[Business Deductions]
Owner Personal Card Expenses: Corporate Reimbursement|Connect the receipt to one reimbursement|An owner expense report should identify the merchant, date, amount, business purpose and company that incurred the cost. Attach the invoice and show whether any personal share was removed. Record the corporate reimbursement against that report and confirm the cost was not already imported from another account. A personally paid expense does not become nondeductible merely because of the card, but reimbursement rules and the underlying expense restrictions still apply.|A $240 software invoice is paid personally and reimbursed by the corporation. The company should not also deduct a second $240 from an imported credit-card feed. Assign an expense-report identifier so payment and deduction can be reconciled.|Attach itemized support and business purpose;Separate personal amounts before approval;Match reimbursement to the ledger once|Can my card statement replace the underlying invoice?|It can show payment, but may not establish what was purchased or its business purpose.
Accountable Plans and Personal Meals|Determine meal eligibility before reimbursement|Separate ordinary personal meals from meals that qualify under the applicable business rules. Record the participants, business relationship, purpose, date and amount for a qualifying claim. The reimbursement policy does not eliminate limitations on the underlying deduction. Certain categories have different rules, so classify meals by their actual facts instead of putting every restaurant charge in one account. Ask the preparer to review unusual events independently.|An owner submits lunch eaten alone near home and a separate documented business meal with a client. Both used the same card, but the card and reimbursement policy do not make their tax treatment identical.|Identify the qualifying business event;Retain itemized receipt and attendee details;Apply the correct meal category and limitation|Does reimbursement make an ordinary personal lunch deductible?|No. An accountable arrangement cannot supply a business purpose that the meal itself lacks.
Mileage Reimbursement for a Mixed-Use Owner Vehicle|Separate each trip by purpose|Keep a log showing dates, destinations, business purposes and business miles, along with the records needed for the chosen method. Personal use and ordinary commuting should not be included simply because the company reimburses the owner. Coordinate standard-mileage and actual-expense treatments with the preparer, including any prior depreciation elections. A vehicle percentage calculated from memory at year-end is weaker evidence than records tied to actual trips.|A day includes 18 miles between jobsites, 12 miles to a personal appointment and a routine commute. Only qualifying business legs belong in the reimbursement analysis. The fact that all trips happened during a workday does not make them identical.|Record business trips as they occur;Separate commuting and personal legs;Confirm the method before combining reimbursements and expenses|Can I reimburse every mile driven on a business day?|No. The purpose and applicable rules for each trip determine whether it qualifies.
Business Trip With Vacation: Expense Allocation|Allocate the itinerary before totaling deductions|Write down where the trip occurred, its primary purpose, business days, personal days and who traveled. Domestic and foreign travel can require different analysis. Separate incremental personal lodging and family expenses from costs connected to qualifying business activities. Save conference materials or appointment records alongside receipts. A brief meeting during a vacation should not be used as a blanket justification for all airfare and lodging.|A conference lasts three days and the owner stays four additional nights for vacation. Keep the conference schedule, hotel nightly detail and personal extension separate before the preparer determines transportation and lodging treatment.|Preserve the complete dated itinerary;Separate business and personal travelers and days;Review domestic or foreign allocation rules|Does one business meeting make an entire vacation deductible?|No. The overall purpose, itinerary and specific expense rules still control.
Business Owner Commuting vs Business Mileage|Identify regular workplaces and qualifying exceptions|Map the home, regular workplace, temporary assignments and trips between work locations. Ordinary travel to a regular workplace is generally personal even when the traveler owns the company. A qualifying principal-place-of-business home office can change the analysis of certain trips, but keeping a laptop at home does not establish eligibility. Record the actual route and work purpose rather than claiming a round number of miles each month.|A salon owner drives from home to the regular salon and later between two operating locations. The second trip has different facts from the daily commute. Review each route separately and retain the location schedule.|List regular and temporary work locations;Evaluate any qualifying home office facts;Log business routes separately from commuting|Does owning the destination business make the commute deductible?|No. Ownership alone does not change ordinary commuting into qualifying business transportation.
Electronic Expense Receipts: What Records Must Show|Keep a readable evidence trail|A digital receipt should retain item detail, date, amount and merchant information. Pair it with the business purpose and any specially required substantiation. Store records in a retrievable format and test that exports remain readable after changing software. A bank feed proves a charge occurred but may not identify whether it was supplies, a personal purchase or a refundable deposit. Retain a clear link between the evidence and ledger entry.|A hotel charge of $900 could include business lodging, a personal extra night and family charges. The itemized digital folio and itinerary allow a review that the single card transaction cannot support.|Save itemized digital evidence;Add business purpose and allocation notes;Keep records accessible after account or software changes|Are electronic records automatically insufficient?|No. Their usefulness depends on whether they preserve the required information and remain available for review.
Accountable Plan Travel Advances: Excess Cash|Close each advance with actual expenses|Track the date and purpose of the advance, the expenses substantiated and the balance returned. The arrangement must satisfy reasonable-period requirements; use the applicable IRS guidance when setting deadlines. Unsubstantiated amounts or excess funds retained can require nonaccountable-plan wage treatment. Keep the advance separate from final expenses until reconciliation so the company does not deduct both the initial transfer and the later receipts.|The company advances $2,000 for travel. Supported expenses total $1,550, leaving $450 to return. The payment trail should show the return of excess funds or the preparer's resolution of any amount retained.|Create an advance identifier and deadline;Match actual substantiated expenses;Return or properly report the remaining balance|Is the initial advance itself the final deductible expense?|No. Reconcile it with qualifying actual costs and any excess returned or otherwise reported.
Travel to Inspect a Potential Rental Property|Separate acquisition work from ongoing operations|Record the properties visited, meetings, trip dates and reason for each expense. Determine whether the owner is operating an existing activity, investigating a new one or incurring costs tied to a particular acquisition. Costs connected with buying property can require capitalization rather than a current travel deduction. Mixed personal travel needs a separate allocation. The outcome should follow the activity and transaction facts, not the label scouting trip.|An investor combines a family vacation with inspections of three possible rentals and later buys one. Keep property-specific expenses separate from shared travel and personal days before deciding which amounts are current, capital or personal.|Document each property's connection to the trip;Separate family and operating expenses;Ask the preparer about acquisition capitalization|Is every real estate scouting trip currently deductible?|No. The activity, acquisition connection and personal component can change the treatment.
Spouse Business Travel: Employee Role and Purpose|Evaluate the accompanying person's expenses separately|A spouse's airfare or lodging should not be included merely because the owner had a qualifying trip. Establish the spouse's actual role, bona fide business purpose and applicable deductibility requirements. A job title added after travel does not replace evidence of services performed. Where the spouse travels for personal reasons, separate incremental charges from the owner's eligible costs, including any difference between single and shared accommodation costs.|The owner attends a trade conference while a spouse joins for sightseeing. The owner's conference records do not substantiate the spouse's ticket. If the spouse instead performs real employee duties, preserve the agenda and work evidence for a separate review.|Identify actual employee duties and work performed;Retain the spouse's business itinerary;Separate incremental personal travel charges|Does attending a company dinner establish a spouse's business purpose?|Not by itself. Review actual services and the requirements applicable to accompanying travelers.
Personal Charges on Owner Expense Reports|Correct both the reimbursement and company books|Identify the personal items before approving the report. If the company already paid them, determine whether the owner repaid the amount or whether compensation, a distribution or another treatment applies. The choice depends on the entity and transaction facts. Retain the correction trail so the annual profit report and shareholder schedule agree. Do not delete the original charge without recording where the money went.|An expense report totals $1,200, including $250 of personal groceries. The business review starts with the remaining $950, subject to its own support. If all $1,200 was reimbursed, resolve the extra $250 rather than leaving it in operating expenses.|Flag the personal amount and reason;Record repayment or supported owner treatment;Reconcile corrected expense and shareholder ledgers|Can I leave personal costs in expenses if the business is profitable?|No. Profitability does not create a business deduction for personal spending.
[Retirement Planning]
S Corp Distributions vs 401(k) Compensation|Separate contribution capacity from cash funding|Ask the plan administrator to identify eligible compensation under the actual document. An S corporation shareholder's distributions do not replace W-2 compensation for calculating plan contributions. Increasing wages can change payroll costs, QBI and contribution capacity, so model the combined result before making a payroll change. Cash available to fund a contribution and the amount the plan permits are separate constraints. Coordinate employee deferrals, employer contributions and amounts already contributed through other employment.|An owner receives $50,000 of wages and $200,000 of distributions. The plan calculation cannot simply treat the combined $250,000 as wages. Use the eligible compensation and applicable annual rules for each contribution type.|Obtain the plan's compensation definition;Reconcile payroll and all current contributions;Model payroll and funding changes together|Do distributions increase my eligible plan compensation?|No. Distributions can provide cash, but do not themselves establish compensation for S corporation retirement contributions.
Solo 401(k) After Hiring Employees|Review employee eligibility before the plan remains owner-only|Send the administrator the hire dates, service hours, ages and ownership relationships of all workers. Review the document and applicable eligibility requirements, including rules that may cover part-time employees. Calling a worker part-time or a contractor does not settle their status or eligibility. If an employee should have participated, ask about correction rather than waiting until the next plan year. Related-company employees may also matter.|A consultant starts with an owner-only plan and later hires an assistant who works a regular schedule. Keep the service history from the first day. Eligibility review should occur before assuming the plan can continue excluding everyone except the owner.|Maintain a complete employee census;Review eligibility and related employers with the administrator;Resolve any missed participation promptly|Does hiring one worker immediately answer the plan question?|No. The worker's status, service, plan terms and applicable eligibility rules need review.
Cash Balance Plan After a One-Time Profit Spike|Stress-test funding before adopting the plan|Ask the actuary to show required funding under several profit scenarios and explain how employee costs change. A defined benefit plan carries obligations that differ from an optional annual business expenditure. Compare the owner's desired contribution with staff funding, fees and available working capital. If cash flow later falls, discuss plan amendments, funding obligations and any termination process with the administrator instead of simply skipping a contribution.|A business receives a one-time $500,000 project profit. Model the following years at its ordinary earnings before adopting a plan around that windfall. A large first-year deduction does not demonstrate sustainable future funding.|Prepare a multi-year operating cash forecast;Obtain owner and employee funding estimates;Discuss low-profit-year options before adoption|Can I treat required funding as optional whenever sales decline?|No. Review the actual plan obligations and available changes with the actuary and administrator.
Retirement Plan Employee Costs: Full Company Budget|Compare total employer outlay with owner benefit|Request a proposal showing owner allocations, staff contributions, administration, actuarial costs and other fees separately. Use accurate compensation and service data, not only the number of employees. Ownership in other businesses can affect the population considered. Compare both the current design and a simpler alternative using the same census. This makes the cost of achieving the owner's retirement goal visible before the company commits cash.|A proposal allocates $100,000 to the owner, $30,000 to staff and $5,000 in fees. The company funding requirement is $135,000 in this illustration, not $100,000. The resulting deduction and tax savings require a separate calculation.|Provide a complete employee and ownership census;Request itemized owner and staff costs;Compare cash requirements across plan alternatives|Is the owner's contribution the entire cost of a business plan?|No. Employee funding and administration can materially change the company's total commitment.
Spouse Eligibility for a Business Retirement Plan|Connect employment evidence with plan compensation|Document the spouse's duties, hours and reasonable compensation for actual services. Then confirm the employer's plan terms and the spouse's contributions through other work. Marriage does not independently establish participation or authorize an arbitrary payroll amount. Ownership attribution and related-employer rules can affect the analysis. Keep the employment file and plan census consistent so the administrator works from the same facts as payroll and the preparer.|A spouse performs bookkeeping and scheduling for twelve hours weekly. Their job record and eligible pay belong in the plan proposal. A spouse who provides no services presents a different case, even if the household wants a larger deduction.|Record genuine services and supported pay;Disclose ownership and other employer plans;Confirm eligibility and annual contribution limits|Can I put a spouse on payroll only to create a deduction?|A defensible plan contribution begins with genuine employment, eligible compensation and compliance with plan requirements.
Two Businesses and 401(k) Deferral Limits|Give each administrator the complete plan picture|Identify all plans receiving employee deferrals, including plans at an unrelated W-2 job. Applicable elective-deferral aggregation rules are distinct from employer contribution limits. For employer limits and testing, disclose ownership and related-business relationships rather than assuming a separate EIN produces a separate allowance. Ask for a written allocation of contributions across plans before payroll processes the final deposits.|An owner defers through a day job and a side-business plan. Both administrators need the amounts already deferred elsewhere. Two bank accounts and two EINs do not by themselves permit the same annual individual limit twice.|List every plan and contribution type;Disclose direct and attributed ownership;Confirm aggregate employee deferrals before final payroll|Does a second EIN automatically double my employee deferral limit?|No. Individual aggregation rules and related-employer analysis apply independently of the number of EINs.
Rental Income and Solo 401(k) Eligibility|Identify the income source before calculating contributions|Determine whether the receipts arise from ordinary investment rentals or an actual operating activity with different tax treatment. Owning an LLC or reporting a property profit does not independently create retirement-plan earned income. If the owner also operates a separate service business, evaluate that business's eligible earnings on their own terms. Do not relabel rental receipts or route them through another entity solely to manufacture compensation.|An owner has $80,000 of ordinary rental profit and $30,000 of eligible consulting earnings. Those are different income streams for retirement-plan analysis. The rental profit should not automatically be added to consulting compensation.|Identify each activity's tax classification;Reconcile eligible earnings with the plan administrator;Avoid assuming LLC ownership changes income character|Does putting a rental in an LLC make its profit retirement compensation?|No. Legal ownership form alone does not transform ordinary investment rental income into eligible earned income.
Retirement Deduction vs Future Taxable Withdrawals|Compare today's deduction with future access and tax|Identify whether the contribution is deductible, Roth or after-tax under the actual plan. Deductible contributions generally defer tax, with future distributions subject to the applicable rules. Compare the current marginal benefit, expected retirement withdrawals, liquidity constraints and distribution requirements. An account funded for retirement is an asset, not money permanently removed from tax analysis. Avoid presenting the contribution amount as an equal amount of tax savings.|A hypothetical $50,000 deduction at an assumed 30% marginal rate produces a simplified $15,000 current tax reduction. Financed Rental Purchase: Debt, Basis and Loss Limits|Separate acquisition basis from cash invested|A property purchase financed with genuine debt can include amounts beyond the down payment in acquisition basis. [1031 Exchanges]
Receiving Sale Proceeds Before a 1031 Exchange|Arrange the exchange before closing disbursements|Talk with the qualified intermediary and tax adviser before selling, signing final instructions or directing proceeds. Preserve agreements and closing instructions, and distinguish a completed taxable sale from a transaction that still has a viable exchange path.|A seller receives proceeds in a personal bank account and buys another rental a month later. The later purchase does not automatically convert the earlier sale into a qualifying deferred exchange. Rental Mortgage Payoff vs Taxable Sale Gain|Calculate gain independently from debt settlement|Start with amount realized, selling costs and adjusted tax basis. Section 1031 Real Estate vs Business Equipment|Separate real property from personal property|Current Section 1031 treatment applies to qualifying real property, not a general swap of business assets. A transaction involving a furnished building may therefore need separate analysis for its components. Have the adviser and intermediary review the proposed assets rather than assuming everything conveyed with real estate receives the same treatment.|An investor sells a rental building and wants to acquire restaurant equipment. There is no universal holding period that proves investment intent in every transaction. Keep contemporaneous evidence of the property's purpose and changes in plan rather than relying on a last-minute description as an investment.|An operator buys, renovates and immediately markets homes as part of a recurring resale business. 1031 Exchange and Future Depreciation Recapture|Model deferral and the replacement property's basis|A qualifying exchange can defer eligible gain, but is not a blanket promise to permanently erase depreciation-related tax. Carryover basis, additional investment, nonqualifying property and future dispositions affect the outcome. Cost-segregated components can raise asset-specific questions that should be reviewed with the preparer. Compare current recognized gain and the replacement asset's future tax history rather than summarizing the transaction as no tax forever.|An owner exchanges a depreciated rental for a larger property. The new purchase price does not necessarily become entirely fresh depreciation basis. 1031 Replacement Property in a Different LLC|Compare federal taxpayer identity before retitling|Determine how the selling owner and proposed buying entity are classified for federal tax purposes. Adding a partner or changing classification during the exchange can introduce substantial complications. Coordinate the deed, financing, ownership documents and tax structure with the intermediary before closing.|An individual sells through a wholly owned disregarded LLC and proposes buying through a newly formed two-member LLC. Keeping Cash in a 1031 Exchange: Recognized Gain|Model cash, liabilities and nonqualifying property together|Ask the preparer to show realized gain, recognized gain and deferred gain separately. Cash retained can trigger current recognition, while debt relief and replacement liabilities can also affect the calculation. Recognition is limited and characterized under applicable rules, so neither all cash is tax nor all tax is deferred is a reliable shortcut. Reserve enough liquidity for the modeled liability before committing the rest of the proceeds.|An investor retains $40,000 while acquiring replacement property. 1031 Deadlines and Financing Delays|Build the deadline calendar from the transfer date|A deferred exchange generally has a 45-day identification period and completion by the earlier of 180 days or the relevant return due date including extensions. A closing statement alone cannot supply the entire historical tax calculation.|A simplified property begins at $300,000 basis, adds $50,000 of improvements and has $80,000 depreciation adjustments.
