# Calculating and reporting installment gain
rental-installment-sale-gross-profit-percentage|How do I calculate the gross profit percentage on a seller-financed rental sale?|Divide installment gross profit by the contract price. Gross profit is selling price minus adjusted basis, selling expenses and ordinary depreciation recapture. Contract price can differ from selling price when debt is assumed.|A rental sells for $400,000 with no assumed debt. Its adjusted basis is $250,000, selling expenses are $20,000, and ordinary recapture is $10,000. Installment gross profit is $120,000 and the percentage is 30%. The $10,000 of ordinary recapture is reported in the sale year.|How much gain comes from a $50,000 principal payment?|At 30%, the payment generates $15,000 of installment gain. The remaining $35,000 is basis recovery. Separate any interest before applying the rate.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
seller-financed-rental-note-interest-principal|How do I report interest and principal from a seller-financed rental note?|Separate interest from the payment before applying the installment gross profit percentage. Interest generally produces ordinary interest income. The remaining amount is principal, which can include gain and recovery of basis.|A year's note payments total $27,000, including $7,000 of stated interest and $20,000 of principal. With a 40% gross profit percentage, the seller has $8,000 of installment gain, $12,000 of basis recovery, and $7,000 of interest income.|Does zero stated interest always mean all payments are principal?|A note with insufficient stated interest can trigger unstated interest or original issue discount rules. Check the terms and the applicable rules before treating the entire payment as principal.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
rental-installment-buyer-assumes-mortgage-under-basis|What happens to installment reporting when the buyer assumes a mortgage below my basis?|If the qualifying mortgage assumed is no greater than the installment sale basis, the debt generally reduces contract price and is not treated as a payment to you.|The selling price is $500,000, installment sale basis is $320,000 and the buyer assumes a qualifying $200,000 mortgage. Contract price is $300,000. Gross profit is $180,000, making the installment gain rate 60%.|What if the buyer gives me $30,000 in cash at closing?|At a 60% gain rate, the $30,000 cash payment generally produces $18,000 of installment gain for the sale year.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
rental-installment-assumed-debt-exceeds-basis|Does a buyer's mortgage assumption create gain when the debt exceeds my installment basis?|A qualifying mortgage assumed above your installment sale basis generally creates a deemed payment for the excess in the sale year. Add that excess back when computing contract price.|A $500,000 sale has $240,000 of installment sale basis and $300,000 of qualifying mortgage debt assumed. The debt exceeds basis by $60,000. Contract price is $260,000, gross profit is also $260,000, and the $60,000 deemed payment produces $60,000 of installment gain in the sale year.|Does the deemed payment include cash the seller gets at closing?|Track closing cash separately and add it to the deemed payment when calculating total sale-year payments.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
# Closing terms and return choices
installment-sale-furnished-rental-allocate-assets|How do I allocate a seller-financed sale of a furnished rental among assets?|Identify the different assets, allocate the selling price and determine installment eligibility for each. Use the allocation to calculate asset-level gain and any ordinary depreciation recapture.|A furnished rental sells for $420,000. A supported allocation assigns $70,000 to land, $330,000 to the building, and $20,000 to furniture. If the furniture has $4,000 of adjusted basis and $16,000 of prior depreciation, its $16,000 gain is ordinary recapture in the sale year, assuming no furniture selling costs.|What if the transaction includes an operating business?|Review the allocation rules for a sale of a trade or business, including whether Form 8594 and the residual method apply.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
elect-out-rental-installment-sale-reporting|Can I elect out of installment reporting on a seller-financed rental property?|Yes. For a qualifying installment sale, an election out generally reports the full gain in the year of sale using the applicable amount realized rules. Make the election by the sale-year return due date including extensions.|A seller receives $50,000 of cash and a note at closing. If the note's fair market value is $250,000 and there is no other consideration, the general election-out computation starts with $300,000 of amount realized before subtracting basis and applicable selling costs.|How do I make the election?|Report the sale using the applicable full-gain reporting forms for the sale-year return, generally Form 4797 or Form 8949 depending on the property. Keep the note valuation and closing documents.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
irrevocable-escrow-rental-installment-sale|Does an irrevocable escrow change installment treatment for a rental sale?|When the buyer fully funds an irrevocable escrow to make the payments and the seller relies on that escrow rather than on the buyer, the deposited purchase price is generally treated as paid when funded. A substantial restriction on access can affect the analysis.|A seller closes a $400,000 rental sale with $80,000 cash and $320,000 in a fully funded irrevocable escrow that will pay the scheduled principal. If no substantial restriction applies, the seller generally treats all $400,000 as received when the escrow is funded at closing.|What records establish whether a restriction is substantial?|Retain the escrow agreement and any provisions showing the buyer's bona fide purpose and the exact conditions that restrict payment to the seller.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
form-6252-rental-note-payment-years|Do I file Form 6252 every year I receive payments for a seller-financed rental?|Use Form 6252 to report a qualifying installment sale in the year of sale and in subsequent years for the installment obligation, subject to the form instructions. Carry the installment gain to the applicable return form and track interest separately.|An owner receives principal at closing and in each of the next three years. The owner retains the original basis and gross profit calculation and uses the payment records when preparing Form 6252 for the sale year and payment years.|What records make later-year reporting easier?|Keep the first Form 6252, closing statement, signed note and a payment schedule that separates principal, interest and any modification or payoff.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
# Later changes to the installment agreement
seller-financed-rental-price-reduction-refigure-installment-gain|How does a later price reduction change installment gain on a seller-financed rental sale?|Recompute the gross profit using the reduced selling price, subtract installment gain already reported and divide the remaining gross profit by remaining installment principal. Apply the new percentage to remaining payments under the IRS rules.|An original sale had $120,000 of gross profit. After the seller reports $40,000 of installment gain, a price reduction lowers total gross profit to $100,000. If $150,000 of principal remains, the revised gain percentage is 40%.|Should I reclassify interest as part of the revised principal?|Keep interest separate. Use the revised agreement and payment history to reconcile remaining principal before applying the new percentage.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
seller-financed-rental-repossession-note-basis|How do I calculate the basis of a seller-financed rental after repossession?|When the real property repossession rules apply, add the adjusted basis of the installment obligation, qualifying repossession costs and taxable repossession gain. Use the unpaid principal less its deferred gross profit to determine the note basis.|At repossession a note has $100,000 of unpaid principal and a 30% gross profit percentage, leaving $70,000 of note basis. With $5,000 of qualifying costs and $12,000 of taxable repossession gain, the resulting property basis is $87,000.|Does the unpaid note balance alone determine the new basis?|Use the full calculation in Worksheet E of IRS Publication 537. Keep the note ledger and the separate repossession gain calculation.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
# Selling or transferring the installment note
selling-seller-financed-rental-note-gain|How do I calculate gain when I sell a note from a seller-financed rental sale?|Selling the installment obligation generally triggers gain or loss equal to the amount realized on the note sale less its adjusted basis. Basis generally equals unpaid principal less the profit still deferred on that principal.|A note has $80,000 of unpaid principal and a 25% gross profit percentage. Its basis is $60,000. If the seller receives $72,000 for the note with no other adjustments, the note sale produces $12,000 of gain.|Does the original sale affect the character of that gain?|The gain or loss on the obligation generally takes its character from the original sale. Keep the initial reporting and track any separately stated interest.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
gift-seller-financed-rental-note-deferred-gain|Can giving away a seller-financed rental note trigger taxable gain?|An outright gift of an installment obligation generally counts as a disposition. Compare its fair market value at the transfer date with its adjusted basis. Check whether a special transfer rule applies before computing gain or loss.|A landlord gives an installment note to an adult child. It has $50,000 of adjusted basis and $65,000 of supported fair market value. Assuming ordinary gift disposition rules apply, the transfer produces $15,000 of gain for the donor.|What should I keep to support the transfer value?|Retain the signed note and transfer documents, payment history, valuation support and the calculation of deferred gain still embedded in the obligation.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
# Installment notes after the seller's death
inherited-rental-installment-note-future-payments|How do I report payments on a seller-financed rental note I inherited?|If you inherit the note as someone other than the buyer, the transfer at the seller's death generally does not accelerate the outstanding installment gain. Report later payments using the seller's installment treatment, with interest accounted for separately.|An heir receives a $10,000 principal payment on an inherited note with a 40% gross profit percentage and $700 of stated interest. Assuming the original percentage still applies, the heir reports $4,000 of installment gain and accounts for the $700 interest separately.|Which records should the estate hand over?|Keep the original sale calculation, prior Forms 6252, principal balance, note terms and any adjustments to the gross profit percentage.|installment-sale-tax-strategy|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
rental-installment-note-passes-to-buyer-at-death|What happens to deferred gain if the seller-financed note passes to the buyer when the seller dies?|A transfer of the installment obligation to the buyer because of the holder's death is treated as a disposition. The estate needs to calculate the gain or loss. For related parties, use a fair market value at least equal to the note's face value.|A parent sells a rental to a child on an installment note and directs that the remaining obligation pass to the child at death. The estate reviews the note balance, the holder's basis in the obligation and the related-party valuation rule before reporting the disposition.|What if the will cancels the debt instead of transferring the note?|Cancellation because of the holder's death is also a disposition. Give the preparer the note, estate documents and payment history to determine the amount to report.|installment-sale-tax-strategy|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
# Assumptions and settlements of existing rental notes
rental-installment-note-assumed-by-new-buyer|Does a new buyer assuming my existing rental installment note trigger all of the remaining gain?|Generally, if the original buyer resells the property and you agree to let the new buyer assume the existing installment obligation, the assumption itself is not a disposition of the note. Continue tracking principal and interest under the applicable installment reporting rules.|A property buyer resells the rental and the new owner assumes the existing seller-financed note. The note holder keeps the original installment file and adds the signed assumption agreement so each subsequent payment can be tied to the correct obligation.|What if closing also pays part of the outstanding note?|Reconcile any principal paid at closing separately. Ask your preparer to review the agreement if it changes terms beyond a straightforward assumption.|installment-sale-tax-strategy|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
rental-installment-note-partial-payment-debt-forgiveness|How do I report a partial payoff if I forgive the rest of a seller-financed rental note?|When you accept part payment on the note balance and forgive the rest, treat the settlement as a disposition of the installment obligation. Compare the amount realized with your tax basis in the note to determine your gain or loss.|A seller agrees to accept $55,000 of principal in full settlement of an outstanding note with a $40,000 tax basis. Assuming no other consideration or adjustments, the difference is $15,000 on the disposition. Separately reconcile any interest included in the settlement.|Which records support the calculation?|Retain the signed release and payment evidence, the original installment calculation and a rollforward of principal and unreported gain through the settlement date.|installment-sale-tax-strategy|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p537
