# Documenting a study
cost-segregation-missing-construction-invoices|Can a cost segregation study use estimates when original construction invoices are missing?|The IRS audit guide describes methods that use estimates. A supportable report explains the data, quantities, unit costs and assumptions and reconciles allocated costs to the taxpayer’s actual cost or supported basis.|A buyer lacks the original contractor invoices but has plans, photographs and a purchase allocation. Ask the provider to explain which details it verified and how estimated component costs were reconciled to the buyer’s basis.|What if estimates add up to more than the recorded cost?|Ask for a reconciliation before using the study. A construction-cost estimate can inform relative allocations, but does not independently increase the taxpayer’s purchase basis.|cost-segregation-study|https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5653.pdf
cost-segregation-indirect-cost-allocation|How should a cost segregation study explain indirect construction costs?|A study should identify indirect costs, explain its allocation method and reconcile the totals. Review whether each cost is properly included in basis and how it relates to the assets classified.|A construction ledger includes design fees and general contractor overhead. Ask the provider to distinguish costs attributable to specific assets from shared project costs and explain the basis used to allocate the shared amounts.|What documents support the allocation?|Keep the general ledger, contracts and invoices, along with the study’s schedule showing direct costs, indirect costs and reconciliation to the total.|cost-segregation-study|https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5653.pdf
cost-segregation-asset-description-function|Why should a cost segregation study describe what a component does?|An item’s function and relationship to the building can affect its classification. A useful report provides enough detail to understand the factual and legal basis for the classification.|A report lists wiring as one line item. Ask the provider to identify what the circuits serve and distinguish building-wide systems from costs that it claims directly serve particular equipment.|What evidence helps verify the description?|Retain plans, labels, photographs and the study’s explanation linking particular costs to the identified equipment or systems.|cost-segregation-study|https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5653.pdf
# Reconciling the asset schedule
cost-segregation-study-basis-below-invoice-estimate|Should a cost segregation study use what a property would cost to rebuild as its tax basis?|Replacement cost may help evaluate how to allocate a supported basis, depending on methodology. It is not itself the tax basis of an acquired property.|An engineer estimates it would cost $900,000 to recreate the improvements, but the buyer’s supported depreciable basis is $650,000. Ask for the reconciliation that explains how the study used its estimates and reached the amount reported on the tax schedule.|What should the preparer compare?|Review the purchase agreement, applicable capitalized costs and the land allocation as well as the report’s cost schedules.|real-estate-depreciation|https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5653.pdf
cost-segregation-multiple-buildings-property-ledger|How should a cost segregation study track components across multiple buildings?|Use asset identifiers that connect each classified component to its building and supporting cost records. Shared items should have an explained allocation where appropriate.|A purchase includes two buildings and shared exterior improvements. Reconcile the cost schedule by building and track the shared items separately so a later sale or replacement can be tied back to the correct assets.|Does one asset list without property identifiers work?|Ask the provider for a schedule you can reconcile to the property ledger and later disposition records. Keep the source documents referenced by that schedule.|depreciation-and-fixed-asset-review-for-rental-properties|https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5653.pdf
