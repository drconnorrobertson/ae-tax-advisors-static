# Property inherited from an estate
inherited-rental-basis-date-death-value|What is my starting tax basis when I inherit a rental property?|Initial basis is generally fair market value at the owner's death, subject to alternate valuation and other exceptions. For depreciation, document the allocation between nondepreciable land and depreciable property.|A rental has a supported $450,000 date-of-death value. The appraisal assigns $90,000 to land and $360,000 to the building. Before later adjustments, record $90,000 as land basis and $360,000 as building basis.|Does inherited basis always increase?|It can decrease if the applicable fair market value is below the decedent's adjusted basis.|real-estate-depreciation|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p551
inherited-rental-form-8971-schedule-a-basis|What do I do with a Form 8971 Schedule A for an inherited rental?|Use the beneficiary statement to identify the property and the estate tax value reported for it. If the consistent basis requirement applies, follow the reported estate tax value under that requirement and retain any supplemental statement.|A beneficiary receives a Schedule A identifying an inherited 50% interest with a $210,000 reported value. The basis file starts with the reported interest and value, then documents the allocation and adjustments applicable to that interest.|What if my appraisal covers the entire property?|Reconcile the ownership interest and valuation date used by each document. A whole-property appraisal alone does not establish the reportable basis of a fractional interest.|real-estate-depreciation|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p551
inherited-rental-alternate-valuation-sale-date|Which date sets inherited rental basis if the estate elected alternate valuation and sold the property?|With a valid alternate valuation election, property sold or otherwise disposed of within six months after death generally uses the disposition date. Property still held generally uses the date six months after death, subject to the estate tax rules.|An estate validly elects alternate valuation and sells a rental four months after the owner's death. The estate generally uses the sale-date valuation for that property under the election.|Can an heir individually elect an alternate value?|The executor makes the election for the estate. The statutory requirements generally include reducing both the gross estate value and the estate and generation-skipping transfer tax due after allowable credits.|real-estate-depreciation|https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i706
community-property-rental-basis-spouse-death|Can both halves of a community-property rental get a basis adjustment when a spouse dies?|The entire community-property interest generally takes its applicable fair market value as basis when at least half its value is includible in the deceased spouse's gross estate.|Spouses held a rental as community property with $240,000 total adjusted basis and $420,000 fair market value at death. If the rule applies and date-of-death value controls, the total basis is $420,000, including $210,000 for the surviving spouse's half.|What records establish that the rule applies?|Keep ownership and marital property records, the relevant estate inclusion records, and valuation support. Confirm the property's status under applicable state law.|real-estate-depreciation|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p551
appreciated-rental-gift-returned-within-one-year|Does an appreciated rental get a basis adjustment if I inherit it back within a year of giving it away?|The normal inherited-value basis rule generally does not apply if you or your spouse gave appreciated property to the decedent within one year of death and you receive it back from the decedent. In that case, use the decedent's adjusted basis immediately before death.|An owner gives a relative a rental interest worth $300,000 with $160,000 adjusted basis. The relative dies eight months later and leaves it back to the donor. If the decedent's adjusted basis immediately before death was $155,000, the donor generally receives that $155,000 basis.|Which value determines whether the property was appreciated for this rule?|Compare fair market value with adjusted basis when the gift was made. Keep the gift date, ownership documents and basis records through death.|real-estate-depreciation|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p551
# Property received as a lifetime gift
gifted-rental-dual-basis-sale-gain-loss|How do I calculate gain or loss on gifted rental property worth less than the donor's basis?|If gift-date fair market value was below the donor's adjusted basis, start with donor basis to test for gain and gift-date value to test for loss. Apply required adjustments to each starting amount while you held the property.|Assume a donor basis of $260,000, gift-date value of $220,000 and $10,000 of later reductions applicable to both calculations. If amount realized is $230,000, it is below the $250,000 gain basis and above the $210,000 loss basis, so neither gain nor loss results.|What if I only have the gift appraisal?|Obtain the donor's adjusted basis and depreciation schedules as well as gift-date value. The appraisal supports the loss test and the donor's records support the gain test.|real-estate-depreciation|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p551
appreciated-rental-gift-tax-basis-adjustment|Does federal gift tax paid on an appreciated rental increase the recipient's basis?|For a gift after 1976, a recipient generally adds the portion of gift tax attributable to net appreciation to the donor's adjusted basis. Use the IRS ratio of net appreciation to the amount of the gift for gift tax purposes.|Assume an outright gift has $200,000 donor basis and $320,000 fair market value. If the amount of the gift for gift tax purposes is $300,000 and gift tax actually paid on it is $30,000, the $120,000 appreciation represents 40% of that amount. The basis increase is $12,000.|Does filing Form 709 alone create a basis increase?|The adjustment depends on gift tax actually paid and attributable to appreciation. Keep the gift-tax return and proof of payment with the basis calculation.|real-estate-depreciation|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p551
