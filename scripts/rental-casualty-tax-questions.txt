# Measuring rental property damage
partial-storm-damage-rental-casualty-loss|How do I calculate a casualty loss for partial storm damage to a rental?|Start with the smaller of the affected property's adjusted basis or the decline in fair market value caused by the casualty. Subtract insurance or other reimbursement received or expected.|A storm lowers a rental building's market value by $40,000. Its adjusted basis is $160,000, and insurance covers $28,000. The casualty loss is $12,000 before any other applicable rules.|Does the contractor's bill set the amount of the loss?|Qualifying repair costs can serve as evidence of the drop in value if they meet IRS conditions, including being necessary, reasonable, and limited to restoring the damage. Keep the scope and invoices with your valuation records.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
destroyed-rental-building-casualty-loss|How do I calculate a casualty loss when an entire rental building is destroyed?|For completely destroyed income-producing property, begin with its adjusted basis. Subtract salvage value and insurance or other reimbursement received or expected.|A fire completely destroys a rental building with $190,000 of adjusted basis. With $5,000 in salvage and $150,000 in insurance, the casualty loss is $35,000 before other applicable rules.|Should the starting basis include the lot?|Use the adjusted basis of the affected property. For a destroyed building on surviving land, use the building's basis and preserve the land's separate basis in your records.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
# Insurance timing and depreciation
pending-rental-insurance-claim-casualty-year|Which tax year applies when a rental casualty claim is still pending?|Defer the portion for which there is a reasonable prospect of reimbursement until you know with reasonable certainty whether it will be recovered. Evaluate any clearly unreimbursable portion separately.|A rental has $36,000 of measured casualty damage in Year 1. The insurer pays $20,000 and is reviewing coverage for the remaining $16,000. If that amount has a reasonable prospect of recovery, defer it. If the claim is settled in Year 2 for $8,000 more and closes the claim, the remaining $8,000 is generally a Year 2 loss.|What records help establish the deduction year?|Keep the dated claim, coverage correspondence, and signed settlement or final denial. Record when the remaining amount stopped having a reasonable prospect of recovery.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
rental-restoration-basis-after-insurance|How do insurance and repairs affect a rental building's basis after a casualty?|Reduce the affected property's basis by casualty reimbursement and the deductible loss. If either reduction applies, increase basis by qualifying costs that restore the property to its pre-casualty condition.|A rental building has $200,000 of adjusted basis before a casualty. A $30,000 insurance reimbursement and $10,000 deductible loss reduce basis to $160,000. If the owner spends $40,000 on qualifying restoration, basis rises to $200,000 before other adjustments.|How should I document the basis changes for depreciation?|Keep the insurance payment, allowed loss, and restoration invoices as separate entries. Reconcile those entries to the depreciation schedule and document when the restored property is ready for use.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
rental-insurance-proceeds-above-adjusted-basis|Can insurance proceeds create a taxable gain on a destroyed rental building?|Insurance reimbursement above adjusted basis can create a gain. Qualifying replacement property and a timely election may allow postponement under the involuntary conversion rules.|Insurance pays $225,000 for a destroyed rental building with $180,000 of adjusted basis. The owner has a potential $45,000 gain before considering any postponement. If an otherwise qualifying replacement costs $210,000 and the owner elects postponement, $15,000 is generally recognized and $30,000 is deferred.|What happens to the deferred gain?|Reduce the qualifying replacement property's basis by the deferred gain. In this example, $210,000 of replacement cost less $30,000 of deferred gain gives a $180,000 replacement basis before other adjustments.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
