# Measuring rental property damage
partial-storm-damage-rental-casualty-loss|How do I calculate a casualty loss for partial storm damage to a rental?|Start with the smaller of the affected property's adjusted basis or the decline in fair market value caused by the casualty. Subtract insurance or other reimbursement received or expected.|A storm lowers a rental building's market value by $40,000. Its adjusted basis is $160,000, and insurance covers $28,000. The casualty loss is $12,000 before any other applicable rules.|Does the contractor's bill set the amount of the loss?|Qualifying repair costs can serve as evidence of the drop in value if they meet IRS conditions, including being necessary, reasonable, and limited to restoring the damage. Keep the scope and invoices with your valuation records.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
destroyed-rental-building-casualty-loss|How do I calculate a casualty loss when an entire rental building is destroyed?|For completely destroyed income-producing property, begin with its adjusted basis. Subtract salvage value and insurance or other reimbursement received or expected.|A fire completely destroys a rental building with $190,000 of adjusted basis. With $5,000 in salvage and $150,000 in insurance, the casualty loss is $35,000 before other applicable rules.|Should the starting basis include the lot?|Use the adjusted basis of the affected property. For a destroyed building on surviving land, use the building's basis and preserve the land's separate basis in your records.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
# Insurance timing and depreciation
pending-rental-insurance-claim-casualty-year|Which tax year applies when a rental casualty claim is still pending?|Defer the portion for which there is a reasonable prospect of reimbursement until you know with reasonable certainty whether it will be recovered. Evaluate any clearly unreimbursable portion separately.|A rental has $36,000 of measured casualty damage in Year 1. The insurer pays $20,000 and is reviewing coverage for the remaining $16,000. If that amount has a reasonable prospect of recovery, defer it. If the claim is settled in Year 2 for $8,000 more and closes the claim, the remaining $8,000 is generally a Year 2 loss.|What records help establish the deduction year?|Keep the dated claim, coverage correspondence, and signed settlement or final denial. Record when the remaining amount stopped having a reasonable prospect of recovery.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
rental-restoration-basis-after-insurance|How do insurance and repairs affect a rental building's basis after a casualty?|Reduce the affected property's basis by casualty reimbursement and the deductible loss. If either reduction applies, increase basis by qualifying costs that restore the property to its pre-casualty condition.|A rental building has $200,000 of adjusted basis before a casualty. A $30,000 insurance reimbursement and $10,000 deductible loss reduce basis to $160,000. If the owner spends $40,000 on qualifying restoration, basis rises to $200,000 before other adjustments.|How should I document the basis changes for depreciation?|Keep the insurance payment, allowed loss, and restoration invoices as separate entries. Reconcile those entries to the depreciation schedule and document when the restored property is ready for use.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
rental-insurance-proceeds-above-adjusted-basis|Can insurance proceeds create a taxable gain on a destroyed rental building?|Insurance reimbursement above adjusted basis can create a gain. Qualifying replacement property and a timely election may allow postponement under the involuntary conversion rules.|Insurance pays $225,000 for a destroyed rental building with $180,000 of adjusted basis. The owner has a potential $45,000 gain before considering any postponement. If an otherwise qualifying replacement costs $210,000 and the owner elects postponement, $15,000 is generally recognized and $30,000 is deferred.|What happens to the deferred gain?|Reduce the qualifying replacement property's basis by the deferred gain. In this example, $210,000 of replacement cost less $30,000 of deferred gain gives a $180,000 replacement basis before other adjustments.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
# Documenting property value after a casualty
rental-casualty-completed-repairs-value-decline-evidence|Can completed repairs show how much value a rental lost after a storm?|Repair costs may support the decline in fair market value if the repairs are actually made, reasonably priced, necessary to restore the prior condition, and limited to casualty damage. The repairs must not raise the property's value above its pre-casualty value.|A contractor repairs storm-damaged siding and also adds a new covered entry. The owner keeps the scopes separate and evaluates only the completed siding repairs as evidence of the casualty-related decline in value.|Does the repair bill determine the deduction by itself?|Use the qualifying costs as evidence of value decline. Apply the applicable adjusted basis limit and account for insurance or other reimbursement separately.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
rental-casualty-landscaping-restoration-value-evidence|How can I document loss in rental property value after a storm damages its landscaping?|Costs that restore the landscaping to its prior condition can help show the decline in fair market value. Retain evidence of damaged plantings, qualifying removal and replacement costs, and any salvage receipts.|A storm damages several mature trees at a rental. The owner preserves photos, itemized removal bills and records for replanting that restores the prior landscaping. The owner separately identifies the cost of adding a new patio.|How should I handle money received for salvage?|Document salvage proceeds and subtract them from the related removal costs when using those costs as evidence of value decline. Preserve separate insurance and valuation records for the loss calculation.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
# Reconciling the final insurance settlement
rental-casualty-final-insurance-less-than-expected|When can I deduct the rest of a rental casualty loss if insurance pays less than expected?|For the portion previously deferred because reimbursement was reasonably possible, determine the loss in the year you can establish with reasonable certainty that no further reimbursement will be received. Reconcile the final settlement to the original loss calculation.|A landlord has $12,000 of otherwise allowable casualty loss before reimbursement, expects $9,000 from insurance, and deducts the uncovered $3,000. The insurer settles for $7,000 the next year. If no further recovery is reasonably expected, the owner evaluates the additional $2,000 loss in the settlement year.|Which records establish when the claim ended?|Keep the settlement or denial letter, payment records and documentation of any appeal or other recovery rights. Reconcile the amount already deducted to avoid counting it again.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
rental-casualty-extra-insurance-after-loss-deduction|What if insurance pays more after I already deducted a rental casualty loss?|A later reimbursement of a prior casualty deduction is generally included in income in the year received to the extent the earlier deduction produced a tax benefit. Reconcile the recovery to the prior return and evaluate any separate gain or basis consequences.|A landlord deducted an uninsured portion of storm damage based on the information available when filing. A later review increases the insurance payment by $1,500. The owner calculates how much of that payment recovers a deduction that provided a tax benefit.|Do I amend just because the later payment was higher?|The recovery rule generally puts the taxable amount in the year received. Review the original filing separately if facts known at that time were reported incorrectly.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547
# Reporting damaged rental assets
rental-casualty-building-and-equipment-separate-losses|Do I combine the rental building and equipment when calculating a storm loss?|Generally calculate the loss separately for each damaged rental asset. Identify the adjusted basis, any applicable decline in value, and the reimbursement attributable to each item before combining results on the federal return.|A storm damages a rental building and destroys separately tracked appliances. The owner gives the preparer the building valuation, appliance schedules, and the insurer's allocation of proceeds so each loss calculation can be traced to the underlying records.|What if the settlement does not break out the assets?|Ask the insurer for the claim detail and document how the reimbursement is assigned. Reconcile the asset amounts to the full settlement before filing.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/publications/p547

The preparer locates the original deduction schedule and follows the entity reporting instructions so partners receive the information needed for their returns.|What records should the owners retain?|Keep the Schedule K-1 and its supplemental statement with earlier Section 179 deduction and carryover records. The owner gives the preparer the two claim numbers and separate scopes, so the calculations can be prepared and summarized by event.|What if one repair invoice covers both storms?|Get a breakdown by damage event or document a supported allocation. Reconcile the invoice and insurance payments to the two event worksheets.|rental-property-tax-planning|https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i4684
